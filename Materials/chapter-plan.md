# Chapter Plan

Twenty-two chapters in four parts. Every chapter follows the order in `CLAUDE.md`. Every MicroSim is p5.js 1.11.10, schematic (never official borders), starts in a useful state, and teaches one objective. "Ask Your Elders" uses the exact template in `CLAUDE.md`.

**Part 1, Foundations:** Chapters 1-6. **Part 2, Living Culture:** Chapters 7-16. **Part 3, The Eight Countries:** Chapters 17-20. **Part 4, Here and Now:** Chapters 21-22.

---

## Chapter 1: What Is South Asia?   (slug: 01-what-is-south-asia, sim: ch01-region-explorer)
- **Hook:** Ben asks Zara whether Bangladesh is "part of India". Zara realizes she can't explain the difference between a region, a subcontinent and a country.
- **Running example use:** Zara and Ben plan the club's first meeting and need a one-slide answer to "what counts as South Asia?"
- **Ask Your Elders:** where the family comes from (village, city, river, region), what the weather and landscape were like, what the monsoon meant to them.
- **MicroSim:** Region Explorer. A schematic tile map: eight country tiles positioned roughly geographically, plus drawn ranges (Himalaya, Hindu Kush, Western and Eastern Ghats), rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra) and seas (Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal, Indian Ocean). Click a tile to see capital, 2024 population (canonical), main languages. A "Monsoon" toggle animates the southwest monsoon arriving from the sea (paused at start, Start/Pause). Objective: locate the eight countries and explain how mountains and monsoon shape where people live. Bloom: Understand.
- **Misconceptions:** "South Asia = India"; "the Himalaya is in one country"; "monsoon means constant rain"; "all of South Asia is hot".
- **Key sources:** World Bank population data; Britannica "South Asia" and "monsoon"; NTI SAARC page; NOAA/IMD monsoon explainers.
- **Watch out for:** the World Bank regional-total trap (use the eight-country sum, canonical). Do not draw borders. Present "Indian subcontinent" as a geographic term, and say some prefer "South Asia" because it doesn't center one country.

## Chapter 2: Ancient Roots   (slug: 02-ancient-roots, sim: ch02-ancient-timeline)
- **Hook:** At the Minneapolis Institute of Art, Ben sees a tiny Indus seal with an animal and unknown writing. Zara: "Nobody can read it?"
- **Ask Your Elders:** stories they heard about the ancient past, places they visited (Mohenjo-daro, Sarnath, Anuradhapura, Bodh Gaya), how history was taught in their school.
- **MicroSim:** Ancient Timeline. A horizontal timeline from about 7000 BCE (Mehrgarh) to 550 CE (end of the Guptas) with a scrubbing slider. As the year changes, labeled dots on a schematic map light up (Mehrgarh, Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Pataliputra, Taxila, Madurai, Nalanda, Anuradhapura) and an event card shows what was happening. Objective: put the major ancient periods in order and connect them to places. Bloom: Remember/Understand.
- **Misconceptions:** "history began with the Vedas"; "the Indus script has been deciphered"; "Aryans invaded and destroyed the Indus cities"; "Ashoka was always peaceful".
- **Key sources:** Harappa.com; UNESCO Mohenjo-daro listing; Britannica Indus civilization, Maurya, Gupta; The Met Heilbrunn Timeline.
- **Watch out for:** date ranges (use Britannica/Met ranges and say "about"); Indo-Aryan migration framing (see CLAUDE.md); Sangam Age dates are debated (give a range with "about"); Nalanda founding (5th century CE, Gupta era).

## Chapter 3: Empires and Encounters   (slug: 03-empires-and-encounters, sim: ch03-empire-timeline)
- **Hook:** Ayesha says her grandfather calls Lahore "the city of the Mughals"; Arjun says his family's Hyderabad was ruled by the Qutb Shahis. Ben: "How many empires were there?"
- **Ask Your Elders:** the oldest building or ruin they remember near home; who built it; family stories about kings, nawabs, rajas or trade.
- **MicroSim:** Empire Swim Lanes. A timeline 600-1800 CE with one lane per major power (Cholas, Sri Lankan kingdoms, Delhi Sultanate, Vijayanagara, Mughals, Marathas, Durrani Empire, European companies). Hover or tap a bar for a card (dates, capital, one fact). A vertical "year" cursor shows who overlapped. Objective: see that many powers coexisted and overlapped rather than one replacing another neatly. Bloom: Analyze.
- **Misconceptions:** "Islam arrived only by conquest" (also trade, Sufis, Arab merchants on the Malabar coast); "Mughals ruled all of South Asia"; "the Taj Mahal is a palace"; "the East India Company was the British government".
- **Key sources:** Britannica on each dynasty; The Met timeline; UNESCO Taj Mahal, Hampi, Great Living Chola Temples; British Library on the East India Company.
- **Watch out for:** neutral framing of conquest and religion; dates of founding (Delhi Sultanate 1206; Mughal 1526 Panipat; Durrani 1747; Plassey belongs to Chapter 4).

## Chapter 4: Colonialism, Independence, and Partition   (slug: 04-colonialism-and-partition, sim: ch04-borders-then-and-now)
- **Hook:** Zara's Nanu tells her that her own grandmother was born in "India", raised in "Pakistan" and died in "Bangladesh", without ever moving house. Zara can't make it add up.
- **Ask Your Elders:** what their family remembers or was told about 1947 or 1971 (only if they want to share), independence days, how history is told differently in different families. Include a gentle note that some memories are painful.
- **MicroSim:** Borders Then and Now. A schematic, non-geographic block diagram (not a real map): a slider moves through 1857, 1947, 1948, 1971. Blocks labeled "British India", "Princely States", "India", "Pakistan (West)", "Pakistan (East)", "Bangladesh", "Ceylon / Sri Lanka" split and re-form; an event panel explains each step. Caption the sim clearly as schematic. Objective: explain how one colonial territory became several nations in two separate events. Bloom: Understand.
- **Misconceptions:** "Partition created Bangladesh"; "independence came only from nonviolence"; "Partition violence happened on one side"; "Britain ruled every part directly".
- **Key sources:** UK National Archives (Partition, Bangladesh 1971 pages); 1947 Partition Archive; Britannica on the Raj, Partition, 1971; Library of Congress.
- **Watch out for:** death and displacement figures (use ranges from scholarly sources, e.g. "estimates range from several hundred thousand to two million deaths; about 10 to 20 million people displaced", verify); 1971 death toll is disputed, say so and give the range with sources; Bengal Famine 1943 death estimates (verify); Ambedkar's role; Kashmir neutrality.

## Chapter 5: Faiths of South Asia   (slug: 05-faiths-of-south-asia, sim: ch05-faith-explorer)
- **Hook:** On one Saturday, Zara, Ben, Simran and Arjun visit a mosque, a gurdwara and a mandir in the Twin Cities. Ben notices everyone takes their shoes off.
- **Ask Your Elders:** what faith practice meant in their childhood home; a prayer, song or ritual they remember; how they practice now; what they want younger people to understand. Also: "if your family is not religious, ask about values and how they were passed down."
- **MicroSim:** Faith Explorer. Six faith cards (Hinduism, Islam, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, Christianity) plus Zoroastrian and Jewish communities in a "smaller communities" row. Choose a faith and a lens (Origins, Sacred texts, Place of worship, A major festival, A core idea) and see a short card. A "Compare" mode places two faiths side by side for the same lens. Objective: compare faiths on the same dimensions without ranking them. Bloom: Analyze.
- **Misconceptions:** "Hinduism has one holy book"; "Sikhism is a mix of Hinduism and Islam"; "all Buddhists are the same"; "South Asian Muslims are recent converts"; "religion and country map one-to-one".
- **Key sources:** Pew Research religion data (India 2021 report), Britannica entries, Harvard Pluralism Project, SikhNet/SikhiWiki only as leads (prefer academic), BBC Religions (archived) as a lead.
- **Watch out for:** percentages (Pew, with census year); respectful insider language; Sikhism founded by Guru Nanak (1469-1539); Theravada in Sri Lanka, Vajrayana in Bhutan.

## Chapter 6: Languages and Scripts   (slug: 06-languages-and-scripts, sim: ch06-script-family-tree)
- **Hook:** Zara writes her name in Bangla for Ben, then Arjun writes it in Telugu and Simran in Gurmukhi. They look completely different. Are they related?
- **Ask Your Elders:** what languages they grew up speaking, at home vs school; a word with no English translation; whether they worry about the language being lost.
- **MicroSim:** Script Family Tree. A tree rooted at Brahmi branching to Devanagari, Bengali-Assamese, Gurmukhi, Gujarati, Odia, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Sinhala, Tibetan (used for Dzongkha). A separate root shows Aramaic-derived Perso-Arabic (Urdu, Pashto, Sindhi) and Thaana (Dhivehi). Click any node to see how the sound "ka" is written in that script (real Unicode characters) and which languages use it. Objective: explain that many scripts share an ancestor and how an abugida works. Bloom: Understand.
- **Misconceptions:** "Hindi is India's national language" (India has no national language; Hindi and English are official languages of the Union); "Urdu and Hindi are unrelated"; "Sanskrit is the mother of all South Asian languages" (Dravidian languages are a separate family); "a script and a language are the same thing".
- **Key sources:** Constitution of India Eighth Schedule; Britannica on Brahmi, Devanagari, Dravidian languages; Unicode charts; Ethnologue only for counts with year (prefer census); UNESCO Mother Language Day.
- **Watch out for:** Unicode correctness of every character; the "national language" trap; Thaana's origin (influenced by Arabic numerals); Dzongkha is written in Tibetan script.

## Chapter 7: Festivals Through the Year   (slug: 07-festivals, sim: ch07-festival-wheel)
- **Hook:** Ben looks up Diwali and Eid for the club calendar and notices their dates change every year, and not in the same way.
- **Ask Your Elders:** their favorite festival as a child; food, clothes, sounds; how they celebrate in Minnesota vs back home; one tradition they want you to keep.
- **MicroSim:** Festival Wheel. A circular 12-month Gregorian wheel. Festival markers sit at their usual season (Diwali Oct-Nov, Holi Mar, Vaisakhi 13-14 Apr, Pohela Boishakh 14 Apr in Bangladesh, Pongal mid-Jan, Onam Aug-Sep, Vesak May, Losar Feb-Mar, Dashain Sep-Oct, Christmas 25 Dec, Nowruz 20-21 Mar). A "Year" slider shows how Ramadan/Eid move about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year (computed from the lunar year length, not hard-coded dates). Color by faith or calendar type. Objective: explain why lunar, lunisolar and solar festivals move differently. Bloom: Understand.
- **Misconceptions:** "Diwali is only a Hindu festival" (Sikhs, Jains and some Buddhists mark it for their own reasons); "Eid is the Muslim Christmas"; "Holi is just about colors"; "every festival is celebrated the same way everywhere".
- **Key sources:** Britannica festival entries; UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage (Durga Puja in Kolkata 2021, Nowruz, Mangal Shobhajatra); government holiday lists; timeanddate.com for 2026-27 dates.
- **Watch out for:** any specific date must be verified; Ashura framed respectfully (mourning for Shia, fasting for many Sunnis); Pohela Boishakh fixed 14 April in Bangladesh since the calendar reform.

## Chapter 9: Music   (slug: 09-music, sim: ch09-tala-clapper)
- **Hook:** Zara, a violinist, hears Carnatic violin at a concert in Minneapolis and can't find the "beat one" the audience is clapping.
- **Ask Your Elders:** songs from their childhood, film songs they love, whether anyone in the family learned an instrument, a song they sing at weddings.

A circle of beat dots lights up in order with a tempo slider; the sam (first beat) and khali (wave) beats are marked and play different WebAudio clicks (synthesized tones, no recordings, sound only after the user presses Start). - **Watch out for:** khali in Teentaal is beat 9; Rupak starts on khali; verify each tala's structure. Never embed copyrighted audio.
