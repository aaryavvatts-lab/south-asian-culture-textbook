    Chapter 2: Ancient Roots¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter follows South Asia's long story from the farming village of Mehrgarh, about 7000 BCE, through the planned cities of the Indus Valley, the Vedic period and the birth of Buddhism and Jainism, to the empires of the Mauryas, Kushans and Guptas, ending around 550 CE. You will see what historians know for sure, what they still argue about, and why those arguments matter today. By the end you'll be able to put the major ancient periods in order and connect each one to real places across Pakistan, India, Afghanistan, Nepal, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka.

    Prerequisites¶

    • Chapter 1: What Is South Asia?, especially the Indus and Ganges (Ganga) rivers, the Indo-Gangetic Plain and the Hindu Kush.

    Welcome to the deep past!

    The symbol on India's money, the wheel on its flag, the Sanskrit words in a wedding ceremony and the Buddha statue in your neighbor's garden all come from this chapter's time period. When people argue online about "who was here first," they are arguing about this history too. Knowing the real evidence helps you join those conversations calmly and fairly.

    2.1 "Nobody Can Read It?"¶

    It's the club's second planning meeting, and Ben Lindqvist is supposed to be making a sign-up sheet. Instead he's holding his phone out to Zara Hossain. "Look at this," he says. On the screen is a museum photo of a small square stone, about the size of a postage stamp. Carved into it is an animal that looks like a bull with one horn, and above it a short row of neat little symbols.

    "It's a seal from Mohenjo-daro," Ben reads. "In Pakistan. About four thousand years old. People pressed it into clay, like a stamp." He scrolls. "It says the writing on it has never been deciphered." He looks up. "Wait. Nobody can read it? Like, nobody on Earth?"

    Zara shakes her head slowly. Her Nanu talks about the old ruins in Bangladesh as if they were neighbors, so Zara knew South Asia was old. But she pictured "old" as a few kings and temples, not planned cities with writing that has stumped experts for a hundred years. "So how do we know anything about them?" she asks. "And what came after?" That is what this chapter is about: how we know what we know about ancient South Asia, from the first farmers to the end of the Gupta Empire.

    2.2 How Do We Know? Clues from the Ground and from Memory¶

    Think about how a detective works. There is no confession, so the detective reads small clues: a footprint, a receipt, a coffee cup. Historians of ancient South Asia work the same way. For the oldest periods, there are no books that anyone can read. The clues come from the ground.

    Archaeology is the study of the human past through the things people left behind: buildings, tools, pots, beads, bones and seeds. Archaeologists dig carefully, layer by layer. The deeper layers are usually older. Scientists can measure the age of things like charcoal, seeds and bone using radiocarbon dating, which reads the slow, steady decay of a form of carbon. That gives a date range, not an exact year, which is why almost every date in this chapter has the word "about" in front of it.

    For later periods there are words too: texts that were memorized and passed on by voice for centuries, then inscriptions carved in stone, coins with kings' names, and reports by travelers from Greece and China. Each kind of clue has limits. Pots can't tell you what people believed, and a king's inscription tells you what he wanted people to think. Good history uses all of them together.

    A quick note on dates. BCE means "before the Common Era," and CE means "Common Era." They count the same years as BC and AD. When you count BCE dates, bigger numbers are further back in time, so 7000 BCE is older than 2600 BCE.

    2.3 The First Farmers: Mehrgarh¶

    Mehrgarh is an archaeological site on the Kachhi Plain in Balochistan, Pakistan, near the Bolan Pass, a gap in the hills that leads toward Afghanistan and Iran. It is one of the earliest known places in South Asia where people farmed and herded animals. French archaeologists Jean-François and Catherine Jarrige discovered it in 1974.

    The first village at Mehrgarh is usually dated to about 7000 BCE. Its people grew wheat and barley and raised sheep, goats and cattle. They lived in houses made of mud brick and buried their dead with ornaments. Over thousands of years the village grew, and its people learned to make pottery and work copper.

    Archaeology keeps changing, and Mehrgarh shows how. In 2025, a team led by the archaeologist Benjamin Mutin radiocarbon-dated tooth enamel from 23 of the oldest graves and got a much younger start, about 5200 to 4900 BCE (Scientific Reports, 2025). So the start of Mehrgarh is a live debate, but nobody disputes that it was a very early farming village.

    There's one more surprising find. In 2006, scientists reported in the journal Nature that some teeth from Mehrgarh had tiny holes drilled in them while the people were still alive. It is some of the oldest known evidence of something like dentistry anywhere in the world (how old exactly depends on which Mehrgarh dates turn out to be right).

    Mehrgarh matters because cities need food. Farming villages like it are the roots the region's first cities grew from.

    2.4 The Indus Valley Civilization¶

    A civilization bigger than Egypt and Mesopotamia¶

    The Indus Valley Civilization was a Bronze Age civilization that grew up along the Indus River and nearby rivers, mostly in what is now Pakistan and northwestern India, with outposts as far as northeastern Afghanistan. It is also called the Harappan Civilization, after Harappa, the first of its cities to be excavated. Its full city-building phase, which archaeologists call the Mature Harappan period, lasted from about 2600 to 1900 BCE. Early Harappan towns go back to about 3300 BCE.

    It was one of the world's three earliest civilizations, alongside Mesopotamia (in today's Iraq) and Egypt. Of the three, it covered by far the most land. According to Encyclopaedia Britannica, its sites stretch from Sutkagen Dor near the Arabian Sea coast of Balochistan to Ropar at the foot of the Shimla Hills in India's Punjab, and as far east as the Yamuna River basin north of Delhi. A Harappan trading post, Shortugai, even sat on the Amu Darya (Oxus) River in northern Afghanistan. Of more than a thousand known sites, most were villages and small towns.

    Harappa and Mohenjo-daro¶

    Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are the two best-known cities of the Indus Valley Civilization, both in present-day Pakistan. Excavations began at Harappa, in Punjab, in 1921, and at Mohenjo-daro, in Sindh, beside the Indus River, in 1922. Other large cities include Dholavira and Rakhigarhi, both in India, and Ganeriwala in Pakistan's Cholistan Desert.

    These cities stand out because they were planned so carefully. At Mohenjo-daro:

    • The streets meet at right angles, like a grid.
    • Most buildings were made of baked brick.
    • Many houses had a room for bathing, and waste water flowed into covered drains that ran along the main streets.
    • A large, watertight brick pool, now called the Great Bath, may have been used for ritual washing.
    • The city had a raised western area that archaeologists call the citadel, and a larger lower town.

    UNESCO, which made the ruins of Mohenjo-daro a World Heritage Site in 1980, describes it as the best-preserved city in South Asia from the 3rd millennium BCE. The site covers about 240 hectares, and only about a third of it has been dug up since 1922. Britannica cites estimates of about 35,000 to 41,000 people at Mohenjo-daro and about 23,500 to 35,000 at Harappa, which would make them very large cities for their time.

    Indus people grew wheat, barley, peas, sesame and some of the earliest known cotton, and Indus objects such as red carnelian beads have turned up as far away as Mesopotamia. A tiny bronze figure nicknamed the "Dancing Girl" and a stone bust nicknamed the "Priest-King" are two famous finds. Modern archaeologists invented both nicknames, and nobody knows who the figures really showed.

    Spot the guess

    Notice how many words in this section are labels that modern people made up: "Great Bath," "citadel," "Priest-King." When you read about the Indus cities, ask: is this something the evidence shows, or a guess dressed up as a name?

    Why did the cities fade?¶

    The big Indus cities did not end in one dramatic day. Britannica describes a decline in stages between about 2000 and 1750 BCE. Mohenjo-daro had been hit by serious floods more than once, and its houses got more crowded and more poorly built over time. Many scholars think shifting rivers and a drier climate made it harder to farm near the old cities. People did not vanish. They moved to smaller villages, many of them farther east and south, and kept farming and making crafts.

    2.5 The Indus Script: A Puzzle Still Unsolved¶

    The Indus Script is the set of signs that Indus people carved on seals, pottery, tools and small tablets. It has not been deciphered, which means nobody can read it with confidence.

    Four problems make it so hard:

    • The inscriptions are tiny. The average one is about five signs long. Imagine learning a language from license plates.
    • There are many signs. Scholars count around 400 (the Indian scholar Iravatham Mahadevan listed 419 in the 1970s), too many for an alphabet.
    • There is no "Rosetta Stone." No inscription has been found with the same text in a language we can already read.
    • Nobody knows the language. Some scholars link it to the Dravidian family, which includes Tamil and Telugu, and others disagree. A few argue the signs were not full writing at all, more like merchants' marks.

    The puzzle still makes news. In January 2025, M. K. Stalin, the chief minister of the Indian state of Tamil Nadu, announced a prize of 1 million US dollars for anyone who deciphers the script to the satisfaction of archaeological experts. As of 2026, no decipherment has been accepted by scholars.

    Be careful with 'solved!' headlines

    Every few years, someone claims to have cracked the Indus Script. Until other experts can test a decipherment and read new inscriptions with it, treat those claims as ideas, not facts.

    2.6 New Languages and the Vedic Period¶

    The Indo-Aryan Migration¶

    After the Indus cities faded, a new language family spread across northern South Asia. The languages most people in Pakistan, northern India, Bangladesh, Nepal and Sri Lanka speak today, including Hindi, Urdu, Punjabi, Bangla, Nepali and Sinhala, belong to the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European language family. That family also includes Persian, Greek, Latin, English and Ben's family languages, Swedish and Norwegian.

    How did related languages end up in places as far apart as Norway and Bangladesh? The explanation most scholars accept is the Indo-Aryan Migration: the idea that people speaking early Indo-Aryan languages moved into the northwest of the subcontinent from Central Asia in gradual waves, starting after about 2000 BCE, and that over centuries their languages spread and mixed with the languages and peoples already living there.

    Three kinds of evidence support it:

    1. Language. Words in Sanskrit, Persian, Greek and Latin match in regular, predictable ways, which points to a common ancestor spoken far to the northwest.
    2. Genetics. A 2019 study of ancient DNA from 523 people, published in Science (Narasimhan and colleagues), found that ancestry linked to herders of the Central Asian steppe spread into South Asia starting around 4,000 years ago. A second 2019 study, in Cell, read the DNA of a person buried at the Indus city of Rakhigarhi in India and found little if any steppe ancestry. Together they suggest this ancestry arrived after the Indus cities were built.
    3. Texts. The oldest Vedic hymns were composed in the northwest of the subcontinent, the region where the languages first arrived, and only later texts describe lands farther east.

    Just as important is what the evidence does not show. An older colonial-era theory imagined an "Aryan invasion" in which conquerors destroyed the Indus cities. Scholars reject it. The cities were already declining for other reasons, there's no sign of one great war, and the movement of people looks slow. The colonial version was also tied to racist ideas about skin color.

    The word "Aryan" itself needs care. In the old texts, arya meant "noble," a name for people who shared a language and customs, not a "race." European racists, and later the Nazis, twisted it into a racist label.

    Whose Story?

    Most linguists, archaeologists and geneticists worldwide accept that Indo-Aryan languages arrived through gradual migration over centuries, mixing with the people already living in the region. Some writers and scholars in India dispute this. They argue that Vedic culture grew up locally, sometimes linking it directly to the Indus cities, and they point to cultural continuity across the region. In Pakistan, the Indus cities are taught as the deep roots of the nation's own land, from Mohenjo-daro in Sindh to Harappa in Punjab. Tamil scholars and politicians often stress a possible Dravidian link to the Indus Script. The same ancient DNA results have been quoted by people on different sides of these debates, so it is worth reading the actual studies, not just headlines about them.

    The Vedic Period¶

    The Vedic Period is the era, from about 1500 to 500 BCE, when the Vedas were composed in northern South Asia. It sits between the end of the Indus cities and the rise of new cities and kingdoms around 600 BCE.

    Early Vedic people lived mostly in the Punjab region, organized into tribes rather than kingdoms, and lived largely by herding. Priests performed rituals around a sacred fire while chanting hymns. Between about 1200 and 1000 BCE, Vedic culture spread east into the western Ganga plain, and the Kuru kingdom became its new center.

    This is also when varna, a division of society into priests, warriors and rulers, farmers and traders, and laborers, appears in Vedic texts. Varna is an idea from the texts. In everyday life, people later belonged to thousands of local groups, called jati, that were usually inherited and married within the group. Over many centuries, the textual varna idea and these local jati groups together shaped what is now called caste, and how they fit together varied from region to region. You'll learn about that long and painful history, and about the movements against caste discrimination, in Chapter 13.

    The Vedas¶

    The Vedas (वेद, VAY-duh, "knowledge") are four collections of sacred hymns, chants, ritual instructions and teachings composed in an early form of Sanskrit. They are the oldest scriptures of Hinduism. The four are:

    • the Rigveda, a collection of hymns of praise to gods such as Agni (fire) and Indra (storm and war);
    • the Samaveda, hymns arranged for singing;
    • the Yajurveda, prayers and formulas for priests to use during rituals;
    • the Atharvaveda, which includes prayers for health, protection and everyday life.

    Most scholars date the bulk of the Rigveda, the oldest, to about 1500 to 1000 BCE. Later layers include the Upanishads, philosophical dialogues about the self and ultimate reality that shaped central ideas in Hinduism.

    The way the Vedas survived is unusual. For centuries nobody wrote them down at all. Teachers passed them to students by voice, using memory techniques so precise that the sound of each word stayed fixed for thousands of years. UNESCO recognized the Tradition of Vedic Chanting as part of the world's intangible cultural heritage (it was proclaimed in 2003 and inscribed on the list in 2008). In Hindu tradition, the Vedas are shruti, "what is heard," revealed to ancient sages rather than written by human authors.

    Sanskrit¶

    Sanskrit (संस्कृत, SUNS-krit, "refined" or "perfected") is the classical language of ancient South Asia, part of the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European family. The oldest form, used in the Vedas, is called Vedic Sanskrit. Later, a grammarian named Panini, who most scholars place around the 4th century BCE (some argue earlier), described the language in a famous grammar called the Ashtadhyayi, "Eight Chapters." His rules shaped Classical Sanskrit for the next two thousand years.

    Sanskrit became the language of Hindu scripture, philosophy, drama, poetry and science, and many Buddhist and Jain texts used it too. For centuries it was a shared language of learning, a bit like Latin in medieval Europe. Its words live on in many modern languages and even in English: "yoga," "karma," "avatar" and "jungle" all trace back to Sanskrit.

    2.7 Cities Return: The Mahajanapadas¶

    Around 600 BCE, something happened in the Ganga plain that had not happened in South Asia since the Indus cities: big towns and cities appeared again. Historians call this the "second urbanization."

    The Mahajanapadas (महाजनपद, muh-HAA-jun-uh-puh-duh, "great realms") were the sixteen large kingdoms and republics that ruled northern South Asia from about the 6th to the 4th century BCE. The word comes from maha, "great," and janapada, a territory where a people (jana) had settled. Old Buddhist texts list sixteen of them, stretching from Gandhara in the northwest (today's northern Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan) to Anga in the east (today's Bihar, India).

    Most were ruled by kings, but a couple were governed by councils of leading families, which historians sometimes call republics. Over time, the kingdom of Magadha, in today's Bihar, swallowed its neighbors. Its rulers built a fort at a village on the Ganga that grew into the city of Pataliputra, near modern Patna. Pataliputra would become the capital of two of the biggest empires in this chapter.

    In the far northwest, the city of Taxila, near today's Islamabad in Pakistan, became an important stop on trade routes to Central Asia. UNESCO's description of the site notes that Alexander the Great entered Taxila in 326 BCE, during his campaign into the Punjab.

    2.8 New Paths: The Rise of Buddhism and Jainism¶

    The new cities brought new wealth and new kinds of work, and with them came new questions. Some people began to doubt that elaborate fire rituals were the only path to a good life. Wandering teachers, called shramanas, gave up home and possessions to search for answers through meditation and simple living. Two of these teachers started traditions that are still alive today.

    The Rise of Buddhism and Jainism describes how these two traditions began in the Ganga plain in the 6th or 5th century BCE, during the age of the Mahajanapadas, and grew into religions followed by millions.

    The Buddha¶

    Siddhartha Gautama, who became known as the Buddha, "the awakened one," lived in the 6th or 5th century BCE. Historians are not sure of his exact dates. According to Buddhist tradition:

    • He was born into a ruling family of the Shakya clan at Lumbini, in what is now southern Nepal.
    • He left his comfortable life to search for an end to suffering.
    • He reached awakening (enlightenment) while meditating under a fig tree at Bodh Gaya, in today's Bihar, India.
    • He gave his first teaching at the Deer Park at Sarnath, near Varanasi.
    • He died at Kushinagar, in today's Uttar Pradesh, India.

    The Buddha taught a "Middle Way" between luxury and extreme self-denial. His core teachings are the Four Noble Truths (life involves suffering; suffering has a cause in craving; suffering can end; and there is a path to end it) and the Noble Eightfold Path, a practice of ethics, mindfulness and meditation. He founded a community of monks and, later, nuns, called the sangha.

    Mahavira and Jainism¶

    Mahavira, "the great hero," was born Vardhamana in today's Bihar and lived around the same time as the Buddha. Jains consider him the 24th and last Tirthankara, or "ford-maker," of the present age: a teacher who made a crossing over the sea of rebirth for others. Jainism's most basic rule is ahimsa, non-violence toward every living being. Some Jain monks carry a soft brush to gently move insects out of their path.

    Buddhism Jainism
    Key teacher Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha Mahavira (the 24th Tirthankara)
    When 6th or 5th century BCE 6th or 5th century BCE
    Where Ganga plain (Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath) Ganga plain (today's Bihar)
    Central idea The Middle Way; the Four Noble Truths Ahimsa, non-violence to all living things

    Both traditions questioned the idea that rituals performed by priests were the only path to a good life. Hindu thought was changing at the same time, too, and the Upanishads were asking similar deep questions about the self, rebirth and freedom. You'll explore all three faiths, and more, in Chapter 5.

    2.9 The Maurya Empire and Ashoka¶

    The first great empire¶

    The Maurya Empire was the first empire to rule most of the Indian subcontinent. It lasted from about 321 to 185 BCE, with its capital at Pataliputra.

    It was founded by Chandragupta Maurya, who took the Punjab after Alexander the Great's death in 323 BCE. Around 305 BCE he fought Seleucus, one of Alexander's generals, and the two made a treaty that gave Chandragupta lands in the northwest. A Greek ambassador, Megasthenes, lived at his court. His book is lost, but pieces survive in later Greek and Roman writers. Chandragupta's son Bindusara pushed the empire south into the Deccan. The Mauryas ran a large government with a standing army and officials, and kept up a great royal road running from the northwest to Pataliputra.

    Ashoka: a ruler who changed course¶

    Ashoka (also spelled Asoka) was the third Maurya emperor and Chandragupta's grandson. He ruled from about 268 to 232 BCE (Britannica gives two possible ranges, about 265 to 238 BCE or about 273 to 232 BCE). His empire stretched from present-day Afghanistan in the west to present-day Bangladesh in the east.

    In the eighth year of his reign, about 260 BCE, Ashoka conquered Kalinga, a region on the Bay of Bengal coast in today's Odisha, India. The war was brutal. In one of his own inscriptions, Ashoka said that 150,000 people were carried away as captives, 100,000 were killed, and many times that number died. He wrote that he felt deep sorrow and regret.

    After Kalinga, Ashoka gave up conquering new lands by war. He became a follower of Buddhism and promoted what he called dhamma (धम्म, DUM-muh, "righteousness" or "right conduct," the Prakrit form of the Sanskrit word dharma). For Ashoka, dhamma meant things like non-violence, tolerance of all religious groups and opinions, obedience to parents, respect for priests and religious teachers, and humane treatment of servants.

    He ended the royal hunts, and Buddhist tradition says he built many stupas (dome-shaped shrines holding relics) and sent missions to other lands. The Sri Lankan chronicle Mahavamsa says he sent his son Mahinda and his daughter Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka as missionaries.

    Ashoka is on your money

    Look at an Indian rupee note. The lion emblem on it is India's state emblem, adapted from the four-lion pillar top Ashoka put up at Sarnath, and the 24-spoked wheel in the middle of India's flag is the Ashoka Chakra.

    The Edicts of Ashoka¶

    The Edicts of Ashoka are messages that Ashoka had carved on rocks and on tall polished stone pillars across his empire. They are some of the oldest readable written documents in South Asia. Most were written in Prakrit, an everyday form of the languages related to Sanskrit, using the Brahmi script. Brahmi is the ancestor of dozens of modern scripts, including Devanagari, Bangla, Tamil and Sinhala.

    The edicts were written for local readers. In today's Pakistan, some used the Kharoshthi script, written right to left. Near Kandahar, in Afghanistan, one was carved in Greek and Aramaic. At Lumbini in Nepal, Ashoka put up a pillar marking the Buddha's birthplace, and UNESCO dates it to 249 BCE.

    Over the following centuries Brahmi changed so much that nobody could read the edicts, and Ashoka was nearly forgotten. Between 1836 and 1838, James Prinsep, a British official and scholar in Calcutta (now Kolkata), worked out the Brahmi script, and Ashoka returned to history.

    Not every Ashokan inscription fits the gentle image, though. Ashoka did not give back the lands he had conquered, and Kalinga stayed part of his empire. In the Kalinga region itself, his inscriptions leave out the edict that expresses regret for the war. One possible reason is that confessing to the people he had conquered did not seem politically wise. Ashoka really did rethink power after a terrible war, but he was still an emperor.

    After Ashoka's death, the empire weakened. The last Maurya ruler was overthrown about 185 BCE.

    2.10 Crossroads in the North: The Kushan Empire¶

    After the Mauryas, many smaller kingdoms ruled the north, and new groups came through the northwest passes, including Greek kings from Bactria (today's northern Afghanistan and Central Asia). The most powerful newcomers built the Kushan Empire, which ruled from about the 1st to the 3rd century CE (some scholars extend it into the 4th). The Kushans came from Central Asia.

    At its height, their empire covered much of today's Afghanistan, Pakistan and northern India, along with parts of Central Asia. Its most famous ruler, Kanishka, came to the throne around 127 CE. His main capital was Purushapura (today's Peshawar, Pakistan), with a second major capital at Mathura in northern India.

    The Kushan Empire sat on the Silk Road, the trade routes linking China, Central Asia, Persia, India and the Roman world. Some Kushan kings honored the Hindu god Shiva, and Kanishka strongly supported Buddhism. Two things grew out of this crossroads:

    • Gandhara art. In the region of Gandhara, around Peshawar and Taxila, artists mixed Buddhist subjects with styles learned from Greek and Roman art. Some of the earliest surviving statues showing the Buddha in human form were made in Gandhara and in Mathura during Kushan rule.
    • Buddhism's journey east. Monks, merchants and pilgrims carried Buddhism from Gandhara across the Karakoram mountains into Central Asia and China.

    2.11 The Tamil South: The Sangam Age¶

    The south had its own kingdoms, language and literature. It wasn't simply a copy of the north.

    The Sangam Age is the period in the Tamil-speaking south of the subcontinent when the earliest Tamil poetry was composed. Most scholars place it between about 300 BCE and 300 CE, though some argue for a narrower range (the Tamil literature scholar Kamil Zvelebil suggested about 100 BCE to 250 CE). The dates are debated because the poems rarely mention events that can be dated.

    The word Sangam (சங்கம், SUNG-um) means "assembly" or "academy." Tamil tradition says poets gathered in academies under the Pandya kings at Madurai. Scholars treat the oldest parts of that story as legend, but the poems are real. They fall into two groups:

    • Akam ("inner") poems about love, each tied to a particular landscape, such as mountains, forests, farmland or seashore.
    • Puram ("outer") poems about war, kings, generosity, death and public life.

    Three ruling families, called the "three crowned kings," dominated the Tamil country: the Cheras on the west coast (in today's Kerala), the Cholas in the Kaveri River delta, and the Pandyas around Madurai. They often fought each other.

    The Tamil south grew rich from sea trade. Ports like Muziris, on the Malabar Coast in today's Kerala, traded pepper with the Roman world and the Middle East. Roman writers such as Pliny the Elder described it, and a Tamil poem describes foreign ships arriving with gold and leaving with pepper. You'll follow this ocean trade in Chapter 3.

    Meanwhile, in Sri Lanka and Bangladesh¶

    Ancient history happened across the region, not only in today's India.

    • In Sri Lanka, Anuradhapura became the capital of a Sinhala kingdom in the 4th century BCE (377 BCE, according to the chronicles) and stayed a capital for well over a thousand years. Tradition says Sanghamitta brought a cutting of the Bodhi tree there in the 3rd century BCE, and a tree grown from it still stands. The city became a center of Theravada Buddhism. UNESCO listed the Sacred City of Anuradhapura as a World Heritage Site in 1982.
    • In Bangladesh, Mahasthangarh, in today's Bogura (Bogra) District, is the earliest city site found so far. A limestone slab carved with six lines of Prakrit in the Brahmi script, found there in 1931, shows the city existed by at least the 3rd century BCE, when it was an important town under the Mauryas. Zara's Nanu calls it "very, very old," and she's right.

    2.12 The Gupta Empire and Nalanda¶

    The Guptas¶

    The Gupta Empire ruled much of northern and central South Asia from about 320 to 550 CE. Its kings came from Magadha, the same region as the Mauryas, and Pataliputra was again a major capital.

    Chandragupta I (not the Maurya one) began the dynasty's rise around 319 or 320 CE. His son Samudragupta expanded it through war, and Chandragupta II extended it west to Ujjain, though his reign is remembered more for culture than conquest. Sanskrit literature and science did well under the Guptas. The poet and playwright Kalidasa wrote famous Sanskrit works such as the play Shakuntala. The mathematician and astronomer Aryabhata wrote his book, the Aryabhatiya, in 499 CE, when he was 23 years old, according to the book itself. You'll meet Aryabhata again in Chapter 16, along with the story of zero and the decimal place-value system.

    Historians used to call the Gupta period India's "Golden Age." Many now question that label: some achievements once credited to the Guptas began earlier, and "golden" mostly describes life for the wealthy. The empire weakened under attacks by the Hunas from Central Asia and the breakaway of its own local rulers, and by the mid-500s it had shrunk to a small kingdom.

    Nalanda¶

    Nalanda was a great Buddhist monastery and center of learning in today's Bihar, India. Most historians date its founding to the reign of the Gupta emperor Kumaragupta I, around 427 CE. It grew into one of the most famous places of study in Asia and operated for roughly 800 years.

    Monks and students there studied Buddhist philosophy along with grammar, logic, medicine, mathematics and astronomy. They lived in brick buildings called viharas, and the monastery had a famous library. Students and scholars came from as far away as China and Korea. The Chinese pilgrim and scholar Xuanzang studied there in the 7th century and carried many texts back to China. Nalanda was badly damaged in attacks around 1200 CE and was later abandoned.

    UNESCO made the Archaeological Site of Nalanda Mahavihara a World Heritage Site in 2016, calling it the most ancient university of the Indian subcontinent. Some historians note that "university" is a modern word that fits imperfectly, since Nalanda was first of all a monastery.

    Our story ends around 550 CE, as the Gupta Empire fades. New kingdoms, new faiths and new traders are about to arrive, and that is the story of Chapter 3.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: South Asian history begins with the Vedas.
    Reality: Farming villages like Mehrgarh go back to about 7000 BCE (some say later), and the planned cities of the Indus Valley Civilization flourished from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, centuries before the Rigveda was composed.

    Myth: Scholars have figured out how to read the Indus Script.
    Reality: The Indus Script is still undeciphered. Its inscriptions are very short, there is no bilingual text to help, and the language is unknown. In 2025 the government of Tamil Nadu offered a 1 million US dollar prize for a decipherment that experts accept.

    Myth: "Aryans" invaded and destroyed the Indus cities.
    Reality: The Indus cities declined gradually between about 2000 and 1750 BCE, likely because of floods, shifting rivers and a drier climate. Most scholars think Indo-Aryan speakers arrived slowly over centuries and mixed with people already there. The violent "Aryan invasion" story is an outdated colonial-era theory that scholars reject.

    Myth: Ashoka was always a peaceful Buddhist king.
    Reality: Ashoka fought a bloody war to conquer Kalinga, around 260 BCE, before turning to Buddhism and dhamma. Even afterward he kept his army and his empire, and the edict in which he expresses regret was left out of the inscriptions in Kalinga itself.

    Myth: Ancient South Asian history happened only in what is now India.
    Reality: Mehrgarh, Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Taxila are in Pakistan; Lumbini is in Nepal; Ashoka's Greek and Aramaic edict is near Kandahar in Afghanistan; Mahasthangarh is in Bangladesh; and Anuradhapura is in Sri Lanka.

    Check Yourself¶

    1. Why is Mehrgarh important?

    It is one of the earliest known farming villages in South Asia, usually dated to about 7000 BCE. Its people grew wheat and barley and herded animals, and villages like it were the base the later Indus cities grew from.

    2. Name three features that show Mohenjo-daro was a planned city.

    Any three of these: grid streets meeting at right angles, baked-brick buildings, covered drains along the streets, bathrooms in houses, and a raised citadel area with the Great Bath.

    3. Give two reasons the Indus Script is so hard to decipher.

    The inscriptions are very short (about five signs on average), there is no bilingual text like the Rosetta Stone, and nobody knows what language it recorded. Any two are correct.

    4. What changed after Ashoka's war in Kalinga?

    Ashoka gave up conquering new lands by war, turned to Buddhism and promoted dhamma, or right conduct, through edicts carved on rocks and pillars across his empire.

    5. Which empire founded Nalanda, and about when?

    The Gupta Empire. Most historians date Nalanda's founding to the reign of Kumaragupta I, around 427 CE.

    Exercises¶

    Remember¶

    1. Put these in order from oldest to newest: Maurya Empire, Mehrgarh, Gupta Empire, Indus cities at their height, Vedic Period.

      Answer

      Mehrgarh (about 7000 BCE), Indus cities at their height (about 2600 to 1900 BCE), Vedic Period (about 1500 to 500 BCE), Maurya Empire (about 321 to 185 BCE), Gupta Empire (about 320 to 550 CE).

    2. Name the four Vedas.

      Answer

      The Rigveda, the Samaveda, the Yajurveda and the Atharvaveda.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why almost every date in this chapter starts with "about."

      Answer

      Ancient dates come from methods like radiocarbon dating, which give a range, and from texts that often don't name exact years. Scholars also disagree about some dates, such as Mehrgarh's start and the Sangam Age.

    2. Explain the difference between the Indo-Aryan Migration that most scholars accept and the old "Aryan invasion" theory.

      Answer

      The migration idea says Indo-Aryan speakers arrived gradually over centuries after about 2000 BCE and mixed with people already living there. The invasion theory claimed a violent conquest that destroyed the Indus cities. The evidence does not support a single invasion, and the cities were already declining for other reasons.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Farming began early in South Asia: Mehrgarh, in Pakistan's Balochistan, is usually dated to about 7000 BCE.
    • The Indus Valley Civilization (about 2600 to 1900 BCE at its height) built planned cities like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, and its script is still undeciphered.
    • Most scholars think Indo-Aryan languages arrived through gradual migration after about 2000 BCE; the violent "Aryan invasion" story is rejected, and some people in India dispute migration altogether.
    • The Vedic Period produced the Vedas in Sanskrit, kept alive for centuries by careful memorization.
    • The Buddha and Mahavira lived in the world of the Mahajanapadas, the new cities and kingdoms of northern South Asia, and their teachings became Buddhism and Jainism.
    • The Mauryas, especially Ashoka, built the first empire across most of the subcontinent; the Kushans linked the north to the Silk Road; Tamil kingdoms of the Sangam Age traded across the sea; and the Guptas supported Sanskrit learning and founded Nalanda.
    • Ancient South Asian history belongs to the whole region: Pakistan, India, Afghanistan, Nepal, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka all hold key sites.
