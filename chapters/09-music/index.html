    Chapter 9: Music¶

    Summary¶

    South Asian music runs on two big ideas: raga, a set of rules for building a melody, and tala, a repeating cycle of beats. This chapter explains both in plain terms, then compares the two classical systems, Hindustani music in the north and Carnatic music in the south, and the instruments and teaching traditions that carry them. It moves on to songs of devotion and poetry (qawwali, ghazal and Baul music), the folk music of all eight countries, bhangra, and the film songs that many people in the region hum every day. By the end you'll be able to clap a tala and find its first beat.

    Turn it up

    Maybe you've heard a film song at a wedding, a qawwali on your parents' playlist, or a tabla beat sampled in a hip-hop track and wondered how it all fits together. Music is one of the easiest ways into a culture, and one of the hardest to explain. This chapter gives you the vocabulary to talk about it, and even to count along. Every tradition has a story. Let's find it!

    9.1 "Where Is Beat One?"¶

    Zara Hossain has played violin since she was seven, so when her friend Arjun Reddy invites her to a South Indian classical concert at a community hall in Minneapolis, she's curious. Ben Lindqvist comes too, mostly for the snacks at intermission.

    The first surprise is the violinist. He sits cross-legged on a low stage, and instead of tucking the violin under his chin, he rests its scroll against his foot and holds it against his chest. Next to him a singer and a drummer with a long, two-headed drum face the audience. The second surprise is the audience. All around Zara, people are slapping their thighs with an open palm, tapping their fingers one at a time, then flipping their hands over and back, in perfect time with the music.

    Zara tries to tap along the way she would in orchestra, counting "one, two, three, four." It doesn't work. The melody seems to float over the beat, and the musicians land together on some moments with a huge smile, as if they've all arrived home at once. "Where is beat one?" she whispers. Arjun grins and shows her his hand: palm, pinky, ring, middle, palm, flip, palm, flip. "It's Adi tala. Eight beats. Watch where they all land."

    How does a whole room know where "one" is when Zara, a trained musician, can't find it? That's what this chapter is about.

    9.2 Two Big Ideas: Melody Rules and Beat Cycles¶

    Think about how most pop songs work. A song is in a key, like C major, which tells you which notes to use, and it has a time signature, usually four beats to a bar, which tells you where to clap. Western classical and pop music add harmony on top: chords, several notes at once.

    South Asian classical music works with similar ingredients but puts the weight in different places. There is usually no chord harmony. Instead there's a steady drone, a note held underneath everything, often from a stringed instrument called a tanpura (or tambura) or an electronic box. Over that drone, one melody unfolds in great detail, guided by a raga, while a drummer keeps a repeating beat cycle called a tala.

    A cooking comparison helps. A scale is like a list of ingredients. A raga is more like a recipe: it tells you not just which ingredients to use but how to combine them, which ones to feature, and which steps give the dish its character. Two cooks can follow the same recipe and make different meals, and two musicians can play the same raga and sound completely different. The rest of this chapter fills in that picture.

    9.3 Raga and Sargam¶

    Sargam: the names of the notes¶

    Before you can talk about a raga, you need names for the notes. In South Asian music the seven basic notes are sa, re, ga, ma, pa, dha, ni, then sa again an octave higher. Singing the notes with these syllables is called Sargam (a word made from the first four: sa-re-ga-ma). It works a lot like "do, re, mi" in Western solfege, which you may know from music class or The Sound of Music. In the south, re is often called ri.

    There's one big difference from a piano keyboard. Sa isn't fixed to one pitch. The singer or main player chooses a comfortable note to be sa, and everything else is measured from it. Sa and pa (the fifth note) never change. Four of the other notes (re, ga, dha and ni) can each be lowered (komal, "soft"), and ma can be raised (tivra, "sharp"), which gives twelve notes in all, the same twelve as in Western music.

    Raga: a melodic framework¶

    A Raga (राग, RAH-ga) is a framework for creating melody. The word comes from a Sanskrit root meaning "color" or "feeling," because each raga is meant to color the listener's mood. A raga sets:

    • which notes may be used (often five, six or seven of them),
    • how to go up and down: the ascending line (aroha) and descending line (avaroha) can use different notes,
    • which notes to emphasize and where to rest,
    • characteristic phrases that instantly identify the raga, a bit like a musical signature, called the pakad,
    • and how to ornament notes with slides and shakes.

    That's why a raga is not just a scale. Two ragas can use exactly the same notes and still sound different because of their phrases and emphasis. For example, the Hindustani ragas Bhoopali and Deshkar share the same five-note scale (sa, re, ga, pa, dha) but are treated as separate ragas. In Carnatic music, the raga with Bhoopali's notes is called Mohanam.

    In northern India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, many ragas are linked to a time of day or a season. Some are traditionally performed at dawn, some at dusk, some during the monsoon. Southern musicians pay less attention to these timings. A raga performance can last a few minutes or more than an hour, often starting slowly, with no beat at all, while the musician explores the raga note by note, then speeding up as the drum enters.

    Try singing it

    Pick a comfortable note as your sa and sing "sa re ga pa dha sa," skipping ma and ni. You've just sung the five notes of Bhoopali (Mohanam in the south). Now try singing them in a different order or lingering on ga. Does it start to sound like a tune?

    9.4 Tala: The Beat That Comes Back Around¶

    A Tala (ताल, TAAL) is a repeating cycle of beats that organizes time in a piece of music. The word also means "clapping of the palms," which makes sense, because talas are traditionally counted with claps, waves and finger taps.

    The key idea is the cycle. Western music thinks in bars that go by one after another. A tala is more like a clock face: the beats go around and around, and the most important moment is when the cycle comes back to the start. That first beat is called the sam (pronounced like the English word "sum"). Musicians may improvise wildly in the middle of a cycle, playing across the beat or against it, but they aim to land together on sam. When they do, listeners often nod, smile or clap. That's what Zara saw in the concert hall.

    A tala is divided into sections, called vibhag in the north. Each section starts with either:

    • a clap (tali), a strong hand clap or palm slap, or
    • a wave (khali, ख़ाली, KHAA-lee, "empty"), a sideways wave of the hand or the back of the hand. On the tabla, the khali section is played mostly with light strokes that leave out the deep, ringing bass, so it sounds lighter. Hearing the khali tells a listener that sam is coming soon.

    In written notation, sam is marked X, khali is marked 0, and the other claps are numbered 2, 3 and so on.

    Four talas to count¶

    Here are four common talas. The first three are Hindustani; Adi tala is Carnatic. Read the "how to count" column out loud while clapping, the same way beginning music students are taught.

    Tala System Beats Sections How to count it
    Teentaal (Tintal) Hindustani 16 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 Clap on 1 (sam), clap on 5, wave on 9 (khali), clap on 13
    Rupak Hindustani 7 3 + 2 + 2 Wave on 1 (sam is also khali), clap on 4, clap on 6
    Dadra Hindustani 6 3 + 3 Clap on 1 (sam), wave on 4 (khali)
    Adi tala Carnatic 8 4 + 2 + 2 Palm on 1, then pinky, ring, middle finger on 2-4; palm on 5, flip hand on 6; palm on 7, flip on 8

    Teentaal ("three claps") is one of the most common talas in Hindustani music, used for countless compositions. Rupak is unusual: it's the only popular Hindustani tala that begins with a wave, so sam is a quiet beat. That can be confusing at first, and it's a good test of whether you're really counting or just waiting for the loudest beat. Dadra's six beats are often used in lighter classical songs and film music. Adi tala ("first" or "primary" tala) is the most widely used tala in Carnatic music. In Adi tala the hand keeps counting the same eight beats while the singer fits two, four or more notes into each beat, so the music can feel faster or slower even when the beat itself stays steady.

    Listen for the wave

    When you're lost in Teentaal, listen for the stretch where the deep bass goes quiet. It starts at khali, beat 9, and sam is eight beats from khali. Count "9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16" and you'll land on one with everyone else.

    9.5 Two Classical Systems: Hindustani and Carnatic¶

    South Asia has two great classical traditions. They share raga, tala and sargam, and grow from the same old music theory, but by about the 1500s they were treated as separate systems with their own repertoires, styles and vocabularies.

    Hindustani music¶

    Hindustani Music is the classical music of the northern part of the subcontinent: northern and central India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nepal. It took shape over centuries in temples and in the courts of the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal Empire, where musicians blended older Indian traditions with ideas from Persian music. The poet and musician Amir Khusrau (1253-1325) is a major figure from this time, and Tansen, a singer at the court of the Mughal emperor Akbar, is remembered as one of the greatest musicians in the tradition.

    Hindustani performances often give a lot of time to improvisation. A typical performance opens with a slow, unaccompanied exploration of the raga (the alap), followed by a composition set to a tala, with the tabla keeping time. The main vocal styles include the older, solemn dhrupad and the more flexible khyal. In the 20th century, state radio stations, including All India Radio, Radio Pakistan and Bangladesh Betar, helped classical musicians reach huge new audiences after the royal courts that once paid them disappeared.

    Carnatic music¶

    Carnatic Music is the classical music of southern India, especially Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Kerala, and of Tamil communities in Sri Lanka and around the world. It centers on composed songs, many of them devotional, in languages such as Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, Sanskrit and Malayalam. The composer Purandara Dasa (1484-1564) is called the "father" of Carnatic music because he set out the basic lessons students still learn. Three composers who lived around the same time, Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar and Syama Sastri, are known as the Trinity of Carnatic music, and their songs are still the core of concerts today.

    A typical Carnatic concert has a singer, a violinist who follows and answers the singer, a mridangam (a double-headed drum made of jackfruit wood) and a drone. Improvisation happens too, inside and around the compositions. Chennai's December music season, which grew out of a music conference held in Madras in December 1927 (the Music Academy that runs it opened in 1928), now fills the city with hundreds of concerts every winter.

    The violin Zara saw came from Europe. Balaswami Dikshitar, a brother of the composer Muthuswami Dikshitar, heard Western orchestral music at Fort St. George in Madras (now Chennai) and pioneered the violin in Carnatic music. Players sit on the floor, rest the scroll on the foot, and tune the strings to sa and pa. Today the violin is so central to Carnatic music that many listeners no longer think of it as a foreign instrument.

    Hindustani Music Carnatic Music
    Where Northern and central India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal Southern India, Tamil communities in Sri Lanka and worldwide
    Main drum Tabla (also pakhawaj) Mridangam (also ghatam, kanjira)
    Common instruments Sitar, sarod, bansuri (flute), sarangi, harmonium Veena, violin, flute, nadaswaram
    Emphasis Long improvisation on the raga Composed songs, with improvisation around them
    Common languages Hindi, Urdu, Braj, Punjabi, Bangla Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, Sanskrit, Malayalam
    Counting the tala Claps and waves (tali and khali) Palm beats, finger counts and hand flips
    Famous tala Teentaal (16 beats) Adi tala (8 beats)

    9.6 Instruments: Sitar, Tabla and Veena¶

    Hundreds of instruments are played across South Asia. Three of them have become symbols of its classical music.

    Sitar¶

    The Sitar (सितार, si-TAAR) is a long-necked plucked string instrument with a round gourd body. Its name comes from the Persian sehtar, "three strings," after a small Persian lute. A modern sitar has 18 to 21 strings: six or seven are played, and the rest are sympathetic strings that run under the frets and ring by themselves when the played strings sound the same notes. That's what gives the sitar its shimmering, buzzing sound. Its curved metal frets can be moved, and players pull the string sideways across them to bend a note smoothly, a technique called meend.

    The sitar is most closely linked with Ravi Shankar (1920-2012), a Bengali musician born in Benares (now Varanasi). In the 1960s he performed at the Monterey Pop Festival and Woodstock and taught George Harrison of the Beatles, who put the sitar on Beatles records. In 1971 Shankar told Harrison about the millions of refugees who had fled the war in what is now Bangladesh, and together they organized the Concert for Bangladesh, held on 1 August 1971 at Madison Square Garden in New York, to raise money for them. Shankar received India's highest civilian honor, the Bharat Ratna, in 1999.

    Tabla¶

    The Tabla (तबला, TUB-la) is a pair of hand drums and the main drum of Hindustani music. The smaller, wooden drum, played with the dominant hand, is called the dayan (right) and produces clear, tuned tones. The larger drum, often made of metal or clay, is the bayan (left) and produces deep bass sounds that the player can bend by pressing the heel of the hand. Each drumhead has a black circle of paste that gives it its ringing tone.

    Tabla players learn every stroke as a spoken syllable, called a bol, like dha, dhin, na and tin. A tala's basic pattern of bols, called its theka, is recited and played over and over. That makes the tabla an instrument you can practice with your voice on a school bus.

    Veena¶

    The Veena (वीणा, Tamil வீணை, VEE-nah) is an ancient name for stringed instruments, used in Indian texts for about 3,000 years, and today it means several instruments. In Carnatic music, the Saraswati veena is a large plucked lute carved from jackfruit wood, with 24 fixed frets, four playing strings and three drone strings. It's named after Saraswati, the Hindu goddess of learning and the arts, who is usually shown holding one. Muthuswami Dikshitar was himself a veena player. In the north, older forms like the rudra veena belong to the dhrupad tradition.

    Other instruments you'll hear: the sarod (a fretless lute), bansuri (bamboo flute), shehnai and nadaswaram (double-reed wind instruments for weddings and temples), sarangi (a bowed instrument that imitates the voice), and the harmonium, a small hand-pumped keyboard that arrived from Europe in the mid-1800s. Indian craftsmen redesigned it to be played sitting on the floor, and it is now everywhere, even though All India Radio banned it from broadcasts from 1940 to 1971 because it can't slide between notes the way a voice can.

    9.7 How Music Is Passed On: Gharana and Guru-Shishya¶

    For most of history, nobody learned South Asian classical music from a book. They learned it from a person.

    The Guru-Shishya Tradition (गुरु-शिष्य, GOO-roo SHISH-ya, "teacher-disciple") is the practice of learning an art through a long, close relationship with a teacher. A student might live with or near the guru for years, listening, copying and practicing for hours a day, and serving the teacher's household in return. Lessons were given orally, phrase by phrase, so the music lived in memory rather than on paper. Muslim musicians often call a master teacher ustad, and pandit is a common title for Hindu masters. Today many students learn in music schools, colleges and online lessons, but the respect for the teacher remains: many students touch their teacher's feet or bow before a lesson or a concert.

    In Hindustani music, these teaching lines grew into the Gharana (घराना, gha-RAA-na), from ghar, "house." A gharana is a "musical household": a lineage of musicians connected by family or by teacher and student, sharing a recognizable style. Most gharanas are named after a place, often the court where their founders worked: Gwalior, Agra, Kirana, Jaipur-Atrauli and Patiala are well-known singing gharanas, and Delhi, Lucknow, Benares and Punjab are known tabla gharanas. Many gharana families are Muslim, many are Hindu, and students from one faith have often learned from masters of another. After 1947, some gharana families found their members living in two different countries. The Patiala gharana, for example, has well-known singers on both sides of the India-Pakistan border.

    You're part of a lineage too

    If you learned a song from a grandparent, a dance from an aunty or a tabla rhythm from a teacher at your temple, mosque or gurdwara, you're already part of a guru-shishya chain. Ask who taught them, and you might trace your music back several generations.

    9.8 Songs of Devotion and Poetry¶

    Some of the most loved music in South Asia was made to express faith, longing and love, and to set great poems to melody.

    Qawwali¶

    Qawwali (Urdu قوالی, kuh-WAH-lee) is a form of Sufi Muslim devotional music. Sufism is a tradition within Islam that focuses on inner devotion and the love of God (see Chapter 5), and qawwali is sung at Sufi shrines, called dargahs, to help listeners feel closer to God. Its development is credited to Amir Khusrau, a follower of the Sufi saint Nizamuddin Auliya of Delhi, who blended Persian poetry with Indian ragas in the 1200s and 1300s.

    A qawwali group, called a party, usually has a lead singer, side singers, harmoniums, tabla or dholak, and a chorus that claps hard on the beat. A song often starts slowly and quietly, then repeats key lines, faster and louder, building toward a feeling of spiritual ecstasy. Performances can last a very long time. Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (1948-1997), from Faisalabad in Pakistan, brought qawwali to audiences around the world and recorded over 125 qawwali albums. The Sabri Brothers of Pakistan are another famous qawwali group, and the Wadali Brothers of India sing closely related Sufi music. Qawwali is entertainment for many listeners, but at its root it is prayer.

    Ghazal¶

    A Ghazal (Urdu غزل, GUZ-ul) is a form of love poetry, and the music made from it. The form began in Arabic poetry, flourished in Persian, and from the 1700s became the leading poetic form in Urdu. A ghazal is built from at least five rhyming couplets, each called a sher. Each couplet can stand alone, like a tiny complete poem, but the second line of every couplet (and both lines of the first) ends with the same refrain word or phrase, called the radif. The "beloved" in a ghazal can be a human love, God, or both at once, and that double meaning is part of what readers love about the form. Great Urdu ghazal poets include Mir Taqi Mir and Mirza Ghalib (1797-1869), and Kazi Nazrul Islam, the national poet of Bangladesh, wrote ghazals in Bangla.

    Sung ghazals became hugely popular in the 20th century through singers like Begum Akhtar and Jagjit Singh in India and Mehdi Hassan (1927-2012) in Pakistan, who was called the "Emperor of Ghazal." Ghazal singing is quieter and more intimate than qawwali: the poem is the star.

    Baul music¶

    Baul Music is the music of the Bauls (বাউল, BAH-ool), wandering singers of Bangladesh and India's West Bengal. The word can mean "mad" in Bangla, in the sense of being mad with love for God. Bauls draw on both Sufi Islam and Vaishnava Hindu devotion, and many reject divisions of caste and religion. Many play the ektara, a one-stringed instrument made from a gourd and bamboo, while singing and dancing.

    The most famous Baul, Lalon (c. 1774-1890), lived near Kushtia in present-day Bangladesh. His songs ask why people divide themselves by religion and caste, and they are still sung at a large festival at his shrine. Baul songs also influenced Rabindranath Tagore. UNESCO proclaimed the Baul songs of Bangladesh a masterpiece of humanity's intangible heritage in 2005 and placed them on its Representative List in 2008.

    Whose Story?

    Ask who invented the sitar and the tabla, and you'll get several answers. A popular story, repeated in many books and websites, credits Amir Khusrau in the 1300s with both. Many historians now doubt this: there is no record of either instrument from his time, and they suggest the sitar was developed from the small Persian setar in the 1700s, possibly by a later musician also named Khusrau, and reached its modern form in the 1800s. Others point to old Indian stringed instruments and to temple carvings of paired drums as the deeper roots of the sitar and tabla. Each account emphasizes a different strand, Persian, Indian or both, and the instruments themselves are probably the product of all of them meeting over centuries.

    9.9 Folk Music Across Eight Countries¶

    Classical music has stars and concert halls, but many South Asians grew up with Folk Music: songs and rhythms passed down in villages and communities, tied to work, seasons, weddings, festivals and faith, usually learned by ear and performed by ordinary people. Every region has its own, and folk music often feeds classical and film music too.

    Country Folk tradition What to listen for
    Afghanistan Rubab music A short-necked lute of mulberry wood, called the national instrument; UNESCO listed the art of making and playing it in 2024, jointly with Iran, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan
    Bangladesh Baul songs, boat songs (bhatiyali) Ektara and voice; songs of rivers and longing
    Bhutan Zhungdra and boedra Two main folk styles; zhungdra comes from the central valleys and dates to the 1600s
    India Rajasthani folk songs, Bihu (Assam), lavani (Maharashtra) and many more Regional drums and fiddles, often for festivals and harvests
    Maldives Boduberu ("big drum") Groups of about 20 performers with three drummers and a lead singer; the drums are made from coconut trunks
    Nepal Songs with the madal The madal, a two-headed hand drum, is Nepal's national instrument and the backbone of its folk music
    Pakistan Sindhi Sufi songs, Pashto folk, Punjabi folk Sufi poetry sung at shrines; dhol and tumbi in Punjab
    Sri Lanka Kandyan drumming, baila The geta bera drum of Kandyan dance; baila, a dance music shaped by Portuguese and Afro-Sri Lankan communities from the 1500s onward

    In some places, music is under real pressure. In Afghanistan, since the Taliban returned to power in 2021, the authorities have imposed what AFP described in December 2024 as a near-total ban on music, and musicians who make or play the rubab have been forced to work quietly or leave the country. UNESCO's 2024 listing recognized the instrument's long history across the region at a time when that history is under threat.

    Bhangra¶

    Bhangra (ਭੰਗੜਾ, BHUNG-ra) began as a folk dance and music of Punjab, a region split between Pakistan and India, traditionally linked with the harvest and with festivals like Vaisakhi. Its main drum is the dhol, a big two-sided drum played with two sticks, often joined by the tumbi, a high, twangy one-string instrument.

    Bhangra changed when it traveled. In the 1970s and 1980s, young Punjabis in Britain, in cities like Birmingham and Southall in London, mixed folk bhangra with synthesizers, drum kits and rock and dance beats. Bands like Alaap, formed in 1977, sold huge numbers of cassettes in local shops. "British bhangra" became a sound of British Asian identity, and it later blended with hip-hop and reggae. Today Punjabi pop is a global industry. In April 2023, singer Diljit Dosanjh became the first Indian Punjabi artist to perform at the Coachella festival in California. You'll read more about bhangra as dance in Chapter 10.

    9.10 Film Music and Music Today¶

    For many people in South Asia and the diaspora, "Indian music" or "Pakistani music" means film songs. Film Music (Filmi) (फ़िल्मी, FILL-mee) is the music written for South Asian movies, especially the song-and-dance numbers that are a key part of many films. Filmi borrows from everything: ragas, folk songs, qawwali, ghazal, Western pop, jazz, disco and hip-hop.

    Filmi depends on the playback singer, because actors rarely sing their own songs. Instead, professional singers record them in a studio, and the actors lip-sync on screen. Playback singers can become as famous as movie stars. Lata Mangeshkar (1929-2022) recorded songs in dozens of languages over about seven decades and received the Bharat Ratna in 2001. Her sister Asha Bhosle, and singers like Mohammed Rafi and Kishore Kumar, are just as loved. Film industries in Mumbai (Hindi), Chennai (Tamil), Hyderabad (Telugu), Lahore (Punjabi and Urdu), Dhaka (Bangla) and elsewhere each have their own composers and stars. You'll read more about them in Chapter 15.

    Film music has also won the world's top awards. A. R. Rahman won two Academy Awards in 2009 for the score and the song "Jai Ho" from Slumdog Millionaire. In 2023, "Naatu Naatu" from the Telugu film RRR, composed by M. M. Keeravani, became the first song from an Indian film to win the Academy Award for Best Original Song.

    Not all popular music comes from films. Pakistan's television series Coke Studio pairs folk and classical singers with modern bands. Its 2022 song "Pasoori," sung by Ali Sethi and Shae Gill in Punjabi, was the most-streamed Pakistani song worldwide on Spotify that year and was hugely popular in India too. Bangladeshi rock bands, Nepali pop, Sri Lankan baila-pop and diaspora artists in the United States, Britain and Canada keep remixing the old sounds in new ways.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: Indian classical music is just improvised, with no rules.
    Reality: Improvisation is important, but it happens inside strict frameworks. The raga sets which notes, phrases and emphases are allowed, and the tala sets a cycle that every musician must track. Musicians train for years to improvise freely while landing exactly on sam.

    Myth: A raga is just a scale.
    Reality: A raga includes a set of notes, but also rules for going up and down, notes to stress, signature phrases and ornaments. Two ragas, such as Bhoopali and Deshkar, can share the same notes and still be different ragas.

    Myth: Bollywood music is all of South Asian music.
    Reality: Hindi film songs are hugely popular, but South Asia also has two classical systems, qawwali, ghazal, Baul songs, folk music in every country, bhangra, and film industries in many languages, plus pop and rock scenes from Lahore to Dhaka to Kathmandu.

    Myth: Qawwali is just party music.
    Reality: Qawwali can be thrilling to hear, but it began as Sufi devotional music sung at shrines to bring listeners closer to God. For many performers and listeners it is still a form of prayer.

    Check Yourself¶

    1. What is the difference between a raga and a tala?

    A raga is a framework for melody: which notes to use and how to combine them. A tala is a repeating cycle of beats that organizes time.

    2. What is sam, and what is khali?

    Sam is the first beat of a tala cycle, where musicians aim to land together. Khali ("empty") is a section marked with a wave instead of a clap; on the tabla it is played mostly without the deep bass.

    3. How many beats are in Teentaal, and on which beat is the wave?

    Teentaal has 16 beats in four sections of four. The wave (khali) is on beat 9; claps fall on beats 1, 5 and 13.

    4. Name one main difference between Hindustani and Carnatic music.

    Possible answers: Hindustani music is from the north and puts more weight on long improvisation, with tabla and sitar; Carnatic music is from the south and centers on composed songs, with mridangam, violin and veena.

    5. What is a playback singer?

    A professional singer who records film songs in a studio while actors lip-sync them on screen.

    Exercises¶

    Remember¶

    1. Name the seven sargam syllables in order.

      Answer

      Sa, re, ga, ma, pa, dha, ni (then sa again an octave higher).

    2. Which UNESCO list includes Baul songs, and in what year were they placed on it?

      Answer

      UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, in 2008. They were first proclaimed a masterpiece in 2005.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why Rupak tala can be tricky for beginners.

      Answer

      Rupak's first section is khali, so sam (beat 1) is a wave, not a clap. Listeners waiting for the loudest beat may mistake beat 4 for the start of the cycle.

    2. Explain what a gharana is and why gharanas are usually named after places.

      Answer

      A gharana is a lineage of musicians, connected by family or by teacher and student, who share a style. Most are named after the city or court where the founders worked, such as Gwalior or Patiala.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A raga is a framework for melody (notes, movement, emphasis and signature phrases), not just a scale; sargam (sa re ga ma pa dha ni) names its notes.
    • A tala is a repeating cycle of beats counted with claps, waves and finger taps. Sam, the first beat, is where musicians land together; khali is the "empty" wave.
    • Teentaal has 16 beats (wave on 9), Rupak 7 (starting on a wave), Dadra 6 and the Carnatic Adi tala 8.
    • Hindustani music (north) and Carnatic music (south) share roots but differ in instruments, emphasis and repertoire; both pass from teacher to student through the guru-shishya tradition, and in the north through gharanas.
    • The sitar, tabla and veena are classical icons, and instruments like the violin and harmonium show how traditions absorb new sounds.
    • Qawwali (Sufi devotion), ghazal (sung poetry) and Baul songs (sung by wandering Bengali devotional singers) express faith and love; every one of the eight countries has its own folk music, and bhangra went global from Punjab and Britain.
    • Film music, sung by playback singers, is among the most widely heard music in the region, and it has won Academy Awards in 2009 and 2023.
