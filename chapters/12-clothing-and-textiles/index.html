    Chapter 12: Clothing and Textiles¶

    Summary¶

    South Asia has been growing, spinning and weaving fiber for thousands of years, and its cloth has traveled the world. This chapter starts with three fibers (cotton, silk and pashmina wool), then looks at famous ways of weaving and dyeing them, such as Jamdani from Bangladesh, ikat from India and ajrak from Pakistan. Next it sorts everyday clothes into draped garments (the saree, dhoti and lungi) and stitched garments (the kurta, shalwar kameez and dupatta), and visits national dress in Bhutan, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Afghanistan. It ends with the turban, the bindi and bangles, and the many meanings people give them.

    Clothes that carry stories

    Hi, it's Mora! Maybe you have a kurta you only wear to weddings, or an aunt's saree folded in a closet that nobody explains. Clothes are one of the first ways people read your background, sometimes wrongly. By the end of this chapter you'll be able to tell the story behind what's in that closet, and answer the questions your friends ask about it.

    12.1 Culture Day Guessing Game¶

    The South Asian Student Association at Prairie Ridge High School is holding its first culture day, and everyone has been asked to wear something from home. Zara Hossain comes in a cream-and-gray cotton saree her Nanu brought from Bangladesh, covered in tiny woven flowers. Ayesha Qureshi, whose family is from Lahore, wears a green shalwar kameez with a long scarf. Simran Kaur Gill wears a shalwar kameez too, with a bright embroidered scarf from her grandmother in Ludhiana. Dilan Perera wears a white shirt and a checked cloth wrapped around his waist, and Nima Sherpa has brought a small brimmed cloth cap with a busy pattern.

    Ms. Okafor turns it into a game. Ben Lindqvist has to guess which country each outfit comes from. "Saree, so India," he says, pointing at Zara. Wrong: Bangladesh. "Shalwar kameez is Pakistani," he says. Right for Ayesha, but Simran's family is from India. He guesses Dilan's wrap is "a Bangladeshi lungi," which is close but not quite: Dilan's family is from Sri Lanka, where a similar wrap is called a sarama. He gets Nima's cap right only because she gives him a hint. Ben scores two out of five and groans. "How is anyone supposed to know? Half of these are worn in, like, four countries." He has stumbled onto the big question of this chapter: if the same clothes cross so many borders, what can a garment tell you about where someone is from?

    12.2 Clothes Speak a Language¶

    Ben already knows the answer from his own family. His grandmother in Minnesota has a Norwegian bunad, a folk costume with embroidery and silver jewelry. Norway has hundreds of kinds of bunad (counts run from about 200 to more than 400, depending on what is counted), and each is tied to a region. People usually choose the one from the place their family comes from, then wear it for weddings, confirmations and especially Norway's National Day on 17 May. A bunad doesn't tell you "Norwegian" so much as "my grandmother's people came from this valley."

    South Asian clothing works in a similar way, with one big difference: the region is far bigger and its borders are much newer than its clothes. The saree, the kurta and the turban are much older than the countries of India, Pakistan or Bangladesh, which became independent in 1947 and 1971. So a garment usually tells you less about a passport and more about these things:

    • Where the cloth was made. A Jamdani weave points to Dhaka. An ajrak print points to Sindh.
    • What the occasion is. Many families keep everyday cotton for home and heavy silk for weddings.
    • Sometimes, faith or community. A Sikh man's dastar, a married Bengali Hindu woman's white and red bangles, or a Bhutanese man's white ceremonial scarf each carry a specific meaning.
    • Personal taste. Plenty of teens wear a kurta with jeans simply because they like it.

    To read clothes well, it helps to start one layer down, with the fibers themselves.

    12.3 Three Fibers: Cotton, Silk and Pashmina¶

    Cotton¶

    Cotton in South Asia is the long history of growing, spinning and weaving the cotton plant's soft seed fibers in the region, a history that goes back more than 7,000 years. The oldest known cotton fibers in the world come from Mehrgarh, an early farming village in Balochistan, Pakistan. Archaeologists found a set of eight copper beads next to the wrist of a skeleton buried there in the first half of the sixth millennium BCE, roughly 7,500 to 8,000 years ago. (Some news stories round this up to 8,500 years.) When the copper corroded, its salts soaked into the thread that once strung the beads and preserved it. Under a microscope, French researchers identified the fibers as cotton.

    Cotton suited South Asia well. It grows in warm places, and cotton cloth is light and breathable in the heat and humidity of the monsoon. Later, cotton cloth from the region became one of the most traded goods in the world. Merchants carried fine Indian cotton to the Roman Empire, to Baghdad and Basra, and east toward China.

    The finest of all was muslin from Bengal, especially around Dhaka and Sonargaon in present-day Bangladesh. Weavers there used a local cotton called phuti karpas, which grew along the banks of the Meghna River, and spun it into thread so thin that travelers wrote that a whole length of muslin could be pulled through a ring. Mughal emperors prized it, and so did European nobles in the 1700s. Under British colonial rule the trade collapsed as cheap machine-made cloth from British factories flooded the market and weavers lost their livelihoods. The special cotton plant was eventually lost. In recent years, researchers and artisans in Bangladesh have been working to revive Dhaka muslin.

    Count the cotton

    Check the labels on three things in your closet right now. How many say "cotton," and how many say "Made in" Bangladesh, India, Pakistan or Sri Lanka? The cloth trade I'm describing never really stopped. It just moved into factories.

    Silk¶

    Silk comes from the cocoons of silkworms, the caterpillars of certain moths. Silk Weaving in South Asia means raising silkworms, unwinding or spinning their cocoon thread, and weaving it into cloth, usually for festive and wedding clothes. India's government recognizes four main kinds of silk:

    Kind of silk What the silkworms eat Where it is mostly made What it is known for
    Mulberry Mulberry leaves Karnataka, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir By far the most common; smooth and shiny
    Tasar Oak, asan and arjuna tree leaves Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Bihar and nearby states A rougher, textured silk
    Eri Castor leaves Assam and the northeast Spun rather than reeled; warm, with a copper tint
    Muga Som and soalu leaves Almost entirely in Assam A natural golden color

    Some of the most famous silk sarees are named after the towns that weave them:

    • Banarasi sarees come from Varanasi (also called Banaras or Benares), on the Ganga. They are known for zari, thread wrapped in gold or silver color, woven into flowers, vines and net-like patterns. A complex Banarasi saree can take from about two weeks to several months to finish. In 2009, weavers' groups in Uttar Pradesh won Geographical Indication (GI) status for Banaras brocades and sarees. A GI is a legal label, like the one that protects the name "Champagne," which says a product's quality comes from a particular place.
    • Kanjivaram (or Kanchipuram) sarees come from the town of Kanchipuram in Tamil Nadu. They are woven from mulberry silk with wide borders in a contrasting color, often with a classic "temple" border design. In a traditional Kanjivaram saree the border and the body are woven separately and then interlocked so firmly that the border stays attached even if the saree tears. The Government of India recognized Kanchipuram sarees as a GI in 2005-06.
    • Rajshahi silk comes from Rajshahi in northwestern Bangladesh, where people trace silk production back to the 13th century. Bangladesh gave it GI status in 2021.

    Pashmina¶

    Pashmina is the very fine wool from the soft undercoat of goats that live high in the Himalaya, and also the name for the shawls made from it. The word comes from pashm, the Persian word for wool. In Hindi it is written पश्मीना (push-MEE-nah).

    The classic source is the Changthangi goat, raised by nomadic Changpa herders on the high, cold Changthang plateau of Ladakh. Winter temperatures there can fall below −30 °C (−22 °F), so the goats grow a thick, fine undercoat. Each spring, when the goats shed, herders comb the undercoat out by hand rather than shearing it. Weavers in Kashmir turned this wool into the famous Kashmir shawl. Mughal emperors gave pashmina shawls as "robes of honor" to reward nobles, and in the 1800s the shawls became high fashion in Britain and France.

    One pashmina design traveled farther than the wool itself. The teardrop-shaped buta motif on Kashmir shawls was copied by factories in the town of Paisley, Scotland, in the early 1800s. That's why English speakers today call the shape "paisley," even though it began on South Asian looms.

    Nepal has its own pashmina tradition. There the high-mountain goat is called the chyangra, and Nepal's pashmina makers, working with the International Trade Centre, created a "Chyangra Pashmina" brand to mark genuine Nepali products.

    A disputed region

    Kashmir, where the famous shawls are woven, is a region whose control is disputed among India, Pakistan and China, and people from all sides claim its crafts with pride. When I say "Kashmir shawl," I mean the craft of the region, not a statement about whose territory it is.

    12.4 Weaving and Dyeing: Making Pattern¶

    Once you have thread, there are two main ways to put a pattern on cloth. You can weave the pattern in, by choosing which colored threads go over and under each other. Or you can dye or print the pattern on, by blocking color from some parts of the thread or cloth. South Asia has famous examples of both.

    A quick piece of vocabulary helps here. On a loom, the warp threads run the long way and are stretched tight. The weft thread is passed back and forth across them.

    Jamdani¶

    Jamdani (জামদানী, JAHM-dah-nee) is a fine, sheer cotton cloth from the Dhaka region of Bangladesh, with patterns woven directly into it on a hand loom. Its name comes from Persian. The weaver adds each little flower, leaf or diamond by hand, using extra weft threads that go back and forth only across that one small motif instead of the full width of the cloth. This is called a discontinuous weft technique. It is slow, careful work, so a single saree can take weeks or months, and the result is light and breathable.

    In 2013, UNESCO added the traditional art of Jamdani weaving to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. UNESCO notes that the skills pass mostly from parents to children in home workshops around Dhaka, and that many Bengali women wear Jamdani sarees as a symbol of identity, both in Bangladesh and abroad. In 2016, Bangladesh also gave the Jamdani saree GI status. Zara's culture-day saree, with its woven flowers, is a Jamdani.

    Ikat¶

    Ikat (EE-kaht) is a way of patterning cloth in which the threads are tied and dyed before they are ever woven. The word comes from Indonesia, where it means "to bind," and similar techniques grew up independently in many parts of the world. It works in four steps:

    1. The weaver wraps tight bindings around small sections of a bundle of threads, following a planned pattern.
    2. The threads are dyed. The tied sections resist the dye and stay their original color.
    3. The bindings are moved or added, and the threads are dyed again in a new color. This can repeat many times.
    4. Finally the bindings are removed, and the patterned threads are set up on the loom and woven.

    Because the pattern is in the threads, the edges of each shape come out slightly feathery and blurred, which is ikat's signature look. In double ikat, both the warp and the weft are tie-dyed, and the weaver has to line them up exactly. It's so hard that it is woven in only a few places in the world.

    India has several famous ikat centers:

    • Pochampally, in Telangana, known for bold geometric ikat sarees in cotton and silk.
    • Sambalpur and nearby districts in Odisha, where ikat is called bandha and often uses motifs such as the conch, the wheel and the flower.
    • Patan, in Gujarat, home of patola, a silk double ikat. Dutch traders once used patola as prized trade cloth in the spice trade with Indonesia.

    Ajrak¶

    Ajrak is a cloth printed by hand with carved wooden blocks, made mostly in the Sindh region of Pakistan, and also by families in Kutch, in the Indian state of Gujarat, who trace their craft to Sindh. The printers use natural dyes, especially deep indigo blue and crimson red, and "resist" techniques that keep some areas from taking the dye, skills passed down through generations of the same families. Its dense patterns of stars, circles and lattices are easy to recognize.

    For many people in Sindh, ajrak is a badge of identity. Families give an ajrak shawl to honor a guest, and it appears at weddings and cultural events. Some writers even point out that the famous stone bust of a "priest-king" from Mohenjo-daro, more than 4,000 years old, wears a robe with a trefoil (three-lobed) pattern that looks a little like ajrak. That link can't be proven, but it shows how strongly people feel the cloth belongs to the Indus region.

    Whose Story?

    Cloth often crosses borders that were drawn long after the craft began, so different groups tell its story differently. In Pakistan, ajrak is celebrated as a symbol of Sindhi culture, and in 2025 the Government of Sindh even put an ajrak-inspired design on new vehicle license plates. Across the border, block printers in Kutch in India make ajrak too, and they tell it as the story of their own families, who brought the craft from Sindh. In Bhutan, the government describes the gho and kira as national dress that protects a distinct Bhutanese identity, but many Lhotshampa (Nepali-speaking Bhutanese in the south) saw the 1989 dress rule as forcing another community's tradition on them. The rule was part of wider identity and citizenship policies, and after protests in 1990 more than 100,000 Lhotshampa left or were forced out to refugee camps in Nepal, events that Bhutan's government and the refugees still describe very differently. Asking "whose cloth is this?" usually has more than one true answer.

    12.5 Khadi: Cloth as Protest¶

    Khadi (खादी, KHAH-dee) is cloth that is both hand-spun and hand-woven, made from cotton, silk or wool. Its importance comes from the role it played in India's freedom movement.

    Under British rule, raw cotton was often shipped from India to mills in Britain, woven there by machine, and sold back to Indians as finished cloth. Mohandas K. Gandhi, a leader of the Indian independence movement, argued that buying that cloth kept India poor and dependent. He called on people to boycott foreign cloth and to spin their own thread on a charkha, a hand-turned spinning wheel. This was part of swadeshi, a movement whose name means "of one's own country."

    The first piece of hand-woven cloth at Gandhi's Sabarmati Ashram, in Gujarat, was made in 1917-18. He began spinning himself in 1919 and asked members of the Indian National Congress to spin and even pay their dues in yarn. In 1921, he began wearing only a simple dhoti, often without a shirt, to show his solidarity with India's rural poor. That same year, according to local histories, he visited the Comilla region, now in Bangladesh, to encourage local weavers, and weaving centers grew up there.

    After independence, India's government set up the Khadi and Village Industries Commission in April 1957, under a 1956 law, to support khadi and other village crafts. Khadi is still sold across India today. In Bangladesh, a khadi tradition continued in the Comilla area too, and in May 2025 Bangladesh gave Comilla khadi Geographical Indication status.

    Khadi is also a symbol of self-reliance, and many politicians in India still wear it for that reason.

    12.6 Draped Garments: Saree, Dhoti and Lungi¶

    Many South Asian clothes are not cut and sewn at all. They are long, unstitched rectangles of cloth that are wrapped, pleated and tucked around the body. This makes them easy to weave on a hand loom and easy to adjust to any body size.

    The saree¶

    The Saree (also spelled sari; Hindi साड़ी, Bangla শাড়ি, SAH-ree) is a long, unstitched length of cloth that a woman wraps around her waist and drapes over one shoulder. A saree is usually between 4.5 and 9 yards (about 4 to 8 meters) long. It is worn over a petticoat (a long underskirt) and a fitted blouse, which is called a choli in northern India and a cholo in Nepal. The loose end that hangs over the shoulder is the pallu, and it is often richly decorated.

    The saree is worn in India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka and parts of Pakistan. In Bangladesh it is widely seen as the national dress for women, which is why Zara's Jamdani makes perfect sense there. Sri Lanka has its own famous style, the osariya or Kandyan saree, which comes from the old Kandyan kingdom in the hill country. It has a frill at the waist and is worn with a full blouse that covers the midriff, and many women in Sri Lankan public life wear it.

    There isn't one right way to drape a saree. The most common style today is the Nivi drape, with pleats tucked in at the front and the pallu over the shoulder. But there are many regional drapes. In the Deccan region of western and central India, for example, the Nauvari style passes the pleats between the legs and tucks them in at the back, which makes it easy to move in. Some Newar women in Nepal traditionally fold the saree to knee length and wrap a shawl across the chest instead of the pallu.

    Ask for a draping lesson

    If someone in your family wears sarees, ask them to show you their drape slowly, step by step. Many people learned their style from their own mother or grandmother, so the way they pleat and tuck is a little piece of family history.

    The dhoti¶

    The Dhoti (धोती, DHOH-tee) is a man's unstitched lower garment, a rectangle of cloth about 4 yards (3.7 meters) long that is wrapped around the waist and then passed between the legs and tucked in, so it looks a bit like loose trousers. It is often white or cream cotton, and silk dhotis are worn for weddings and religious occasions. It has local names too. In parts of South India, the formal style is called panchakacham. The dhoti is common in India and in Nepal, and it is the garment Gandhi made famous worldwide in the 1920s.

    The lungi¶

    The Lungi (লুঙ্গি, LOONG-gee) is a length of cloth worn by men around the waist like a skirt, without passing between the legs. It is usually cotton, often in checks or stripes, and it is loved for staying cool in hot, humid weather. In Bangladesh, the lungi is the most commonly seen everyday clothing for men, usually sewn into a tube, though it is not worn for formal occasions.

    Wrapped garments like this have different names in different places. In Kerala, a white or cream wrap with a decorative border is called a mundu. In Sri Lanka, it is a sarama, which is what Dilan wore to culture day. Across the Bay of Bengal in Myanmar, it is a longyi.

    12.7 Stitched Garments: Kurta, Shalwar Kameez and Dupatta¶

    Other South Asian clothes are cut and sewn. Many of these styles came to South Asia from Central Asia and Persia over centuries, then took on local forms.

    The kurta¶

    The Kurta (कुरता, KOOR-tah) is a loose tunic, traditionally collarless, with straight sleeves and side seams left open at the bottom. It can be short or long, plain or embroidered, cotton for every day or silk for festivals. Both men and women wear kurtas, over loose trousers, fitted trousers or, increasingly, jeans. Lucknow in India is famous for chikankari, a delicate embroidery traditionally done in white thread on light cotton or muslin, often seen on kurtas.

    Two close relatives show how widely the idea spread. In Nepal, men's national dress is the daura-suruwal: the daura is a kurta-like shirt that closes with ties, and the suruwal is a pair of trousers that is loose at the hips and narrow at the ankles. It is often worn with a patterned cloth cap, the Dhaka topi, the cap Nima brought to culture day. Under King Mahendra, who ruled from 1955 to 1972, men were required to wear the Dhaka topi in official photos for passports and documents. In Afghanistan, many men wear the perahan tunban, a long, loose shirt over very full trousers. The style from Kandahar is known for fine geometric embroidery on the chest.

    The shalwar kameez¶

    The Shalwar Kameez (Urdu شلوار قمیض, SHUL-war ka-MEEZ) is a two-piece outfit of loose trousers and a long tunic, worn by both men and women. The shalwar is wide at the waist, held up by a drawstring, and narrows to the ankle. The kameez is a long shirt with slits up both sides, which makes it easy to walk, sit and move in.

    The shalwar kameez spread across northern South Asia over many centuries, especially in the Punjab region. Today it is Pakistan's national dress, and it is worn every day by millions of men and women there. It is also very common in India, especially in Punjab, where many people call the women's version a "Punjabi suit," and in Bangladesh and Afghanistan. So Ben was half right: Ayesha's outfit is Pakistani national dress, and Simran's matching outfit is just as much a part of her family's Punjabi Sikh tradition in India.

    The dupatta¶

    The Dupatta (Hindi दुपट्टा, Urdu دوپٹہ, doo-PUT-tah) is a long scarf or shawl, usually worn by women with a shalwar kameez or with a long skirt and blouse. Its name roughly means "a doubled strip of cloth." A dupatta can be draped over one or both shoulders, wrapped across the chest, or worn over the head, for example in a place of worship or in front of elders. Families and communities have different customs about when and how to wear it.

    Dupattas are often where the most beautiful handwork goes. In Punjab, on both sides of the India-Pakistan border, women have long made phulkari, which means "flower work," a bright darned embroidery in silk thread on coarse cotton. Simran's culture-day scarf is a phulkari dupatta.

    Garment Draped or stitched Mainly worn by Countries where you'll often see it
    Saree Draped Women India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, parts of Pakistan
    Dhoti Draped Men India, Nepal
    Lungi (and mundu, sarama) Draped Men Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka
    Kurta Stitched Men and women India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, the diaspora
    Shalwar kameez Stitched Men and women Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, Afghanistan
    Dupatta Draped over stitched clothes Mostly women Pakistan, India, Bangladesh

    12.8 National Dress: Bhutan and Its Neighbors¶

    Some countries in South Asia have an official or widely accepted national dress. The clearest example is Bhutan.

    Gho and kira¶

    Gho and Kira are the national dress of Bhutan: the gho (བགོ, GOH) for men and the kira (KEE-rah) for women.

    • The gho is a heavy, knee-length robe that is folded and tied at the waist with a woven belt called a kera. The fold creates a large pouch above the belt that people use like a pocket. Under it, men wear a white jacket called a tego, whose long white cuffs are folded back over the gho's sleeves.
    • The kira is an ankle-length dress made from one large rectangle of woven cloth. It is wrapped around the body, pinned at both shoulders with brooches called koma, and belted at the waist. Women wear a long-sleeved blouse called a wonju underneath and often a short jacket called a toego on top.

    Everyday gho and kira are usually cotton or wool in simple checks and stripes. For festivals, many people wear richly patterned silk kira woven by hand. Weaving, called thagzo, is one of Bhutan's traditional arts, and the Royal Textile Academy of Bhutan, founded in 2005 in Thimphu, runs a weaving school and a textile museum to keep it alive.

    Bhutan's national dress is connected to the driglam namzha, Bhutan's official code of etiquette and dress. Its roots go back to the 17th century, when Ngawang Namgyal, the first Zhabdrung, worked to unify Bhutan and give it a distinct identity.

    In 1989, the government made the dress code mandatory in public during business hours. Today men who work in government offices or schools are required to wear the gho, and both gho and kira are worn for formal occasions. When visiting a dzong (a fortress-monastery) or meeting officials, men add a ceremonial scarf called a kabney. Ordinary citizens wear a white one, while other colors are reserved for officials and monks. Women wear a narrow embroidered cloth called a rachu over the shoulder.

    Other national dress in the region¶

    Country Often-named national dress Notes
    Bhutan Gho (men), kira (women) Required in government offices and schools and for formal occasions
    Nepal Daura-suruwal with Dhaka topi (men); saree with cholo blouse (women) Many communities in Nepal also have their own dress
    Pakistan Shalwar kameez (men and women) Worn every day across the country
    Bangladesh Saree (women) Jamdani is the best-known weave
    Sri Lanka Osariya, the Kandyan saree (women) Widely treated as national dress for women

    "National dress" is always a simplification. Each of these countries has many communities with their own clothing, and a single official outfit can leave some of them out, as the Whose Story? box showed.

    12.9 Head, Forehead and Wrist: Turbans, Bindis and Bangles¶

    Some of the most noticeable things people wear are small, and many of them carry religious or family meanings.

    The turban and the dastar¶

    A turban is a long cloth wound around the head. Turbans are worn in many parts of the world and by people of many faiths. In South Asia, you can see turbans on Sikh men and some Sikh women, on many Pashtun men in Afghanistan and Pakistan, on village elders in Rajasthan, where it's often called a pagri (पगड़ी), on some Hindu monks, and on some Muslim religious scholars. Its style, color and way of tying can show region, occasion or community.

    The Turban (Dastar) is, for Sikhs, an article of faith. The dastar (ਦਸਤਾਰ, dus-TAAR) is the Sikh turban, and the word comes from Persian through Punjabi. In Sikh tradition, the Gurus adopted the turban, which in South Asia was once worn mainly by royalty, as a way of declaring the sovereignty and equality of all people. Sikhs who have formally committed to the faith keep their hair uncut (kesh), one of the five articles of faith, and the dastar covers it. According to the Sikh Coalition, a US Sikh civil rights group, both men and women may wear a turban, and wearing one is a public commitment to values such as service, compassion and honesty. Tying a dastar neatly every day, turn by turn, is a skill many Sikh children learn from a parent.

    A good question to ask

    If you're curious about a friend's turban, it's fine to ask a respectful question like "What does your dastar mean to you?" Just never touch someone's turban or ask them to take it off. For many Sikhs it's as personal and sacred as anything they own.

    The bindi¶

    The Bindi (बिंदी, BIN-dee) is a dot, traditionally red or maroon, worn in the center of the forehead, often between the eyebrows. The word comes from the Sanskrit bindu, meaning "point" or "drop." Traditional bindis are made with powders such as kumkum. Today many people wear small stick-on bindis in every color and shape.

    The bindi is worn mainly by Hindu women, and also by some Jains and Buddhists, in India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and in Hindu communities in Pakistan and Bhutan. In Hindu tradition, many people link the spot between the eyebrows with spiritual focus. For some women, a red bindi has also been a sign of being married, though customs vary a lot by region and family, and many unmarried girls wear decorative bindis too. In Nepal, a related forehead mark, the tika, is placed on people's foreheads by elders as a blessing during festivals such as Dashain.

    Bangles¶

    Bangles are rigid bracelets, usually worn in sets, made from glass, metal, shell, lac, plastic or other materials. They have a very long history in the region. The famous "Dancing Girl," a small bronze figure from Mohenjo-daro in what is now Pakistan, made sometime between about 2300 and 1750 BCE, wears a stack of bangles on her left arm. Hindi speakers call a bangle a चूड़ी (CHOO-ree), and Urdu speakers call it a چوڑی, the same word in a different script.

    Bangles can carry very specific meanings:

    • Weddings: many brides across South Asia wear sets of glass or gold bangles, and the sound of bangles is part of wedding celebrations. Many Punjabi Hindu and Sikh brides wear a chooda, a set of red and white bangles, on their wedding day.
    • Marriage in Bengal: many married Bengali Hindu women, in both Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal, wear a white conch-shell bangle called a shankha (শাঁখা, SHAN-kha) together with a red bangle called a pola. These are traditionally given to the bride at her wedding.
    • Faith: many Sikhs wear a kara, a single plain steel or iron bangle, which is one of the five articles of faith in Sikh tradition. It is worn by men and women.
    • Everyday style: gold bangles are often given to babies and little girls, and plenty of people wear bangles just because they like them.

    The town of Firozabad in Uttar Pradesh, India, is famous as a center of glass bangle making.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: The saree is only Indian.
    Reality: The saree is worn in India, Bangladesh, Nepal and Sri Lanka, and in parts of Pakistan. It is widely seen as the national dress for women in Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka has its own Kandyan style, the osariya.

    Myth: The shalwar kameez is only Pakistani.
    Reality: It is Pakistan's national dress, but it is also widely worn in India (especially Punjab), Bangladesh and Afghanistan, by people of many faiths.

    Myth: A turban means a person belongs to one particular religion.
    Reality: Turbans are worn by Sikhs, by many Pashtun men, by elders in Rajasthan, by some Hindu monks and by some Muslim scholars, among others. For Sikhs, the dastar has a specific religious meaning, but the turban itself belongs to many traditions.

    Myth: Khadi is just another word for cotton.
    Reality: Khadi means cloth that is both hand-spun and hand-woven. It can be cotton, silk or wool, and it became a symbol of self-reliance in India's freedom movement.

    Myth: "Paisley" is a Scottish design.
    Reality: The shape began as the buta motif on Kashmir shawls. Factories in Paisley, Scotland, copied it in the 1800s, and the town's name stuck to the pattern.

    Check Yourself¶

    1. Where were the oldest known cotton fibers found, and about how old are they?

    At Mehrgarh, an early village in Balochistan, Pakistan. They were preserved inside copper beads buried roughly 7,500 to 8,000 years ago, in the first half of the sixth millennium BCE.

    2. What is the difference between Jamdani and ikat?

    In Jamdani, the pattern is woven in on the loom, with extra weft threads added motif by motif. In ikat, the threads are tied and dyed in a pattern before weaving, which gives the shapes blurred edges.

    3. Name one draped garment and one stitched garment, and say who usually wears each.

    Draped: the saree (women), the dhoti or lungi (men). Stitched: the kurta (men and women) or the shalwar kameez (men and women).

    4. What are the gho and kira, and when are they required?

    They are Bhutan's national dress: the gho is a knee-length belted robe for men, and the kira is an ankle-length wrapped dress for women. Under Bhutan's dress code, men in government offices and schools must wear the gho, and both are worn for formal occasions.

    5. Why did Gandhi promote khadi?

    He wanted Indians to stop buying British machine-made cloth, which kept India dependent, and to spin and weave their own. Khadi became a symbol of self-reliance and the freedom movement.

      Answer

      Mulberry, tasar, eri and muga. Muga, a natural golden silk, is made almost entirely in Assam.

    2. In what year did UNESCO recognize Jamdani weaving, and in which country is it centered?

      Answer
      1. It is centered around Dhaka, Bangladesh.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why ikat patterns have blurry, feathered edges.

      Answer

      The pattern is tied and dyed into the threads before weaving. When the threads are stretched on the loom and woven, they never line up perfectly, so the edges of each shape come out slightly soft.

    2. Explain in your own words why the "paisley" pattern is named after a Scottish town.

      Answer

      The teardrop buta motif came from Kashmir shawls. When factories in Paisley, Scotland, made cheaper copies in the 1800s, English speakers started calling any shawl with that motif a Paisley shawl, and the name stuck to the shape.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • South Asia has spun and woven cotton for more than 7,000 years. The oldest known cotton fibers come from Mehrgarh in Pakistan, and Bengal's muslin was once famous worldwide.
    • Silk (mulberry, tasar, eri and muga) and pashmina wool from Himalayan goats are the region's other great fibers, behind Banarasi, Kanjivaram and Rajshahi silks and Kashmir and Nepali shawls.
    • Patterns can be woven in (Jamdani, recognized by UNESCO in 2013), dyed into the thread first (ikat) or printed on (ajrak from Sindh).
    • Khadi, hand-spun and hand-woven cloth, became a symbol of self-reliance in India's freedom movement.
    • Draped garments (saree, dhoti, lungi) and stitched ones (kurta, shalwar kameez, dupatta) cross national borders. Bhutan's gho and kira are a clear example of official national dress.
    • Turbans, bindis and bangles can carry religious or marriage meanings, like the Sikh dastar and kara or the Bengali shankha, but meanings vary by family, region and faith.
