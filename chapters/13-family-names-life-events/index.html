    Chapter 13: Family, Names, and Life Events¶

    Summary¶

    Family sits at the center of social life in much of South Asia, and you can hear that in the words people use. Many South Asian languages have separate words for your father's brother and your mother's brother, for your father's mother and your mother's mother. This chapter explains those kinship words, joint families and respect for elders, then looks at how names are chosen and why not everyone has a surname. It explains caste honestly, including the laws against caste discrimination, and walks through the rituals that mark birth, growing up, marriage and death in Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, Buddhist and other families, ending with how gender roles are changing.

    Who's Who at the Party?

    Hi, I'm Mora! If you've ever stood at a family wedding or a dinner and had no idea what to call the aunt who just pinched your cheek, this chapter is for you. Knowing the right word for each relative, and the story behind your own name, is one of the easiest ways to feel at home in your family. And if your friends are South Asian, it will help you follow what's happening at their family events.

    13.1 Four Uncles, One English Word¶

    It's a Saturday evening in June, and Zara Hossain's cousin is getting married in a rented hall in St. Paul. Ben Lindqvist is the only guest in a hockey-team blazer, and Zara is steering him through the crowd.

    "Ben, this is my Chacha," Zara says, and a smiling man shakes Ben's hand. Five minutes later: "This is my Mama." Another man, another handshake. Then a woman in a green saree hugs Zara and calls her by a name Ben has never heard. "That's my Khala," Zara explains. "And over there by the cake, that's my Fufu." Ben leans in. "Okay, wait. Your mama is a man? And those are all your uncles and aunts? In English I'd just say uncle, uncle, aunt, aunt."

    Zara laughs, but then she thinks about it. Chacha is her father's brother. Mama is her mother's brother. Khala is her mother's sister. Fufu is her father's sister. Her Nanu, her mother's mother, is sitting at the head table, and Zara would never mix that word up with Dadi, her father's mother. Ben wants to know why one language needs four words where English uses two. And then he asks the bigger question: what else about a South Asian family works differently from his own?

    13.2 One Word or Ten? Kinship Terms¶

    Ben's family already gives him a head start. His relatives are from Sweden and Norway, and in Swedish you don't say "uncle" either. Your father's brother is your farbror ("father-brother") and your mother's brother is your morbror ("mother-brother"). Your father's mother is farmor and your mother's mother is mormor. Swedish speakers can tell, from one word, which side of the family someone is on. Many South Asian languages work the same way, and often go further.

    Kinship Terms are the words a language uses for relatives: mother, uncle, cousin, grandmother and so on. Every language has them, but languages slice the family tree differently. English has one word, "uncle," for at least four different people: your father's older brother, your father's younger brother, your mother's brother, and the husbands of your aunts. Many South Asian languages give most of those people their own word.

    This table compares a few of them, using Hindi-Urdu, the language family many people in northern India and Pakistan speak (Hindi is usually written in Devanagari script and Urdu in Nastaliq script; see Chapter 6).

    Relative English Hindi-Urdu (many families)
    Father's older brother uncle tau (Hindi) or taya (Urdu)
    Father's younger brother uncle chacha
    Father's sister aunt bua (Hindi) or phuphi (Urdu)
    Mother's brother uncle mama (Urdu also mamun)
    Mother's sister aunt mausi (Hindi) or khala (Urdu)
    Father's mother grandmother dadi
    Mother's mother grandmother nani

    Two patterns jump out. First, side matters: the father's side and the mother's side get different words. Second, age matters: in many languages your father's older brother and younger brother are not the same kind of uncle.

    Zara's family speaks Bangla, and the words she used at the wedding come straight from that table. In Bangla her mother's mother is নানী (NAA-nee), and Zara uses the affectionate form, নানু (NAA-noo), "Nanu." Her mother's sister is খালা (KHAA-laa), "khala," and her father's sister is ফুফু (FOO-foo), "phuphu" or "fufu."

    Why separate words for each side?¶

    Part of the answer is about who you live with and who you inherit from. Many South Asian families have traditionally been patrilineal: the family name and property passed mainly through the father's line. Your father's brothers might live in your house, while your mother's brothers live in her birth family's home, often far away. They play different roles in your life, so they get different words.

    Languages of South India and Sri Lanka show a second pattern. In Tamil and Sinhala, your father's older brother is literally your "big father" (Tamil பெரியப்பா, peh-ree-YAP-paa; Sinhala ලොකු තාත්තා, LO-ku TAAT-taa), and your mother's older sister is your "big mother." Your father's sister and your mother's brother, though, get completely separate words.

    Anthropologists call this a Dravidian kinship system: relatives linked through a same-sex pair of siblings (your father's brother, your mother's sister) are treated almost like extra parents, while relatives linked through an opposite-sex pair (your father's sister, your mother's brother) belong to a different category. In this system, the children of your father's sister or your mother's brother were traditionally seen as possible marriage partners in some communities, which is one reason those relatives have their own terms.

    Not every language marks every difference. Tamil and Sinhala use the same word for both grandmothers (Tamil பாட்டி, PAAT-tee; Sinhala ආච්චි, AACH-chee), while Hindi-Urdu, Punjabi and Bangla split them by side (dadi and nani in Hindi-Urdu and Punjabi; many Hindu Bangla speakers say thakuma and didima instead).

    Same language, different words

    Kinship words change with region and community, even inside one language. Many Muslim Bangla speakers in Bangladesh say chacha and khala, while many Hindu Bangla speakers in West Bengal say kaka and mashi for the same relatives. Ask your family which words they use before assuming the dictionary is right.

    Words for people who aren't related¶

    Kinship words also travel outside the family. Kids are often taught to call a parent's friend "Uncle" or "Auntie," or "khala" or "chacha ji," which turns the whole community into a kind of extended family.

    13.3 The Joint Family and Respect for Elders¶

    Imagine living with your parents, your father's parents, and your father's brother with his wife and kids. Meals are cooked for twelve, and your cousins are more like siblings. That is a joint family.

    A Joint Family is a household where several generations and branches of one family live together and often share a kitchen, money and property. In the traditional form found in many parts of South Asia, the joint family is built around brothers: married sons stay in their parents' home with their wives and children, while daughters move to their husbands' families when they marry. In India, the law even recognizes a "Hindu undivided family" as a unit for income tax.

    Living with extended family is common across the region. A 2019 Pew Research Center study of household types found that extended-family households were the most common household type in the world, covering 38% of people. It also found that the average person in Afghanistan lived in a household of about 9.8 people, and in Pakistan about 8.5, far above the Asia-Pacific average of 5.0 (counting from each individual's point of view).

    Not every South Asian family is a joint family. Many city families are nuclear families (parents and children only), and many mix the two, like a diaspora family that brings a grandparent to live with them, as Zara's family did with Nanu.

    Respect for Elders¶

    Respect for Elders is the value that older relatives and community members deserve special honor, care and obedience, and that families should look after them as they age. You can see it in small daily habits across South Asia:

    • Speech. In Hindi-Urdu, you address an elder with the polite pronoun आप (aap), not the more casual तुम (tum). Many families add "ji" to a name or title as a mark of respect, like "Chacha ji." In many families children never call an older sibling or relative by their first name alone. They use a kinship word or "bhai" (brother) or "apa," "didi" or "baji" (sister), depending on the language.
    • Greetings. In many Hindu and Sikh families, younger people bend to touch an elder's feet as a greeting of respect, and the elder gives a blessing. In many Muslim families, younger people greet elders first with "salaam" and may be expected to stand when an elder enters.
    • Care. In many families, grown children are expected to care for aging parents at home rather than in a care facility.

    Respect for elders doesn't mean elders always get their way, and many teens feel a push and pull between honoring grandparents and making their own choices.

    13.4 What's in a Name?¶

    Ben's name is simple: a first name, Ben, and a family name, Lindqvist, which his father and his grandfather also had. Many people assume every name in the world works like that. In South Asia, it often doesn't.

    Naming Traditions are the customs a family follows to choose a child's name: who chooses, when, and what the name should mean or sound like. Here are some you might meet:

    • Sikh families. In Sikh tradition, a baby's name is often chosen in a Naam Karan ceremony at a gurdwara. The Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, is opened at random, and the first letter of the first hymn on that page becomes the first letter of the child's name. Most Sikh first names can be used for boys or girls. Then comes a shared name: Singh for men and Kaur for women. Sikh tradition links these names to Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru, and one aim of giving everyone the same name was to reduce caste prejudice. That's why Simran, the club's treasurer, is Simran Kaur Gill: Kaur is her Sikh name and Gill is her family name.
    • Muslim families. In many Muslim families, a baby is named in the first week, often on the seventh day, sometimes with an aqiqah, a celebration in which an animal is sacrificed and meat is shared with family and the poor. Many names are Arabic or Persian in origin and are chosen for their meaning or to honor a prophet or a relative.
    • Hindu families. The Hindu naming ceremony, the namakarana, is one of the traditional samskaras (Section 13.6). Some families ask a priest or consult astrology for a lucky first sound for the name.
    • Bengali families, both Hindu and Muslim, often give a child two names: a "good name," ভালো নাম (BHAA-lo naam, "bhalo nam"), used at school and on documents, and a "call name," ডাক নাম (DAAK naam, "dak nam"), a nickname used at home. Zara has one too, but she only lets her family use it.
    • Sherpa families in Nepal, like Nima's, traditionally name a child after the day of the week the baby was born. Nima (also spelled Nyima, "sun") is the name for Sunday, Dawa for Monday, Mingma for Tuesday, Lhakpa for Wednesday, Phurba for Thursday, Pasang for Friday and Pemba for Saturday. That's why so many Sherpas share the same few names.
    • Bhutanese families usually do not have family names at all. A Buddhist lama is often asked to choose the baby's name in the first weeks of life, and most people have two given names, such as Sonam or Tshering, Dorji or Karma. The names don't show which family a person belongs to, and many of them are given to both boys and girls.

    Surnames¶

    A Surname is a family name that is passed down from one generation to the next. In much of South Asia surnames exist, but they don't always work the way they do in Minnesota.

    • Many people have none. Traditionally, many Afghans used only a single given name, and so do many Bhutanese today. In Afghanistan, people were told apart by their tribe, birthplace or profession.
    • Some use the father's name. In many Tamil families in India and Sri Lanka, there is no inherited family name. Instead, a person's father's given name, often written as an initial, comes before their own name. So R. Karthik would be Karthik, son of someone whose name starts with R. His children would then use K as their initial. Some Tamils who move abroad turn the father's name into a last name so forms make sense.
    • Some reflect history. In Sri Lanka, Portuguese rule in the 1500s and 1600s left behind surnames such as Perera and Fernando, which became some of the most common names in the country. Dilan Perera's family name carries that history.
    • Some point to caste or community. In many parts of India and Nepal, a family name can show a person's caste or community. This is one reason some people have dropped their family names, used a religious name like Singh or Kaur, or chosen a new surname.
    • Some change at marriage, and some don't. In many families a woman takes her husband's family name when she marries, but many women keep their own.

    Getting a name right

    If a South Asian classmate's name doesn't fit the "first name, last name" boxes on a form, it isn't a mistake. Ask them how they'd like their name written and said, and use it.

    13.5 Caste: Varna, Jati, and the Law¶

    "Is caste still a thing?" Ben asks Ms. Okafor at a club meeting. She tells him it's a fair and serious question, and the honest answer is yes, in different ways, in many places.

    Varna and Jati¶

    People often use "caste" to mean two different things, so it helps to separate them.

    Varna and Jati are the two ideas behind what English speakers call caste. Varna (वर्ण, VAR-na) is a fourfold social classification described in old Brahmanical texts written in Sanskrit, such as the Manusmriti. Those texts list four varnas, Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas and Shudras, and assign each one duties and occupations. Jati (जाति, JAA-tee, "birth" or "kind") is the group a person is born into. There are thousands of jatis across South Asia. A jati is usually local, often linked in the past to a traditional occupation, and in many communities people were expected to marry within it. In everyday life, jati is what matters most. Varna is the textbook framework that people later tried to fit the jatis into.

    Communities that the varna texts left outside the four varnas were treated as "untouchable" and faced the harshest exclusion: they were kept out of temples, wells, schools and many jobs. Today many of these communities call themselves Dalit (दलित, DUH-lit), a word meaning "broken" or "crushed," which reformers began using in the 1800s and which Dalit activists have made a word of pride and protest.

    Mahatma Gandhi used the term "Harijan" ("children of God") for these communities in the 1930s. That word is now a historical term: many Dalits reject it as patronizing, and B. R. Ambedkar, the Dalit leader who chaired the committee that drafted India's Constitution, disliked it. Many tribal communities in India and Bangladesh call themselves Adivasi, a 20th-century word meaning "ancient inhabitants." They have their own histories and were largely outside the varna system.

    The caste system¶

    The Caste System is the social system, built over many centuries, in which people are born into ranked groups that shape whom they marry, what work they are expected to do, and how others treat them. It is historical and continuing: its roots go back more than two thousand years, and it still affects people's lives today. Three points help you understand it fairly.

    1. It isn't only Hindu. A 2021 Pew Research Center survey of about 30,000 adults in India found that Indians of every major religion, including Muslims, Christians and Sikhs, identify with a caste category. Sikh teachings reject caste, and Islam and Christianity teach the equality of believers, yet caste distinctions still appear in many of those communities.
    2. It isn't only in India. Caste shapes society in Nepal too, and caste-based communities also exist in other parts of South Asia and in the diaspora.
    3. It still affects marriage most of all. In the same Pew survey, 64% of Indians said it was very important to stop women in their community from marrying outside their caste, and 62% said the same about men. Most said their close friends were mostly from their own caste. Only about one in five said there was a lot of discrimination against Scheduled Castes, the government's legal term for Dalit communities.

    Caste Discrimination Laws¶

    Caste Discrimination Laws are laws that ban treating people unfairly because of their caste, and that protect communities who faced untouchability. The most important ones in South Asia are in India's Constitution, which came into effect on 26 January 1950:

    • Article 15 says the state shall not discriminate against any citizen only because of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.
    • Article 17 abolished "untouchability" and forbids practicing it in any form. Enforcing any disability that comes from untouchability is a crime.

    Parliament later passed laws to enforce these rules, including the Protection of Civil Rights Act (first passed in 1955 as the Untouchability (Offences) Act and renamed in 1976) and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act of 1989. India also reserves a share of seats in legislatures, government jobs and public universities for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other disadvantaged groups, a policy usually called reservations.

    Nepal passed the Caste-Based Discrimination and Untouchability (Offence and Punishment) Act in 2011, which made caste discrimination a crime in private places such as homes, not just in public ones. Human rights groups still report that these laws are not always enforced in both countries.

    The debate has reached the United States. In February 2023, Seattle became the first US city to add caste to its anti-discrimination laws. In September 2023, Fresno, California, did the same. That October, California's governor vetoed a bill that would have added caste to the state's civil rights law, saying existing protections against discrimination by ancestry already covered it. South Asian Americans were divided: some argued the laws protect people who face real discrimination, and others argued the laws single out South Asians and their faiths.

    Whose Story?

    People tell the story of caste very differently. Many Dalit writers and activists, following B. R. Ambedkar, describe caste as a system of oppression that religious texts helped justify and that must be ended completely. Many Hindus describe varna as a flexible idea about duty and talent that was later hardened into birth-based caste, and say that caste discrimination goes against their faith. Many Indian Muslims, Christians and Sikhs point out that their faiths reject caste, while researchers have documented caste divisions inside those same communities. In the United States, some South Asian groups see caste laws as civil rights protection, while others see them as unfairly targeting Hindus and Indians. Listening to all of these voices, and especially to people who have faced caste discrimination, is part of understanding the topic.

    Handle with care

    Never ask a classmate "what caste are you?" or guess someone's caste from their surname. It can be hurtful and is often discriminatory. If caste comes up, talk about the system and the laws, not about ranking any person or community.

    13.6 Life-Cycle Rituals and Coming of Age¶

    Every family marks big moments in a life: a birth, a first step into adulthood, a wedding, a death. Life-Cycle Rituals are the ceremonies that mark these turning points. In South Asia they are shaped by faith, region and family, and they usually bring the whole extended family together.

    In Hindu tradition: the samskaras¶

    In Hindu tradition, the life-cycle rituals are called samskaras (संस्कार, sun-SKAA-ruh, "making ready" or "refining"). Old texts list different numbers of them; a list of 16 became the best known, and the number and details vary by region and family. Some you'll hear about:

    • Namakarana, the naming ceremony.
    • Annaprashana ("first eating of rice"), when a baby eats solid food, often sweet rice pudding, for the first time, around six months old. In Nepal this is called pasni.
    • Mundan or chudakarana, a child's first haircut, when the head is shaved.
    • Upanayana, the sacred-thread ceremony (see Coming-of-Age Rites below).
    • Vivaha, marriage.
    • Antyeshti, the "last rite," the funeral.

    Muslim, Sikh, Buddhist and other traditions¶

    Muslim families mark a birth with prayers, by naming the baby and, in many families, with the aqiqah around the seventh day.

    Sikh families mark a birth with Naam Karan. Buddhist families in Sri Lanka, Bhutan and Nepal often invite monks to bless a new baby, and many Christian families in South Asia mark a child's place in the church with baptism.

    Coming-of-Age Rites¶

    Coming-of-Age Rites are ceremonies that mark the move from childhood toward adulthood or full membership in a community. Some examples from the region:

    • Upanayana (उपनयन, oo-puh-NUH-yun, "initiation"). In this Hindu ceremony, traditionally for boys from certain communities, a child receives the sacred thread, worn over the left shoulder, and is accepted as a student by a teacher. In Nepal it is called Bratabandha (व्रतबन्ध). Boys have their heads shaved, receive the thread, and make a symbolic first round asking relatives for alms.
    • Amrit Sanchar. In Sikh tradition, people who are ready to commit fully to the faith take amrit (water stirred with a double-edged sword called a khanda) in a ceremony led by five initiated Sikhs, recalling the Panj Pyare ("beloved five") whom Guru Gobind Singh first initiated, and join the Khalsa. There is no fixed age.
    • Puberty ceremonies for girls. In many Tamil families in Tamil Nadu and in the Tamil diaspora, a girl's first period has traditionally been marked by a family celebration, sometimes called manjal neerattu vizha, with a turmeric-water bath, new clothes, food and gifts. Rituals differ by community and region. Some families still hold big celebrations, while others, including some critics in Tamil Nadu, argue the custom puts unfair attention on girls and should change.

    Your family counts

    Your family might do some of these rituals, all of them, none of them, or a mix of old and new. That's normal. A short ceremony in a Minnesota apartment is just as real as a big one in Lahore or Dhaka.

    13.7 Weddings¶

    Weddings in South Asia are often multi-day events that join two families as well as two people, with separate ceremonies before, during and after the main rite. Guest lists of hundreds are common. Details differ a lot by faith, region and family budget, but a few pieces show up again and again.

    Arranged Marriage¶

    An Arranged Marriage is a marriage in which parents or other relatives help find and suggest a partner, and the couple then decides whether to go ahead. Arranged marriage has been common in South Asia for centuries and still is, alongside "love marriages," where the couple meets on their own.

    What arranged marriage looks like has changed a lot. In many families today, parents or relatives suggest possible matches, sometimes through friends, community networks or matrimonial websites and apps. The two young people meet, talk, sometimes over months, and either can say no. Some couples meet on their own and then ask their families to "arrange" the rest, which some writers call a "self-arranged" marriage.

    The most important word here is consent. An arranged marriage, where both people freely agree, is very different from a forced marriage, where one or both people are pressured or made to marry against their will. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 16) says marriage must be entered into only with the "free and full consent" of both people. Islamic law requires both the bride and the groom to consent, and Sikh teaching expects the couple to agree. In India, the Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 lets a court annul a marriage if consent was obtained by force or fraud. Forced marriage is a human rights abuse, and arranged marriage done with real consent is not.

    Mehndi Night and Sangeet¶

    The days before a wedding are often the most fun. Mehndi Night and Sangeet are two pre-wedding celebrations popular in many North Indian, Pakistani and diaspora families.

    • At a mehndi night, the bride's hands and feet are decorated with mehndi (henna), a paste made from a plant that stains the skin reddish brown. Artists draw flowers, vines, paisleys and peacocks, and female relatives and friends get small designs too. There's usually music, food and dancing.
    • A sangeet (संगीत, sun-GEET, "music") is a night of singing and dancing. In many families it was once a smaller gathering of women singing folk songs. Today many families stage dance performances by both sides of the family, often to film songs.

    In Bangladesh and West Bengal, both Muslim and Hindu families hold a gaye holud (গায়ে হলুদ, GAA-ye HO-lood, "turmeric on the body"), where friends and relatives smear turmeric paste on the bride and groom amid songs and yellow and orange clothes.

    Nikah¶

    A Nikah (نکاح, ni-KAAH) is the Islamic marriage contract and ceremony. At its heart, the bride and groom each give their consent in front of witnesses, and the groom agrees to give the bride the mahr, a gift of money or property that belongs to her alone. An imam or other officiant often leads the ceremony, recites prayers and verses from the Quran, and the couple and witnesses sign a marriage contract, called a nikah nama in Pakistan and Bangladesh. Afterward comes the walima, a wedding feast hosted and paid for by the groom's side. Many South Asian Muslim families combine the nikah with older regional customs like mehndi or gaye holud.

    Anand Karaj¶

    The Anand Karaj (ਅਨੰਦ ਕਾਰਜ, uh-NUND KAA-raj, "blissful ceremony") is the Sikh wedding ceremony. It takes place in front of the Guru Granth Sahib, usually in a gurdwara. Many Sikh weddings take place in the morning. The center of the ceremony is the laavan, four hymns composed by Guru Ram Das, the fourth Sikh Guru. As each hymn is sung, the couple walks around the Guru Granth Sahib, the groom leading the bride, who holds one end of a scarf that he holds too. After the fourth round, the couple is married. The Anand Marriage Act of 1909 gave Sikh marriages by this rite legal recognition in British India, and India amended the law in 2012 to let these marriages be registered under it.

    Hindu and Buddhist weddings¶

    In many Hindu weddings, the couple's marriage is witnessed by a sacred fire. The central moment is often the saptapadi ("seven steps"), when the couple takes seven steps or circles the fire together, making a promise at each one. Rituals vary a lot between, say, a Telugu wedding in Hyderabad like the ones Arjun's cousins have had, a Gujarati wedding and a Nepali wedding. In Sri Lanka, many Sinhala couples marry on a poruwa, a decorated wooden platform, in a ceremony with a traditional officiant, betel leaves and blessings from both families. Buddhist monks do not usually lead the wedding itself, and many Sri Lankans trace the poruwa custom to before Buddhism reached the island.

    13.8 Funeral Customs¶

    Death is hard to talk about in any culture, but knowing what a friend's family is doing helps you show up for them. Funeral Customs are the rites families follow when someone dies: how the body is cared for, the funeral itself, and the period of mourning afterward.

    • Hindu families usually cremate the body, often within a day. In the Antyeshti, a son or close relative traditionally lights the funeral pyre, and the ashes are later placed in a river, for many families the Ganga. A period of mourning follows, and rituals called shraddha honor the dead in later years. Details vary by region, community and the age of the person who died.
    • Muslim families bury the body as soon as possible, often within a day. The body is washed and wrapped in simple white cloth, a funeral prayer called the janazah is said, and the person is buried lying on their right side, with the face turned toward Mecca. Cremation is not allowed in Islam.
    • Sikh families usually cremate the body. In the Antim Sanskar ("final rite") the family recites prayers, and the ashes are scattered in running water. Sikh tradition discourages gravestones or monuments.
    • Buddhist families in Sri Lanka, Nepal and Bhutan may cremate or bury. In Sri Lanka, families often offer food (dana) to monks on the seventh day after a death, again about three months later, and at the end of a year, dedicating the merit to the person who has died.
    • Parsi (Zoroastrian) families in Mumbai have traditionally placed the dead in a dakhma, or "tower of silence," where the body is exposed to the sun and birds instead of being buried or burned. As vultures have become rare, some families now choose cremation or burial.

    If a friend's relative dies, a simple "I'm so sorry" is always welcome. Ask your friend before attending a service so you know what to expect.

    13.9 Gender Roles and Change¶

    Back at the wedding, Ben notices that the women are mostly in the kitchen and the men are mostly greeting guests. Then he notices Zara's Khala, a surgeon, giving a speech, and Zara's cousin, the bride, signing her own marriage contract. Both scenes are common in South Asian families today.

    Gender Roles and Change is about the expectations families and societies have for women and men, and how those expectations are shifting. In many traditional South Asian families, men were expected to earn money and represent the family in public, and women were expected to run the home, raise children and care for elders, often in their husband's family's house. Daughters often received less schooling than sons and moved away at marriage.

    Surveys show that many of these expectations remain strong, but views differ. In a Pew Research Center survey of about 30,000 Indian adults, carried out in 2019 and 2020 and published in 2022:

    • 54% said both men and women should be responsible for earning money for the family, while 43% said it is mainly men's job.
    • 62% said both parents should share responsibility for taking care of children, while 34% said it is mainly women's job.
    • About nine in ten agreed, at least mostly, that a wife must always obey her husband.

    At the same time, South Asia has been home to some of the world's firsts for women in politics. In 1960, Sirimavo Bandaranaike of Sri Lanka (then Ceylon) became the world's first woman elected prime minister. In 1988, Benazir Bhutto of Pakistan became the first woman elected to lead a democratic government in a Muslim-majority country. Women have also led governments in India and Bangladesh, and in 2025 Sushila Karki became Nepal's first woman prime minister, leading an interim government until a new government took office after the March 2026 election.

    Change is happening at home too, as more couples choose each other and many diaspora families split chores in new ways, while keeping roles they value, like grandparents' place in raising children. Change looks different in a village in Sindh, an apartment in Dhaka and a Minnesota suburb.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: All South Asian marriages are forced.
    Reality: Many marriages are arranged, which means families help find a partner, but in an arranged marriage both people freely agree and can say no. Forced marriage, without real consent, is a human rights abuse that South Asian faiths and laws reject. Many South Asians also choose their own partners.

    Myth: Caste only exists in India, and only in the past.
    Reality: Caste is a historical and continuing system. It still shapes marriage and social life in India and Nepal, appears among people of many faiths, and has become a civil rights issue in the diaspora, including in Seattle and California in 2023. Laws in India and Nepal make caste discrimination a crime.

    Myth: Everyone has a first name and a family surname.
    Reality: Many Bhutanese people and, traditionally, many Afghans have no family name. Many Tamils use their father's name as an initial instead of a surname, and Sherpa children are often named for the day they were born.

    Myth: Joint families are disappearing everywhere.
    Reality: Living patterns are changing, especially in cities and abroad, but extended-family households are still common in South Asia. A 2019 Pew study found that the average person in Afghanistan and Pakistan lived in a household of more than eight people, and many diaspora families still bring grandparents to live with them.

    Check Yourself¶

    1. Why do many South Asian languages have different words for a father's brother and a mother's brother?

    In many patrilineal families, the father's brothers belonged to the same household and line of inheritance, while the mother's brothers belonged to another family and played a different role, such as a special part at weddings. Different roles led to different words.

    2. What is the difference between varna and jati?

    Varna is a fourfold classification described in old Sanskrit texts. Jati is the local birth group a person actually belongs to; there are thousands of them, and they shape marriage and community life.

    3. What did Article 17 of India's Constitution do?

    It abolished "untouchability," forbade practicing it in any form, and made enforcing any disability arising from it a crime.

    4. What is the difference between an arranged marriage and a forced marriage?

    In an arranged marriage, families help find a partner, but both people freely consent and can refuse. In a forced marriage, at least one person is made to marry without real consent.

    5. What happens during the laavan in an Anand Karaj?

    Four hymns by Guru Ram Das are sung, and as each one is sung the couple walks around the Guru Granth Sahib. After the fourth round, they are married.

      Answer

    2. What is the Sikh name given to all women, and what is the name given to all men?

      Answer

      Kaur for women and Singh for men.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain in your own words what a joint family is and why it might lead to different words for relatives on each side.

      Answer

      A joint family is a household of several generations and branches, traditionally married brothers living with their parents, wives and children. Because the father's relatives live together and the mother's relatives live elsewhere, they play different roles, and languages give them different names.

    2. Explain why a Tamil speaker might call their father's older brother "big father."

      Answer

      In the Dravidian kinship system, relatives linked through same-sex siblings (like a father's brother) are treated almost like parents, so the father's older brother is periyappa, "big father."

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Many South Asian languages use different kinship terms for the father's side and mother's side, and for older and younger relatives, while English often uses one word like "uncle."
    • Joint families and respect for elders are strong traditions across the region, but households are flexible and changing, especially in cities and abroad.
    • Naming traditions vary widely: Sikh names chosen from the Guru Granth Sahib, Sherpa day names, Bengali "good names" and "call names," and many people without family surnames.
    • The caste system (built on varna and jati) is historical and continuing. India's Constitution and laws in India and Nepal ban caste discrimination, and the issue now reaches the diaspora.
    • Life-cycle rituals and coming-of-age rites differ by faith and region, from samskaras and Bratabandha to aqiqah and Amrit Sanchar.
    • South Asian weddings often last several days. An arranged marriage depends on the couple's consent, unlike a forced marriage, and the Nikah and Anand Karaj each have their own heart: consent and contract, or four hymns around the scripture.
    • Funeral customs differ by faith, and gender roles are changing at different speeds in different places.
