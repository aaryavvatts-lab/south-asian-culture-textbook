    3. 16. Science, Math, and Ideas

    Chapter 16: Science, Math, and Ideas¶

    Summary¶

    The digits you write every day, 0 through 9, have a long history that runs through South Asia. This chapter follows that history from Brahmi numerals carved on stone, to the place value system and the zero, to the scholars who carried "Indian reckoning" to Baghdad and on to Europe. It also meets mathematicians such as Aryabhata, Brahmagupta and the Kerala School, the grammarian Panini, healing traditions like Ayurveda and Unani, the board game chaturanga, wootz steel, and modern scientists from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Along the way, it shows how historians test big claims and sometimes change their minds.

    Numbers have a family tree too

    Hi, it's Mora! Every time you type a phone number, check a game score or scroll a price tag, you're using an idea that people in South Asia helped build more than a thousand years ago. Knowing that story can make you proud without needing to exaggerate it, and it helps you answer when someone says the region "never invented anything." Every tradition has a story. Let's find it!

    16.1 "Why Are They Called Hindu-Arabic?"¶

    It's a Wednesday in October, and Ben Lindqvist walks out of geometry class at Prairie Ridge High School looking puzzled. He catches up with Zara Hossain at her locker.

    "Okay, weird question," he says. "Mr. Dahl just said our numbers aren't 'Arabic numerals.' He called them 'Hindu-Arabic numerals.' He said they started in India. Is that true? Did your family know that?"

    Zara thinks about it. Her Nanu does sums in her head faster than anyone Zara knows, and she counts in Bangla under her breath when she's measuring rice. But Zara has never once wondered where the digits themselves came from. "I honestly don't know," she admits. "I didn't even know numbers had a place they came from."

    They decide to make it the first "ideas" topic for their club, and they write the question on the whiteboard: how did a way of writing numbers from South Asia end up in every math class on Earth, and what else did people in the region figure out along the way?

    16.2 Start With an Odometer¶

    Before any history, try a thought experiment.

    Picture the mileage counter in an old car, the kind with rolling number wheels. When the ones wheel passes 9, it rolls back to 0 and nudges the tens wheel up by one. When the tens wheel passes 9, it nudges the hundreds. Every wheel uses the same ten symbols, but each wheel is worth ten times as much as the wheel to its right.

    That is the whole trick behind the way you write numbers today. In 507, the 5 means five hundreds, the 0 means "no tens," and the 7 means seven ones. Notice the 0. Without it, 507 and 57 would look alike. The zero is holding an empty seat so the other digits stay in the right places.

    Now compare that with Roman numerals, which you've seen on clocks and in movie credits. In Roman numerals, 507 is DVII: D (500) plus V (5) plus I plus I. The letters keep their value wherever they sit, and there is no symbol for "nothing in this place." That makes Roman numerals fine for labels like "Super Bowl LX," but slow for multiplying or dividing large numbers on paper.

    Idea Roman numerals Our digits today
    Number of symbols 7 letters (I, V, X, L, C, D, M) 10 digits (0-9)
    Does position change value? Mostly no Yes, each place is worth 10 times the next
    Symbol for an empty place None 0
    2026 written out MMXXVI 2026
    Easy to do long multiplication? Hard Much easier

    Keep the odometer in mind. The story of this chapter is how people in South Asia, building on ideas from many places, put those wheels together.

    16.3 Brahmi Numerals: The Oldest Branch¶

    Brahmi Numerals are the number signs written with the Brahmi script, the ancestor of most South Asian scripts that you met in Chapter 6. They appear on inscriptions from the 3rd century BCE. One of the earliest examples is the number 256 written in an edict of the emperor Ashoka at Sasaram, in today's Bihar, from about 250 BCE.

    Brahmi numerals were not yet a place value system. They had separate signs for 1 through 9, and then extra signs for 10, 20, 30 and so on, and for 100 and 1,000. To write 256, a scribe would use the sign for 200, then the sign for 50, then the sign for 6. There was no zero, because no empty place ever needed filling.

    Even so, these signs matter a lot. Historians of mathematics trace the shapes of our modern digits, through many small changes over many centuries, back to Brahmi. The first three signs seem to have started as simple strokes, one, two and three lines, which is easy to imagine if you've ever kept score with tally marks.

    Some descendants of Brahmi numerals are still used today. Look at the same ten digits in four South Asian writing systems:

    Value Our digits Devanagari (Hindi, Marathi, Nepali) Bengali-Assamese (Bangla) Tamil Urdu (Eastern Arabic style)
    zero 0 ० ০ ௦ ۰
    one 1 १ ১ ௧ ۱
    two 2 २ ২ ௨ ۲
    five 5 ५ ৫ ௫ ۵
    nine 9 ९ ৯ ௯ ۹

    Zara's Nanu can read the Bangla digits at a glance, because they still appear in newspapers and on calendars in Bangladesh. Urdu newspapers and shop signs in Pakistan often use the Eastern Arabic style of digits, which, as you'll see in Section 16.6, came back to the region after a long journey through the Arab and Persian world. Tamil is a special case: traditional Tamil numbering had its own signs for ten (௰), one hundred (௱) and one thousand (௲), and in everyday life today most Tamil speakers simply use the digits 0 to 9.

    16.4 Place Value and the Zero¶

    The place value system¶

    A Place Value System is a way of writing numbers in which a digit's value depends on its position, with each position worth a fixed multiple of the one beside it. Ours is base ten, so each place is worth ten times the place to its right:

    \[ 507 = 5 \times 100 + 0 \times 10 + 7 \times 1 \]

    People in several parts of the world came up with place value. The Babylonians of ancient Iraq used a base-60 system (which is why an hour has 60 minutes). The Maya of Central America used a base-20 system. The South Asian version was base ten, used just nine signs plus a zero, and turned out to be the one that spread around the world.

    By around 600 CE, Indian scribes were writing dates and other numbers in place value form. The oldest dated Indian document that uses place value numbers is a donation charter from 594 CE, though some historians think its date was added later by a forger.

    Zero as a placeholder and as a number¶

    Zero is both a symbol that marks an empty place in a number and a number in its own right, the number you get when you subtract any number from itself. Those are two different ideas, and they didn't arrive at the same time.

    The first idea, an empty-place marker, appeared in more than one civilization. Babylonian scribes started inserting a special mark for an empty place by about 400 BCE. Greek astronomers, including Ptolemy around 130 CE, used a small circle as a placeholder in their tables. The Maya had a zero sign by 665 CE, completely on their own. In South Asia, early manuscripts marked an empty place with a dot.

    In Sanskrit and Hindi, the word for zero is शून्य (SHOON-yuh), which also means "empty" or "void." In Bangla it is শূন্য (SHOON-no). The idea of "emptiness" was also important in Buddhist philosophy, and some historians think that way of thinking made it natural to treat "nothing" as something you could name and work with. That's a reasonable suggestion, but it's hard to prove.

    The second idea, zero as a number you can add, subtract and multiply, is where South Asian mathematicians made a clear first. You'll meet the person who wrote the rules down in Section 16.5.

    The oldest securely dated zero in an Indian inscription that every historian accepts is carved in a small temple at Gwalior, in today's Madhya Pradesh, from 876 CE. It appears in ordinary numbers written into the inscription. By then, zero was already so normal that nobody bothered to explain it.

    The Bakhshali manuscript: a lesson in how history works¶

    In 1881, a farmer digging in a field at the village of Bakhshali, near Peshawar in what is now Pakistan, found a bundle of birch-bark pages covered in mathematics. The Bakhshali manuscript is a set of 70 fragile leaves with rules and worked problems, and it uses a dot for zero many times. In 1902 it was given to the Bodleian Library at the University of Oxford, where it is still kept.

    In 2017, the Bodleian announced that radiocarbon tests suggested some of its leaves dated from as early as the 3rd or 4th century CE. Newspapers around the world reported that the "oldest zero" had been found. But a group of historians of Indian mathematics, writing in the journal History of Science in South Asia, pushed back. They pointed out that the test results for different leaves were centuries apart, so the oldest leaf could not date the whole manuscript. The latest leaf, they argued, was a better guide to when the scribe actually wrote it. They also criticized announcing the result by press release before other experts could check it.

    Then, in 2024, Oxford's radiocarbon lab published a new report on five leaves. It said the very early result for one leaf had been inaccurate, and all five leaves now dated to roughly the late 700s to the early 1000s CE. So the Bakhshali manuscript is still an important early mathematical text, but it is no longer counted as older than the Gwalior inscription.

    Notice what happened. A big claim was made, experts questioned it, the lab tested again, and the answer changed. That is how good history and good science are supposed to work.

    Two kinds of nothing

    Here's a way to keep the two ideas straight. The 0 in 507 is a parking spot saved for "no tens," while the 0 in "5 minus 5 equals 0" is an actual answer you can do math with. Which one do you think is harder to invent, and why?

    16.5 Aryabhata and Brahmagupta¶

    Two names come up again and again when people talk about early South Asian mathematics.

    Aryabhata¶

    Aryabhata (476 to 550 CE) was a mathematician and astronomer who worked at Kusumapura, probably near Pataliputra (today's Patna, in Bihar), during the time of the Gupta kings. He wrote that he finished his book, the Aryabhatiya, in 499, when he was just 23 years old. It's a short book written in verse, so students could memorize it.

    Some of what's in it:

    • He gave a rule that works out to \( \pi \approx \frac{62832}{20000} = 3.1416 \), which is correct to four decimal places.
    • He explained that the stars seem to wheel across the sky because the Earth itself rotates on its axis. Many later astronomers rejected that idea, but Aryabhata was right.
    • He worked with place value, using the Sanskrit word kha ("space") for an empty position, and gave methods for square and cube roots.

    India named its very first satellite Aryabhata, after the 5th-century astronomer. It was launched on 19 April 1975 on a Soviet rocket. You'll meet India's space agency in Chapter 17.

    Brahmagupta¶

    Brahmagupta (born 598 CE) was an astronomer and mathematician who wrote the Brahmasphutasiddhanta in 628 CE at Bhillamala (today Bhinmal, in Rajasthan). He later led the astronomical observatory at Ujjain.

    Brahmagupta's book is the first known text to treat zero as a number with its own rules. He defined zero as what you get when you subtract a number from itself. He also wrote rules for negative numbers, which he called "debts," and positive numbers, which he called "fortunes." A few of his rules, in modern words:

    • A fortune plus zero is a fortune. A debt minus zero is a debt.
    • Zero times any number is zero.
    • A debt times a debt is a fortune. (In other words, a negative times a negative is positive.)

    He didn't get everything right. He said that zero divided by zero is zero, which mathematicians today say is undefined. Later Indian mathematicians, such as Mahavira and Bhaskara, also struggled with dividing by zero. That's nothing to be embarrassed about, since dividing by zero still confuses people today, and Brahmagupta was the first person we know of who even tried to write down the rules.

    Aryabhata Brahmagupta
    Dates 476 to 550 CE born 598 CE
    Where he worked Kusumapura, near Patna Bhillamala (Bhinmal), later Ujjain
    Main book Aryabhatiya (499) Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628)
    Best-known idea Earth's rotation; \( \pi \approx 3.1416 \) First rules for zero and negative numbers

    16.6 How the Numbers Traveled: Hindu-Arabic Numerals¶

    Hindu-Arabic Numerals are the ten digits 0 through 9 and the place value system that goes with them, which began in South Asia and reached Europe through scholars writing in Arabic. Historians use the double name to give credit to both parts of the journey. (Here "Hindu" is an old word meaning "Indian," the way people used it at the time, not a statement about religion.)

    This simplified timeline shows that journey.

    When Where What happened
    662 CE Syria (on the Euphrates) A Christian bishop, Severus Sebokht, praised the Indians' way of calculating with nine signs.
    776 CE Baghdad An Indian scholar brought an astronomy book to the court of the caliph, and it was translated into Arabic. Some historians think it was Brahmagupta's.
    800s Baghdad The scholar al-Khwarizmi wrote a book on Indian reckoning. Its Latin title was Algoritmi de numero Indorum, and his name gave English the word algorithm.
    mid-900s Arab world Al-Uqlidisi wrote the oldest surviving book on "Hindu arithmetic" for pen and paper.
    976 Spain A monk copied the digits into a manuscript, the first surviving example in Europe.
    1202 Pisa, Italy Fibonacci published Liber abaci, teaching the nine figures and zero, which he had learned as a boy in North Africa.
    1600s Europe Zero and the new digits finally came into wide everyday use.

    In Arabic, the zero was called صفر (SIFR), meaning "empty." That word passed into Latin and then into English as "cipher."

    The digits changed shape as they moved. Scribes in Baghdad and Persia developed one set of forms, the Eastern Arabic digits that many Urdu, Persian and Dari writers still use. Scribes in North Africa and Spain developed another, which became the digits in this book. So when Ben's teacher said "Hindu-Arabic," he was describing a real relay race across three continents.

    A math word with a passport

    Next time your computer science teacher says "algorithm," remember it comes from al-Khwarizmi, a scholar in Baghdad who wrote about Indian numbers. That one word has passed through three regions over more than a thousand years.

    16.7 Al-Biruni: A Scholar Who Asked Questions¶

    Al-Biruni (973 to 1048) was a Muslim scholar born in Khwarazm in Central Asia, who wrote one of the most careful early books about South Asian science and society. After 1017 he went to Ghazni (in today's Afghanistan) after Mahmud of Ghazni conquered his home region, probably as a prisoner at first, and he spent the rest of his life connected to that court. He traveled into the Punjab and northern India with Mahmud's expeditions and measured the positions of towns there.

    What makes al-Biruni special is how he studied. He learned Sanskrit so he could read Indian texts in the original, and he translated several of them into Arabic. Around 1030 he finished a long book usually called India. It describes Indian religion, philosophy, social customs, writing, numbers, geography, astronomy and the calendar. He compared Indian ideas with Greek ones, pointed out where he thought each side was right or wrong, and tried to describe beliefs as their followers understood them.

    He wrote it during a time of war, when Mahmud's armies were raiding Indian cities and temples. So al-Biruni is a complicated figure: a scholar who admired Indian learning, working for a ruler many Indians remember as a destroyer. Historians read his book both as a valuable record and as one outsider's view.

    16.8 The Kerala School: Infinite Series¶

    Jump forward to the 1300s and travel to the southwestern coast of India, to Kerala.

    The Kerala School was a line of astronomers and mathematicians in Kerala, beginning in the 1300s, who discovered ways to write some important quantities as infinite series, sums that go on forever but get closer and closer to an exact value. Its founder was Madhava of Sangamagrama (about 1350 to 1425), who lived near Kochi (formerly Cochin). His own writings are lost, but students such as Nilakantha and Jyesthadeva recorded his methods. Jyesthadeva's book, the Yuktibhasha (about 1550), was written in Malayalam, the language of Kerala, rather than Sanskrit, and it explains the proofs.

    One of Madhava's results is a series for \( \pi \):

    \[ \frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{9} - \cdots \]

    Madhava also found series for sine, cosine and arctangent, the same ones you'll meet if you take calculus, and he used extra correction terms to calculate \( \pi \) to 11 decimal places.

    European mathematicians found the same series independently more than two centuries later. The Scottish mathematician James Gregory rediscovered the arctangent series in the 1600s, and Isaac Newton found the sine and cosine series around 1676. (The \( \pi \) series above is often called the Madhava-Leibniz series, after Madhava and the German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz.)

    Some writers claim the Kerala results traveled to Europe through missionaries and traders, but there is no solid evidence for that, so historians call the European results rediscoveries. It is fair to say that the Kerala School found infinite series centuries before Europe. It is not accurate to say that Kerala "invented calculus," because calculus as a full system, with derivatives and integrals linked together, was built later.

    16.9 Panini's Grammar¶

    Not every big idea in this chapter is about numbers.

    Panini's Grammar, the Ashtadhyayi ("eight chapters"), is a complete description of the Sanskrit language written as just under 4,000 short rules called sutras. Panini came from Shalatula, a town near Attock on the Indus River, in what is now Pakistan. Nobody knows exactly when he lived. Scholars have suggested dates from about the 7th to the 4th century BCE.

    Why does a grammar book belong in a chapter about science? Because of how it works. Panini's rules are written like a computer program. Short rules apply in a set order, some rules override others, and special marker sounds act like labels that tell you which rule to use. Feed the rules a root word and some endings, and they produce correct Sanskrit sentences.

    In 1959, the computer scientist John Backus created a notation for describing programming languages, now called Backus-Naur Form. Historians of mathematics have pointed out that Panini's notation is just as powerful, and it is more than 2,000 years older. That's why Panini is often called a forerunner of the formal language theory used in computer science, and why linguists around the world still study him.

    Whose Story?

    Ask "Who invented zero?" and you'll hear different answers depending on who is speaking. Many popular books and speeches in India tell it as a proud national achievement, sometimes with dates pushed earlier than the evidence supports. In the Arab world the digits are often called "Indian numbers," while for centuries Europeans called the same digits "Arabic," because that's who they learned them from. Historians of mathematics such as the team behind the MacTutor archive tell a longer relay story: Babylonian placeholders, Greek astronomers, Indian place value and zero, Arabic-language scholars, then Europe. And the Bakhshali debate shows that even experts in Britain, the United States, France, Japan, New Zealand and Canada can disagree about the evidence until new tests settle it.

    16.10 Healing Traditions¶

    Not medical advice

    This section describes healing traditions as history and culture. They are traditions, not proven treatments, and nothing here is medical advice. If you or someone in your family has a health problem, talk to a doctor or pharmacist, and tell them about any traditional remedies you use.

    Many South Asian families keep healing traditions alive at home: turmeric in warm milk for a cold, ginger tea for an upset stomach, oil massages for babies, morning stretches. These habits connect to long written traditions. Here are four of the best known.

    Ayurveda¶

    Ayurveda (आयुर्वेद, AA-yur-vay-duh, "knowledge of life" or "science of health") is a traditional medical system from South Asia, based on old Sanskrit texts, that uses diet, herbs, daily routines, exercise and sometimes minerals to try to keep the body in balance. It is still widely practiced in India, Nepal and Sri Lanka, often alongside modern medicine, and India has a government ministry for traditional systems called AYUSH.

    What does science say? The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), part of the National Institutes of Health, reports (in its fact sheet last updated in January 2019) that only a few well-designed studies have tested Ayurvedic treatments, and that scientific evidence for most uses is limited. It also warns about safety: some Ayurvedic products sold online or abroad have been found to contain toxic levels of lead, mercury or arsenic, and the US Food and Drug Administration has warned about them. Many families value Ayurveda as part of their heritage, and that heritage is worth understanding. But it is not a substitute for medical care.

    The Sushruta Samhita¶

    The Sushruta Samhita is an old Sanskrit medical text, traditionally linked to a physician named Sushruta, that is famous for its detailed descriptions of surgery. Its 186 chapters describe surgical instruments, how to practice cutting on gourds and other objects before operating on people, and many operations. The most famous is a way to rebuild a damaged nose using a flap of skin from the cheek, a forerunner of what surgeons later called the "Indian method" of nose reconstruction.

    When was it written? Traditional accounts place Sushruta very early, but the text we have was built up in layers over a long time. Many scholars think its composition began in the last centuries BCE and that the version we read today took shape by around the 3rd or 4th century CE. The oldest surviving manuscript is a palm-leaf copy kept at the Kaiser Library in Kathmandu, Nepal, dated 878 CE. The book was later translated into Arabic in Baghdad, another example of ideas moving west.

    Unani medicine¶

    Unani Medicine (یونانی, yoo-NAA-nee, "Greek") is a healing tradition that began with the ancient Greek physicians Hippocrates and Galen, was developed by doctors writing in Arabic and Persian such as Ibn Sina (Avicenna), and came to South Asia by the 1100s. It grew under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal emperors, and its practitioners are often called hakims. Unani is based on the old Greek idea that health depends on balancing four "humors" in the body, an idea that came long before scientists discovered germs.

    Today Unani is officially recognized in India, where it is part of the AYUSH system, and in Pakistan, where practitioners register with the National Council for Tibb (tibb is the Arabic and Urdu word for medicine). Like Ayurveda, it is a tradition with a long history, not a proven way to cure disease.

    Yoga¶

    Yoga (योग, YOH-guh, "joining" or "union") is a set of practices rooted in South Asian philosophy that combines body postures, breathing and meditation, and that began as a spiritual discipline aimed at training the mind. It is important in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions. The poses many Americans learn at a gym are only one part of a much bigger tradition.

    On 11 December 2014, the United Nations proclaimed 21 June the International Day of Yoga. The NCCIH says research suggests yoga may help with stress, sleep, balance and some kinds of pain, but it does not claim yoga cures any disease, and it notes that beginners should avoid extreme poses and practice with a qualified teacher.

    Tradition Where it comes from Key idea Status today
    Ayurveda Sanskrit texts of South Asia Balance through diet, herbs, routine Practiced widely; limited scientific evidence; some products unsafe
    Sushruta Samhita Sanskrit medical text Surgery, instruments, nose repair Studied as medical history
    Unani Greek medicine, developed in Arabic and Persian Balance of four humors Recognized in India and Pakistan; traditional, not proven
    Yoga South Asian philosophy Posture, breath, meditation Practiced worldwide; may help stress and some pain

    16.11 Games and Metals: Chaturanga and Wootz Steel¶

    Chaturanga¶

    Chaturanga is an old board game from India, first clearly mentioned in the 6th or 7th century CE, that is the earliest known ancestor of chess. The name is Sanskrit for "four limbs," meaning the four parts of an old Indian army: elephants, chariots, cavalry (horses) and infantry (foot soldiers). It was played on an 8-by-8 board called the ashtapada. One of the earliest texts to name it is the Harshacharita, a biography of King Harsha written around 625 CE.

    From India the game traveled to Persia, where it became chatrang and then shatranj. Arabic-speaking players spread shatranj across the Middle East and North Africa, and it reached Europe in the Middle Ages. Over the centuries the pieces changed. The elephant became the bishop, the chariot (rukh in Persian) became the rook, and the counselor, a weak piece that moved one square, was renamed the queen in Europe and later became the strongest piece on the board. So chess as you play it today is a group project, with an Indian beginning.

    Wootz steel¶

    Wootz Steel is a high-carbon steel made in South India and Sri Lanka by melting iron with carbon-rich material inside sealed clay pots called crucibles, and it was famous for centuries for its strength and its wavy surface pattern. The English word "wootz" is a garbled form of ukku, the word for steel in Kannada and Telugu. Archaeological finds in Tamil Nadu suggest crucible steel was being made there before the Common Era.

    Traders carried wootz ingots west, and smiths in the Middle East forged them into the famous "Damascus" swords, which had very high carbon content, about 1.5 to 2.0 percent. In the early 1800s, European scientists, including Michael Faraday, studied wootz to learn how it was made.

    Sri Lanka has its own steel story. At Samanalawewa, in Sri Lanka's hill country, archaeologist Gill Juleff found many ancient furnaces built along the western edges of hills and ridges. Her experiments, published in the journal Nature in 1996, showed that these furnaces used the strong monsoon winds to blow air through the fire and could make high-carbon steel. They supported a major industry there during the first millennium CE.

    16.12 Modern Scientists of the Region¶

    The story doesn't stop in the distant past. Here are four modern scientists, from three of today's countries.

    Srinivasa Ramanujan¶

    Srinivasa Ramanujan (1887 to 1920) was a mathematician from Tamil Nadu, mostly self-taught, whose discoveries about numbers, infinite series and fractions still keep mathematicians busy today. He was born in Erode and grew up in Kumbakonam. In 1912 he took a job as a clerk at the Madras Port Trust in Madras (now Chennai), and he kept filling notebooks with formulas. In January 1913 he wrote to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, who was amazed and invited him to England. Ramanujan arrived in 1914.

    In 1918 he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society, one of the highest honors in science, and a Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge. But he was often ill in England. He was a strict vegetarian, and during World War I the foods he needed became hard to find. He returned to India and died in Kumbakonam in 1920, at just 32.

    C. V. Raman¶

    C. V. Raman (Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman, 1888 to 1970) was a physicist born in Tiruchirappalli, Tamil Nadu, who discovered that when light bounces off molecules, a tiny part of it comes back with a slightly different color. This is now called the Raman effect. He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930 "for his work on the scattering of light and for the discovery of the effect named after him," while working at Calcutta University (now the University of Calcutta). Today, scientists around the world use Raman's discovery, in a method called Raman spectroscopy, to identify what materials are made of.

    Satyendra Nath Bose and Abdus Salam¶

    Two more names show how science in the region crosses today's borders.

    • Satyendra Nath Bose (1894 to 1974) was a physicist born in Calcutta who taught at the University of Dhaka, in today's Bangladesh, from 1921. In 1924 he sent a short paper to Albert Einstein, who was so impressed that he translated it into German and had it published. Particles that follow Bose's rules are called bosons in his honor, including the famous Higgs boson.
    • Abdus Salam (1926 to 1996) was born in Jhang, in the Punjab of British India, now in Pakistan. He shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics for helping show that two of nature's basic forces, electromagnetism and the weak force, are two sides of one force.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: Indians invented all of mathematics.
    Reality: South Asian mathematicians made major contributions, especially place value with zero, rules for zero and negative numbers, and early infinite series. But mathematics was built by many peoples, including Babylonians, Egyptians, Greeks, Chinese, Maya and scholars writing in Arabic and Persian. Overclaiming actually hides the real achievements.

    Myth: Zero was invented by one person on one day.
    Reality: Zero grew over centuries. Placeholder marks appeared in Babylon, Greece, the Maya world and South Asia. Brahmagupta, in 628 CE, was the first known writer to give rules for zero as a number, but he was building on earlier Indian place value and the dot used for an empty place.

    Myth: Ayurveda is proven medicine that can cure diseases.
    Reality: Ayurveda is a long-standing tradition that many families value. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says there are few well-designed studies of it, and some products have contained dangerous levels of lead, mercury or arsenic. It is not a substitute for medical care. This is not medical advice.

    Myth: Chess was invented in India in its modern form.
    Reality: Chaturanga, the ancestor of chess, comes from India. But the game changed a lot as it moved through Persia, the Arab world and Europe. The powerful queen, for example, is a much later European change.

    Myth: "Arabic numerals" were invented by Arabs.
    Reality: The digits and place value system began in South Asia. Scholars writing in Arabic, such as al-Khwarizmi and al-Uqlidisi, developed and spread them, and Europeans learned them from Arabic books, which is why historians say "Hindu-Arabic."

    Check Yourself¶

    1. What job does the zero do in the number 305?

    It holds the empty tens place, so the 3 stays in the hundreds place. Without the zero the number would look like 35.

    2. Why couldn't Brahmi numerals be called a place value system?

    They used separate signs for tens, hundreds and thousands instead of reusing nine digits in different positions, and they had no zero.

    3. What did Brahmagupta do in 628 CE that made him famous?

    In the Brahmasphutasiddhanta he wrote the first known rules for zero as a number, along with rules for negative numbers ("debts") and positive numbers ("fortunes").

    4. Why do historians say 'Hindu-Arabic numerals' instead of 'Arabic numerals'?

    The digits and place value began in South Asia, and scholars writing in Arabic developed and spread them to Europe. The double name credits both parts of the journey.

    5. What changed about the Bakhshali manuscript's date between 2017 and 2024?

    In 2017, radiocarbon tests suggested some leaves were from the 3rd or 4th century CE. After other historians questioned this, a 2024 Oxford report said the early result was inaccurate and dated all five tested leaves to roughly the late 700s to early 1000s CE.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain in your own words the difference between zero as a placeholder and zero as a number.

      Answer

      As a placeholder, zero marks an empty position, like the tens place in 507. As a number, zero is a quantity you can add, subtract and multiply, the answer to 5 minus 5.

    2. Explain why the work of the Kerala School is described as a "rediscovery" in Europe rather than a source for European calculus.

      Answer

      Gregory, Leibniz and Newton found the same series in the late 1600s, but there is no solid evidence that Madhava's results reached them. So historians say Kerala came first, but the Europeans found the series independently.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Brahmi numerals, from the 3rd century BCE, are the ancestors of the digits 0 to 9, but they were not yet a place value system.
    • A place value system gives each digit a value by its position. Zero holds empty places, and South Asian mathematicians were the first known to treat zero as a number, with Brahmagupta writing its rules in 628 CE.
    • The digits traveled from South Asia through Baghdad and North Africa to Europe, which is why they're called Hindu-Arabic numerals; al-Khwarizmi's name gave us "algorithm."
    • Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, the Kerala School and Panini made real, specific contributions. Describing them accurately, including their mistakes, honors them more than exaggerating.
    • Ayurveda, the Sushruta Samhita, Unani medicine and yoga are long healing traditions with real history; they are traditions, not proven cures, and this is not medical advice.
    • Chaturanga is the ancestor of chess, and wootz steel from South India and Sri Lanka was prized across the world.
    • Modern scientists from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, including Ramanujan, Raman, Bose and Salam, continued the story into the 20th century.
