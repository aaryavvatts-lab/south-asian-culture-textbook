    Chapter 17: India¶

    Summary¶

    India is the most populous country on Earth and the largest country in South Asia, home to about 1.46 billion people in 2025. This chapter explains how such a big and varied country holds together: its constitution, its parliament and elections, its capital, and its 28 states and 8 union territories, many of them drawn around languages. You'll visit South India and Northeast India, meet India's Adivasi communities, and see how the idea of unity in diversity shows up in its national symbols, its money and its space program. Along the way you'll learn why one visitor from Hyderabad calls India "28 countries in one," and why that is both true and not quite true.

    One country, many homes

    Hi, I'm Mora! India is where my own feathers come from: the peacock is India's national bird. If your family is from India, you've probably noticed that "Indian" can mean very different things to a cousin in Chennai and an uncle in Lucknow. If it isn't, India still touches your world in more ways than you might expect, even the phone in your pocket. This chapter walks you across the whole map, from Ladakh to Kerala.

    17.1 "Like 28 Countries in One"¶

    It's the first Saturday of the school year, and Arjun Reddy's cousin Kavya is visiting Minnesota from Hyderabad, in India. Arjun brings her to the South Asian Student Association's welcome picnic, where Zara Hossain is handing out samosas and Ben Lindqvist is handing out napkins (and eating most of the samosas).

    Ben asks Kavya what India is like. She laughs. "Which part? India is like 28 countries in one. At home we speak Telugu. If I take the train to Bengaluru, the signs are in Kannada. My friend from Kolkata speaks Bengali, and my roommate from Delhi speaks Hindi. We all have to use English or Hindi to talk to each other sometimes." Zara jumps in: "Bengali? That's what my family speaks, and we're from Bangladesh." Kavya nods. "Exactly. Some of India's languages are also other countries' languages."

    Ben is confused. He always thought India was one country with one language and one kind of food. Now he's hearing that it has dozens of languages, dozens of governments and a cousin who needs English to talk to her own roommate. So what holds India together, and what does Kavya mean by "28 countries in one"?

    17.2 A Country the Size of a Continent¶

    One way to picture it is the European Union, which has 27 member countries, more than 20 official languages, and about 450 million people. Each country has its own government, but they share some laws and many share a currency. Now imagine something with about three times as many people, more than 20 major languages, and 36 states and territories, but with one national government, one constitution, one army, one currency and one passport. That's India.

    Or think about the United States, where Minnesota and Texas have their own governors and capitals inside one country. Now imagine if Minnesota's official language were Swedish and Texas's were Spanish. That's closer to India, where many states were drawn around languages.

    The Republic of India¶

    The Republic of India is the official name of the country: a sovereign, democratic republic and a union of states, with an elected parliament and a constitution that has been in force since 1950. The constitution begins its very first article by naming the country twice: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." Bharat (भारत, BHAH-rut) is the Hindi name for India, borrowed from Sanskrit, and many Indians use it in everyday speech.

    India became independent from British rule on 15 August 1947, as you saw in Chapter 4. It became a republic, with an elected president instead of the British king as head of state, on 26 January 1950.

    How many people?¶

    India had 1,463,865,525 people in 2025 (about 1.46 billion, World Bank). It became the world's most populous country in 2023, passing China. About one in every six people on Earth lives in India.

    Most Indians live in villages and small towns. In 2025, about 36% of Indians lived in cities, according to the World Bank. That still means hundreds of millions of city dwellers, in huge cities like Delhi, Mumbai (formerly Bombay), Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru and Hyderabad.

    India's last full census was taken in 2011. The next one is under way, with houses counted in 2026 and people counted in early 2027. Until it is finished, many of India's detailed numbers about religion and language come from 2011.

    17.3 The Constitution of India¶

    A rulebook for a billion people¶

    The Constitution of India is the supreme law of the country: the document that sets up India's government, divides power between the center and the states, and lists the rights of every citizen. It was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into effect on 26 January 1950, a day Indians celebrate every year as Republic Day. It is famous for being one of the longest written national constitutions in the world.

    The constitution was drafted by a committee chaired by B. R. Ambedkar, a lawyer and scholar who was born into a Dalit family and fought against caste discrimination all his life. Among many other things, the constitution:

    • makes all citizens equal before the law;
    • abolishes "untouchability" (Article 17), so discrimination by caste is illegal;
    • protects freedom of religion, speech and assembly;
    • names Hindi in the Devanagari script as the official language of the Union, with English continuing for official use (Article 343);
    • lists the Eighth Schedule of languages, which today has 22 languages (Chapter 6).

    India is officially secular, which in India means the state has no official religion and treats all faiths equally. In the 2011 census, about 79.8% of Indians were Hindu, 14.2% Muslim, 2.3% Christian, 1.7% Sikh, 0.7% Buddhist and 0.4% Jain, with smaller Parsi, Jewish, Bahá'í and tribal-faith communities. India has one of the largest Muslim populations of any country in the world.

    Federal, but with a strong center¶

    India is a federal country: power is shared between the national government (often called "the center" or "the Union") and the state governments. The constitution lists which subjects belong to whom. Defense, foreign affairs and money belong to the center. Police, public health and farming mostly belong to the states. Education is shared. Under Article 345, each state can choose its own official language or languages. That rule is the key to the states you'll meet in Section 17.6.

    The Indian system gives the center more power than the American one does. For example, Parliament can create new states, change state borders or rename states by an ordinary law.

    Think about it

    The constitution let each state pick its own official language. Why might that help a big, many-language country stay united, instead of pulling it apart? Keep your answer in mind for Section 17.7.

    17.4 Parliament and Elections¶

    The Parliament of India¶

    The Parliament of India is the national legislature, the body that makes India's laws. It has two houses, plus the president:

    House Hindi name Members How they are chosen
    House of the People (lower house) Lok Sabha (लोकसभा, LOKE-sub-ha) 543 elected seats Directly elected by voters, one member per constituency, at least every five years
    Council of States (upper house) Rajya Sabha 245 today, up to 250 allowed (12 appointed by the president) Mostly elected by the members of state assemblies

    India uses a parliamentary system, like the United Kingdom and unlike the United States. The prime minister, who runs the government day to day, is the leader of the party or coalition that controls a majority in the Lok Sabha. The president is head of state and has mostly ceremonial powers, acting on the advice of the prime minister and the cabinet.

    As of October 2026, India's prime minister is Narendra Modi, in office since 2014 and in his third term, and its president is Droupadi Murmu, in office since 2022. President Murmu, from the Santal community, is the first person from a tribal community to hold the office. Parliament meets in New Delhi, in a new Parliament building opened in May 2023.

    Elections in India¶

    Elections in India are the regular votes, run by the independent Election Commission of India, in which adult citizens choose members of Parliament and of state assemblies. Every citizen aged 18 or older can vote.

    The 2024 general election shows the size of it. Voting took place in seven phases between 19 April and 1 June 2024, and results were announced on 4 June. About 968 million people were registered to vote, and about 642 million voted, the largest number of voters in any election in history. Turnout at polling stations was 65.79%, according to the Election Commission. The highest turnout was in Lakshadweep, at 84.16%, and the lowest in Bihar, at 56.19%.

    The Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) won 240 seats, short of a majority on its own, and formed a coalition government, the National Democratic Alliance, with 293 seats. The opposition alliance, called INDIA and led by the Indian National Congress, won 234 seats. Like any democracy's, India's politics is lively and argued over; this book describes how the system works and leaves the arguments to the news.

    Each state also elects its own legislative assembly, which chooses a chief minister, the state's head of government.

    17.5 New Delhi¶

    New Delhi is the capital of India and the seat of its national government, home to Parliament, the President's House (Rashtrapati Bhavan) and the Supreme Court. It sits inside the larger city and union territory of Delhi, on the Yamuna River.

    New Delhi is younger than it looks. Under British rule, Kolkata (then Calcutta) was the capital until 1911. That year, King George V announced that the capital would move to Delhi, an old imperial city that had been a Mughal capital (Chapter 3). British architects Edwin Lutyens and Herbert Baker designed a new government district south of the old city, and New Delhi was inaugurated in 1931. After independence, the new republic kept it as the capital.

    Delhi is a city of layers: Mughal monuments in Old Delhi, the colonial avenues of New Delhi, and huge modern suburbs where people from every state now live.

    Delhi or New Delhi?

    "Delhi" is the whole city and union territory; "New Delhi" is the district inside it where the national government sits. If someone says India's capital is Mumbai, gently point out that Mumbai is the biggest business city, but the capital is New Delhi.

    17.6 States and Union Territories¶

    Two kinds of pieces¶

    India is divided into States and Union Territories: 28 states, which have their own elected governments and share power with the center, and 8 union territories, which are governed more directly by the national government. This is the count as of October 2026, according to the National Portal of India.

    • A state has its own elected legislative assembly, a chief minister and a governor appointed by the president. It makes its own laws on state subjects.
    • A union territory (UT) is run by an administrator appointed by the president. Some UTs, like Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir, also have elected assemblies with limited powers.

    Why have union territories at all? Some are small islands far from the mainland (the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep). Some were once ruled by France or Portugal and stayed separate after they joined India (Puducherry; Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu). Chandigarh is the shared capital of two states. Delhi holds the national capital. And Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh became union territories in 2019, as you'll see below.

    Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh¶

    Until 2019, Jammu and Kashmir was a state with special autonomy under Article 370 of the constitution. In August 2019, India's government revoked that special status, and Parliament passed a law splitting the state into two union territories, Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, from 31 October 2019. In December 2023, India's Supreme Court upheld the change and directed that statehood be restored to Jammu and Kashmir "as soon as possible." As of October 2026, both remain union territories.

    These two territories are part of a much larger region whose status is disputed, as the box below explains.

    Whose Story?

    Ask someone in Delhi, Islamabad and Beijing to draw "Kashmir," and you'll get three different maps. In 1947 the Hindu ruler of the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, with a Muslim-majority population, joined India during fighting with forces from Pakistan, and war followed. Today India administers about 55% of the region's land, Pakistan about 30% (Azad Jammu and Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan) and China about 15% (including Aksai Chin). India's position is that the whole former princely state is part of India. China's position is that Aksai Chin is part of China. Pakistan's position is that the region's future should be decided by its people through the vote the United Nations called for in 1948. Many Kashmiris themselves hold their own views, which do not always match either government's, and people of every community there have suffered through decades of conflict.

    17.7 Linguistic States¶

    How languages drew the map¶

    Linguistic States are states whose borders were drawn so that most people inside share one main language, which usually becomes the state's official language. Most of India's states today are linguistic states.

    At independence, India inherited a patchwork: big British provinces like Madras and Bombay that held speakers of several languages, plus more than 500 princely states. Many people wanted states that matched their languages, so that they could go to school, go to court and deal with officials in their own tongue. India's first leaders worried that language-based states might divide the country, and moved slowly.

    In 1952, Potti Sriramulu, an activist demanding a separate state for Telugu speakers, died on the 58th day of a hunger strike. Protests followed, and in 1953 the government created Andhra State from the Telugu-speaking north of Madras State, the first state formed for one language. A States Reorganisation Commission followed, and the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew much of the map on linguistic lines from 1 November 1956. Kerala, for example, was created that day for Malayalam speakers.

    The process continued:

    Year Change
    1953 Andhra State formed for Telugu speakers
    1956 States Reorganisation Act redraws the map; Kerala, Mysore (later Karnataka) and others formed around languages
    1960 Bombay State split into Maharashtra (Marathi) and Gujarat (Gujarati) on 1 May
    2000 Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand formed, more for regional and tribal identity than for language
    2014 Telangana formed from Andhra Pradesh on 2 June, after a long regional movement; both states speak Telugu
    2019 Jammu and Kashmir state split into two union territories

    Notice the later changes. Telangana and Andhra Pradesh share the Telugu language, yet they split, because people in Telangana felt their region's history and needs were different. Language matters a great deal, but it isn't the only thing that makes people feel like a "state."

    So was Kavya right that India is "28 countries in one"? In one sense, yes: many states have their own language, literature, film industry, food and festivals. But they are not countries. They share one constitution, one parliament and one army, and people move freely between them. Kavya can fly from Hyderabad to Kolkata without a passport.

    Hindi, English and the language question¶

    India's constitution named Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union and planned for English to be phased out by 1965. In South India, especially in Tamil Nadu, many people strongly opposed having Hindi imposed on them. After protests in Tamil Nadu, the Official Languages Act of 1963, and an amendment to it in 1967, settled the issue for now: English continues alongside Hindi for official purposes, with no end date.

    How many Indians speak Hindi? In the 2011 census, about 43.6% of Indians named Hindi as their mother tongue. That number includes many related regional tongues, such as Bhojpuri and Braj, that the census groups under Hindi. So Hindi is the biggest single language, but more than half of India grows up speaking something else. Bengali came second, at about 8%.

    17.8 South India¶

    South India is the southern part of the peninsula, usually meaning the five states of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Telangana, plus Puducherry and Lakshadweep, where the main languages belong to the Dravidian family.

    The four big Dravidian languages are Telugu, Tamil, Kannada and Malayalam, each with its own script, literature and film industry (Chapter 6, Chapter 15). Dravidian languages are a completely different family from Hindi, which is Indo-Aryan, in the same way that Finnish is unrelated to Swedish even though Finland and Sweden are neighbors. That is why a Hindi-speaking student who moves to Chennai has to learn a whole new language.

    South India has long faced the sea. Its ports traded with Rome, Arabia and Southeast Asia for centuries (Chapter 3). Kerala has old Christian, Jewish and Muslim communities whose faiths first reached its coast by sea, through trade. The south is also home to big technology cities, Bengaluru and Hyderabad, where many software companies have offices. Arjun's family is from Hyderabad, the capital of Telangana, and he dances Kuchipudi, a classical dance style named after a village in Andhra Pradesh (Chapter 10).

    "South Indian" is a useful label, but it has limits: a family from Kochi and a family from Hyderabad may speak, eat, pray and celebrate quite differently.

    17.9 Northeast India¶

    Northeast India is the easternmost region of the country, made up of eight states: Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland and Tripura (often called the "Seven Sisters") and Sikkim. It is connected to the rest of India by a narrow strip of land in West Bengal, the Siliguri Corridor, and it borders five countries: China, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Bhutan and Nepal.

    The Northeast covers almost 8% of India's area but held only about 4% of its people in 2011. It is one of the most linguistically varied places on Earth. Many of its languages belong to the Tibeto-Burman family, related to Tibetan and Burmese rather than to Hindi or Tamil. Assamese and Bengali are Indo-Aryan, and Khasi in Meghalaya is Austroasiatic. Because no single local language is shared across Nagaland or Arunachal Pradesh, those states chose English as their official language.

    Faiths vary widely too. Assam and Tripura have Hindu majorities and Assam has a large Muslim population. Nagaland, Mizoram and Meghalaya have Christian majorities. Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh have important Buddhist communities. Many communities keep Indigenous faiths alongside or instead of these.

    Many people from the Northeast who move to big Indian cities have reported facing prejudice and being treated as "foreigners" in their own country. This chapter treats the Northeast as a full part of India's story.

    Not all Indians look alike

    If a classmate says they're from India and they don't match the picture you had in your head, that picture is too small. India includes people from Ladakh, Nagaland, Kerala and Gujarat, and none of them is "less Indian" than the others.

    17.10 Adivasi Communities¶

    Adivasi Communities are India's Indigenous or tribal peoples, many of whom have their own languages, faiths, governance and ways of life tied to forests, hills and land. Adivasi (आदिवासी, AH-dee-VAH-see) means "original inhabitants" in Hindi. The government's official term is Scheduled Tribes, groups listed in a schedule under the constitution for special protection and support. Some officials avoid the word Adivasi, but many communities and activists use it with pride, and this book uses both respectfully.

    In the 2011 census, about 104 million Indians, 8.6% of the population, belonged to Scheduled Tribes, and hundreds of different groups are listed. That is more than the entire population of most countries. Madhya Pradesh has the largest tribal population of any state (about 15 million in 2011), and in several northeastern states tribal peoples are the majority. Most live in rural areas.

    Adivasi communities include the Santal, Gond, Bhil, Munda, Oraon and many more, with very different histories. Santali, an Austroasiatic language, was added to the Eighth Schedule in 2003. The state of Jharkhand was created in 2000 partly in response to a long movement for tribal self-rule; its first chief minister took office on 15 November 2000, the birth anniversary of the Munda leader Birsa Munda, who led a movement against British rule and the loss of tribal land in the 1890s.

    Adivasi communities have faced serious problems, especially losing land and forests to dams, mines and other projects. They have also shaped Indian art, music and politics, and today an Adivasi woman, Droupadi Murmu, is president of India.

    17.11 Unity in Diversity¶

    Unity in Diversity is the idea that a country can hold many languages, faiths, peoples and cultures and still be one nation, with the differences seen as a strength rather than a threat. Indian leaders, schools and textbooks have used the phrase since independence to describe what India hopes to be.

    You've already seen the diversity: 22 scheduled languages and hundreds more spoken; Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and others; deserts, rainforests, Himalayan valleys and coral islands. What provides the unity?

    • Shared institutions: one constitution, one parliament, one Supreme Court and one election system.
    • Shared history: the independence movement, which brought people from every region together against colonial rule.
    • Shared culture across lines: cricket (Chapter 15), Hindi and regional films, festivals that neighbors of different faiths often celebrate together, and food that travels between states.

    Unity in diversity is a goal that India keeps working toward. India has seen violence between religious communities, conflicts in Kashmir and parts of the Northeast, and arguments over language. Indians debate, loudly, how a big democracy should balance majority and minority, center and state. Those debates are part of how a diverse country works out living together.

    17.12 National Symbols of India¶

    National Symbols of India are the official emblems, chosen by the government, that stand for the country as a whole: a flag, an emblem, an anthem, a song, an animal, a bird, a flower and more.

    Symbol What it is Adopted or notes
    National flag The tricolor, or tiranga (तिरंगा, ti-RUNG-ah): saffron, white and green bands with a navy blue wheel (the Ashoka Chakra) in the center Adopted 22 July 1947
    State emblem Three lions from the Lion Capital of Ashoka at Sarnath, with the motto Satyameva Jayate, "Truth alone triumphs" Adopted 26 January 1950
    National anthem "Jana Gana Mana," written in Bengali by Rabindranath Tagore Adopted 24 January 1950
    National song "Vande Mataram," by Bankim Chandra Chatterjee Honored equally with the anthem
    National animal Tiger
    National bird Indian peafowl (peacock) Since 1963
    National flower Lotus Listed on the National Portal of India, though a 2019 government answer in Parliament said no official notice names a national flower
    National calendar The Saka calendar, used alongside the Gregorian calendar Adopted 22 March 1957

    Many of these symbols reach back into older history. The wheel on the flag and the lions on the emblem come from the reign of the emperor Ashoka in the 200s BCE (Chapter 2). The anthem connects India to its neighbors: Tagore also wrote "Amar Shonar Bangla," the national anthem of Bangladesh, which you'll meet in Chapter 19.

    17.13 The Indian Economy and the Rupee¶

    The Indian Economy¶

    The Indian Economy is the system of farms, factories, services and trade through which India's 1.46 billion people make and buy goods. It is huge in total but still modest per person. In 2025, India's gross domestic product (GDP), the value of everything produced in a year, was about 3.96 trillion US dollars, the sixth-largest in the world by the World Bank's figures in US dollars, close behind the United Kingdom. But because that output is shared among so many people, India's income per person (gross national income per person) was about 2,760 US dollars in 2025, according to the World Bank, far below that of the United States.

    India's economy has many layers at once:

    • Farming still employs a very large share of Indians, growing rice, wheat, cotton, sugarcane, tea and spices.
    • Services are the biggest part of the economy, including software and IT companies in Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune, banking in Mumbai, and call centers that serve customers worldwide.
    • Manufacturing includes cars, medicines and, increasingly, phones.
    • The informal economy: millions of street vendors, small shops and family workshops that are not registered businesses.

    Since economic reforms in 1991 opened India more to trade and investment, its economy has grown fast, and hundreds of millions of people have risen out of extreme poverty. At the same time, India still has many people living in poverty, and the gaps between rich and poor, and between booming and struggling states, are large.

    The Indian Rupee¶

    The Indian Rupee is India's currency, written ₹ and divided into 100 paise. The word "rupee" comes from the Hindi rupaya (रुपया, ROOP-yah), from a Sanskrit word for wrought silver. The rupee goes back to a silver coin called the rupiya, first issued by the ruler Sher Shah Suri between 1540 and 1545 and later by the Mughals, and several neighbors also call their money rupees: Pakistan, Nepal and Sri Lanka all do.

    The ₹ symbol is new. In 2010, India's government held an open competition for a rupee sign, and on 15 July 2010 it chose a design by D. Udaya Kumar, a young designer from IIT Bombay. The symbol blends the Devanagari letter र (ra) with the Latin letter R, and its two horizontal lines echo the flag. In 2026, one US dollar cost more than 90 rupees (US Federal Reserve exchange-rate data, February 2026).

    17.14 ISRO and India in Space¶

    ISRO, the Indian Space Research Organisation, is India's national space agency, which builds and launches rockets and satellites and sends missions to the Moon and beyond. It grew out of a space research committee set up in 1962, at the urging of the physicist Vikram Sarabhai, and took the name ISRO in 1969. Its headquarters are in Bengaluru, and it launches rockets from Sriharikota, an island on the coast of Andhra Pradesh.

    ISRO is known for doing ambitious things on small budgets: its Mars Orbiter Mission, which reached Mars in September 2014, cost about 450 crore rupees (roughly 74 million US dollars). Its satellites forecast weather, map crops, guide fishing boats and broadcast TV across the country.

    Its most famous recent mission is Chandrayaan-3 ("Moon craft"). Launched on 14 July 2023, it landed its Vikram lander near the Moon's south pole on 23 August 2023, and a small rover called Pragyan drove across the surface. India became the fourth country to land softly on the Moon, after the Soviet Union, the United States and China, and the first to land near the lunar south pole, a region scientists study because it may hold water ice. The rover's instruments confirmed sulfur in the soil near the pole.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: India has one culture.
    Reality: India has 28 states and 8 union territories, 22 scheduled languages and hundreds more, and many faiths, cuisines and festivals. A family in Kerala and a family in Punjab can differ in language, food and customs as much as Swedes and Spaniards do, while sharing one country, one constitution and a lot of history.

    Myth: Everyone in India speaks Hindi.
    Reality: Hindi is the largest language, but in the 2011 census only about 44% of Indians named it as their mother tongue. Most people in South India, Northeast India, West Bengal, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Punjab and Odisha grow up speaking another language, and English is widely used to bridge the gaps.

    Myth: India's capital is Mumbai.
    Reality: The capital is New Delhi, inside the National Capital Territory of Delhi. Mumbai, the capital of Maharashtra, is India's biggest business and film city.

    Myth: India is mostly big cities.
    Reality: India has some of the world's largest cities, but in 2025 only about 36% of Indians lived in urban areas, according to the World Bank. Most Indians still live in villages and small towns.

    Check Yourself¶

    1. How many states and union territories does India have, and what is the difference between them?

    As of 2026, India has 28 states and 8 union territories. States have their own elected governments that share power with the center; union territories are governed more directly by the national government through an administrator, though some also have elected assemblies.

    2. What is a linguistic state, and which was the first?

    A linguistic state is one whose borders were drawn around a shared language, which usually becomes its official language. Andhra State, formed in 1953 for Telugu speakers, was the first.

    3. What are the two houses of the Parliament of India?

    The Lok Sabha (House of the People), with 543 directly elected seats, and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States), mostly elected by state assemblies.

    4. What happened on 23 August 2023, and why did it matter?

    ISRO's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole. India became the fourth country to make a soft landing on the Moon and the first to land near the lunar south pole.

    5. What are Adivasi communities, and what share of India's population did they make up in 2011?

    Adivasi communities are India's Indigenous or tribal peoples, officially called Scheduled Tribes. In the 2011 census they made up about 8.6% of the population, about 104 million people.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh use English as their official language.

      Answer

      Each is home to many peoples speaking many different languages, mostly Tibeto-Burman, with no single local language shared by everyone, so English works as a neutral common language for government.

    2. Explain the difference between Delhi and New Delhi.

      Answer

      Delhi is the whole city and the National Capital Territory, a union territory. New Delhi is the district inside it, built by the British and inaugurated in 1931, that holds Parliament and the national government.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • The Republic of India, with 1,463,865,525 people in 2025 (World Bank), is the world's most populous country; its constitution came into effect on 26 January 1950.
    • India is a federal parliamentary democracy: the Lok Sabha's 543 seats are directly elected, and in 2024 about 642 million people voted in the largest election in history.
    • India has 28 states and 8 union territories (2026), and its capital is New Delhi, inside the National Capital Territory of Delhi.
    • Many states are linguistic states, drawn around a shared language, beginning with Andhra State in 1953 and the States Reorganisation Act of 1956.
    • South India's main languages are Dravidian, Northeast India's are largely Tibeto-Burman, and Hindi was the mother tongue of about 44% of Indians in 2011.
    • Adivasi communities (Scheduled Tribes) made up 8.6% of Indians in 2011, and India's national symbols, economy, rupee and space program all reflect the goal of unity in diversity.
    • Kashmir is disputed: India, Pakistan and China each administer part of the region, and their positions differ.
