    Chapter 19: Bangladesh and Sri Lanka¶

    Summary¶

    Bangladesh and Sri Lanka sit on opposite sides of the Bay of Bengal, and the land in each could hardly be more different. One is a huge, flat river delta, the other a mountainous island. This chapter tours Bangladesh first: its capital, the rivers and floods that shape daily life, the Sundarbans mangrove forest, the language movement that gave the world International Mother Language Day, and its garment and microfinance industries. Then it crosses to Sri Lanka: its capital cities, the ancient city of Anuradhapura, the Sinhala and Tamil peoples, the long civil war, Ceylon tea and the sacred mountain of Sri Pada. For both countries you'll also get a short, dated look at who is in charge as of October 2026.

    Across the Bay of Bengal

    Hi, I'm Mora! The shirt you're wearing may well have been sewn in Bangladesh, and the tea in your cupboard may say "Ceylon" on the box. These two countries touch your daily life more than you might think. If your family comes from either one, this chapter gives you words for the rivers, mountains and stories you hear about at home.

    19.1 Two New Years in One Week¶

    It's April, and Zara Hossain is excited. At home, her family is getting ready for Pohela Boishakh, the Bengali New Year, with new clothes, sweets and her Nanu's stories about the big parade in Dhaka. At the South Asian Student Association meeting, she brings a plate of sweets and announces, "Shubho Noboborsho! Happy New Year!"

    Dilan Perera, whose family is from Kandy in Sri Lanka, looks up from his phone. "Wait, it's our New Year this week too." His family celebrates the Sinhala and Tamil New Year, with oil lamps, a special milk rice and games in the yard at his grandparents' house. "My grandmother says the exact time for lighting the hearth is set by astrologers, down to the minute."

    Ben Lindqvist is lost. "Hang on. You're from Bangladesh, and you're from Sri Lanka. Those aren't even next to each other. How do you both have New Year in the same week of April? And why isn't it in January?" Zara and Dilan look at each other. Neither has ever thought about it. And Ben's next question is even bigger: "What are your countries actually like?"

    19.2 A Delta and an Island¶

    Start with a river you know. The Mississippi begins at Lake Itasca in northern Minnesota and flows about 3,766 km (2,340 miles) south to a delta in the Gulf of Mexico. A delta is the flat, fan-shaped land a river builds where it meets the sea, as it drops the mud and sand it has carried for hundreds of miles. Delta land is low, fertile and often flooded. New Orleans sits near the Mississippi's delta.

    Now imagine a country that is almost all delta, where three great rivers come together, two of them carrying about as much water as the Mississippi or more, and where tens of millions of people live on land only a few meters above the sea. That is Bangladesh.

    Then imagine an island with mountains in the middle, beaches all around, and a history of kingdoms going back more than 2,000 years. That is Sri Lanka.

    The two countries face each other across the Bay of Bengal, the big bay of the Indian Ocean east of India. The monsoon shapes both of them (Chapter 1). But one lives with rivers, and the other with the sea and the mountains. As you read, keep asking how the land shapes the way people live.

    As for Ben's question about April: both new years follow old South Asian calendars that start the year in spring. In Sri Lanka, the new year marks the moment the sun moves into the sign of Aries, usually on 13 or 14 April. In Bangladesh, Pohela Boishakh (পহেলা বৈশাখ, PO-heh-la BOI-shakh), "the first of Boishakh," falls on 14 April. Neighbors in India, Nepal and Southeast Asia celebrate new years in the same season, as you saw in Chapter 7.

    19.3 The People's Republic of Bangladesh¶

    A country born from a language and a war¶

    The People's Republic of Bangladesh is the official name of Bangladesh: a parliamentary republic in the eastern part of the old region of Bengal, independent since 1971, with Bangla (Bengali) as its state language. The name means "country of the Bengalis." Bangla (বাংলা, BANG-lah) is both the name of the language and an old name for the region.

    Bangladesh's story runs through Chapter 4. In 1947, when British India was divided, the Muslim-majority eastern half of Bengal became East Pakistan, part of a Pakistan whose two wings were separated by the whole width of northern India. Many Bengalis felt the government in West Pakistan treated them unfairly, starting with language, as you'll see in Section 19.6. After years of tension and a brutal war, Bangladesh declared independence on 26 March 1971, and the Liberation War ended on 16 December 1971. Its constitution was adopted on 4 November 1972.

    Bangladesh had 175,686,899 people in 2025 (about 176 million, World Bank), making it one of the ten most populous countries in the world. Fit that many people into an area of about 148,000 km², about the size of Iowa, and you get one of the most densely populated large countries on Earth. In Bangladesh's 2022 census, about 91% of people were Muslim and about 8% Hindu, with smaller Buddhist and Christian communities, including Indigenous peoples such as the Chakma and Marma of the Chittagong Hill Tracts.

    Bangladesh's national anthem, "Amar Shonar Bangla" ("My Golden Bengal"), was written by Rabindranath Tagore, the same poet who wrote India's anthem (Chapter 17). Tagore wrote the song in 1905, more than 40 years before either country existed.

    Dhaka¶

    Dhaka (ঢাকা, DHAH-kah) is the capital and largest city of Bangladesh, on the Buriganga River in the middle of the country. It is one of the most crowded cities in the world, full of rickshaws, buses, markets, mosques and universities.

    Dhaka has been a capital before. In the 1600s it was the capital of the Mughal province of Bengal and was named Jahangirnagar, "city of Jahangir," after the emperor. Under the Mughals it became a center of trade and of fine cotton cloth called muslin (Chapter 12). Today the city's most famous modern landmark is the National Parliament building, the Jatiya Sangsad Bhaban, designed by the American architect Louis Kahn.

    Zara's family is from Chattogram (formerly Chittagong), Bangladesh's main seaport on the Bay of Bengal, in the southeast. When her Nanu talks about "going to Dhaka," she means a long trip to the big, busy capital.

    19.4 The Bengal Delta, Rivers and Floods¶

    The world's largest delta¶

    The Bengal Delta (also called the Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta) is the vast, low plain built by the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna rivers where they flow into the Bay of Bengal. It is the largest river delta in the world, and most of Bangladesh, along with the Indian state of West Bengal, sits on it.

    Three great rivers meet here, and in Bangladesh they go by local names:

    River Name in Bangladesh Where it comes from
    Ganges Padma From the Himalaya, across northern India
    Brahmaputra Jamuna From Tibet, through Northeast India
    Meghna Meghna From the hills of Northeast India and eastern Bangladesh

    The Padma and the Jamuna join, then meet the Meghna, and the combined water pours into the sea. These rivers carry huge amounts of mud from the Himalaya, and over thousands of years that mud built the land. About 83% of Bangladesh is floodplain, land that rivers flood and rebuild.

    Rivers and Floods¶

    Rivers and Floods in Bangladesh means the yearly rhythm in which monsoon rain and Himalayan meltwater raise the rivers, which spread across the low delta, bringing both fertile soil and danger. Life in Bangladesh moves with this rhythm.

    Most of the water in Bangladesh's rivers comes from rain that falls outside the country, upstream in India, Nepal, Bhutan and China. It flows in mostly through the Ganges, the Brahmaputra and the Meghna. So when heavy monsoon rain falls on India, Nepal, Bhutan and Tibet, Bangladesh feels it.

    • In an average year, about 21% of Bangladesh floods (about 31,000 km²).
    • In severe years, much more. In 1998, more than two-thirds of the country was under water, including half of Dhaka.

    Floods are not only disasters. A normal flood soaks the fields and leaves behind fresh silt, the fine mud that makes delta soil so fertile for rice, jute and fish. Many farmers depend on it. The trouble comes when floods are too high, too long, or arrive at the wrong time.

    The coast faces another danger: cyclones, the Indian Ocean's hurricanes. The Bhola cyclone of November 1970 killed at least 300,000 people and possibly as many as 500,000, the deadliest tropical cyclone on record. Since then, Bangladesh has built cyclone shelters along the coast and trained tens of thousands of volunteers to warn villages, and cyclone deaths have fallen sharply.

    Think about it

    Most of Bangladesh's river water comes from other countries. What kinds of agreements might Bangladesh need with its neighbors, and why would sharing rivers be tricky?

    19.5 The Sundarbans¶

    The Sundarbans (সুন্দরবন, SHOON-dor-bon) is the world's largest mangrove forest, on the coast of the Bengal Delta, shared by Bangladesh and India. Its name means "beautiful forest" in Bangla, and may refer to the sundari trees that grow there.

    A mangrove is a tree that grows in salty coastal water, standing on tangled roots, some of which poke up out of the mud to breathe. The Sundarbans covers about 10,000 km², and about 60% of it lies in Bangladesh. It is a maze of tidal rivers, mudflats and small islands that flood and drain twice a day with the tides.

    The forest is famous for the Bengal tiger, which here swims between islands, along with about 260 bird species and estuarine crocodiles. UNESCO listed India's part of the Sundarbans as a World Heritage site in 1987 and Bangladesh's part in 1997. The mangroves also protect the coast, softening storm surges from cyclones before they reach villages.

    People live around the forest's edges and enter it to fish, collect honey and cut wood, which can be dangerous because of the tigers. Communities living near the Sundarbans have long told stories of Bonbibi, a forest guardian honored by both Muslim and Hindu families, which you can explore with your elders.

    19.6 Ekushey February: Dying for a Language¶

    Ekushey February (একুশে ফেব্রুয়ারি, ay-KOO-sheh FEB-roo-ah-ree), "the twenty-first of February," is the day in 1952 when students in Dhaka were killed while protesting for Bangla to be a state language of Pakistan, now remembered in Bangladesh as Language Martyrs' Day and around the world as International Mother Language Day.

    After 1947, Pakistan's leaders wanted Urdu to be the only national language. But most people in East Pakistan spoke Bangla, and in fact Bangla speakers were the majority of all Pakistanis. Students at the University of Dhaka and others organized protests. On 21 February 1952 they defied a ban on gatherings, and police opened fire. Several protesters were killed.

    The killings strengthened the movement. In 1956, Pakistan made Bengali one of its official languages. The language movement also helped build the Bengali national identity that led to independence in 1971.

    Today, every 21 February, people in Bangladesh walk barefoot at dawn to the Shaheed Minar (Martyrs' Monument) near Dhaka Medical College to lay flowers. In 1999, UNESCO declared 21 February International Mother Language Day, so the whole world now marks a date that began in Dhaka.

    Zara thinks about this a lot. She understands Bangla but usually answers her Nanu in English. Knowing that students died for the right to speak it makes her want to try harder.

    19.7 Making a Living: Garments and Microfinance¶

    The Garment Industry¶

    Bangladesh's Garment Industry is the large, mostly export-oriented industry of factories that sew clothing for brands around the world, the country's biggest source of export earnings. Look at the tags on your T-shirts and jeans: "Made in Bangladesh" is very common.

    • In the 2024-25 fiscal year, Bangladesh's ready-made garment exports were worth about 39 billion US dollars.
    • Garments made up more than four-fifths of the country's export earnings in that period.
    • Millions of people work in the factories, and many of them are women.

    The industry has brought jobs and growth, but it has also brought hard questions about pay and safety. On 24 April 2013, the Rana Plaza building near Dhaka, which held several garment factories, collapsed. The final death toll was 1,134 people, most of them garment workers. The disaster led to new building-safety agreements between brands, unions and factory owners. Many people around the world began asking who made their clothes and under what conditions.

    Microfinance¶

    Microfinance is the practice of giving very small loans and other banking services to poor people, especially women, who have no property to offer as security and cannot borrow from ordinary banks. A small loan might let someone buy a cow, a sewing machine or stock for a small shop.

    Bangladesh made microfinance famous. In 1976, Muhammad Yunus, an economics professor at the University of Chittagong, began a research project lending tiny sums to villagers in Jobra, near the university. In 1983 it became the Grameen Bank, an independent bank. "Grameen" means "of the village." Its borrowers were mostly women, organized in small groups that supported one another.

    In 2006, Muhammad Yunus and Grameen Bank jointly won the Nobel Peace Prize for their efforts to create development "from below." Microfinance spread to dozens of countries, including the United States. Economists still debate how much it reduces poverty on its own, and some borrowers have struggled with debt.

    19.8 Bangladesh Today¶

    Bangladesh's politics have changed quickly in the last few years, so here is a short, dated summary.

    • July-August 2024: Student-led protests against a quota system for government jobs grew into a nationwide uprising against the government of Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina, who had governed since 2009. A United Nations human rights report released in February 2025 estimated that as many as 1,400 people may have been killed between 1 July and 15 August 2024, and it blamed most of the serious violations on the former government and its security forces. Hasina's supporters rejected the report as inaccurate. On 5 August 2024, Hasina resigned and left for India.
    • August 2024 to February 2026: An interim government led by Muhammad Yunus, the Nobel laureate, as chief adviser, took office on 8 August 2024. In November 2025, a Bangladeshi tribunal sentenced Hasina, in her absence, to death for crimes against humanity over the crackdown. She rejected the verdict and the tribunal. Her party, the Awami League, was barred from the next election.
    • February 2026: A general election was held on 12 February 2026, along with a referendum that approved a package of reforms known as the July Charter. The Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) and its allies won more than two-thirds of the seats. Its leader, Tarique Rahman, was sworn in as prime minister on 17 February 2026, and the Jamaat-e-Islami party and its allies became the main opposition.
    • July-August 2026: President Mohammed Shahabuddin resigned for health reasons on 24 July 2026. Parliament elected Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir of the BNP, who was sworn in as president on 21 August 2026.

    As of October 2026, Tarique Rahman is prime minister and Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir is president. Bangladeshis, at home and abroad, hold strongly different views about these events, and families may disagree at the same dinner table. This book describes what happened and leaves the arguments to the news.

    Ask, don't assume

    Politics in Bangladesh and Sri Lanka can be painful family topics. If you want to ask an elder about them, start with "What do you remember?" rather than "Who was right?"

    19.9 Sri Lanka: An Island Nation¶

    From Ceylon to Sri Lanka¶

    Sri Lanka is an island country in the Indian Ocean, just across the narrow Palk Strait from the southeast tip of India, independent since 1948 and called Ceylon until 1972. Its official name today is the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka. Europeans called the island Ceylon; the Portuguese and Dutch ruled its coasts, and the British ruled the whole island. It became independent on 4 February 1948 as the Dominion of Ceylon, and in 1972 it became a republic and took the name Sri Lanka (ශ්‍රී ලංකා in Sinhala).

    Sri Lanka had 21,756,000 people in 2025 (about 22 million, World Bank). Its own 2024 census, released in October 2025, counted 21,781,800 people.

    The island is not part of India, but the two are close neighbors. A narrow, shallow strait, the Palk Strait, separates them, and a chain of shoals and sandbanks almost links them. Sri Lanka has its own government, flag, currency (the Sri Lankan rupee) and history going back thousands of years.

    Peoples and faiths¶

    According to the 2024 census:

    Group Share of Sri Lanka's people (2024)
    Sinhalese 74.1%
    Sri Lankan Tamils 12.3%
    Sri Lankan Moors (most of the island's Muslims) 10.5%
    Indian-origin (Hill Country) Tamils 2.8%
    Burghers, Malays, Veddas and others 0.3%

    By religion, the census counted about 69.8% Buddhists, 12.6% Hindus, 10.7% Muslims and about 6.9% Christians (5.6% Roman Catholic and 1.3% other Christians). Notice that faith and language don't line up neatly: most Sinhalese are Buddhist and most Tamils are Hindu, but there are Sinhalese and Tamil Christians, and Sri Lankan Muslims mostly speak Tamil.

    The Sinhala Language¶

    The Sinhala Language (සිංහල, SING-ha-la) is the language of the Sinhalese people, the majority of Sri Lankans, an Indo-Aryan language with its own rounded script, related to the languages of North India and most closely to Dhivehi, the language of the Maldives. Sinhala is spoken by about 16 million people. Its rounded letters are written left to right, like Hindi or Bangla, and the language has a written history of more than 2,000 years, starting with inscriptions in a Prakrit close to the languages of the Buddha's time.

    Language became the center of a bitter political fight. In 1956, the Sinhala Only Act made Sinhala the sole official language of Ceylon, replacing English and leaving out Tamil, the first language of about 29% of the population at the time. For many Tamils it became a symbol of being shut out of government jobs, schools and courts. In 1987 a constitutional amendment made Tamil an official language too, with English as the "link language." Today Sinhala and Tamil are both official languages.

    Sri Lankan Tamils¶

    Sri Lankan Tamils are the Tamil-speaking people whose ancestors have lived in Sri Lanka for many centuries, mostly in the north and east of the island, with a long history centered on the Jaffna Peninsula. They are different from the Indian-origin, or Hill Country, Tamils, whose ancestors were brought from South India by the British in the 1800s and 1900s to work on coffee and tea plantations.

    The Jaffna Peninsula, in the far north, is the heartland of Sri Lankan Tamil culture, with Hindu temples, its own style of Tamil speech and food, and a literary tradition that goes back to the medieval Jaffna Kingdom. Tamils celebrate the April new year as Puthandu (புத்தாண்டு, POOT-tahn-doo), "new year," at the same time as Sinhalese families celebrate Aluth Avurudda. That is why Dilan called it the "Sinhala and Tamil New Year."

    19.10 Colombo and Anuradhapura¶

    Colombo¶

    Colombo (කොළඹ, koh-LUM-boh) is Sri Lanka's largest city and its commercial capital, a major port on the west coast. Colombo's harbor has been busy for centuries, visited by Arab, Portuguese, Dutch and British ships, and it is still a major port today.

    Sri Lanka has two capitals. In 1982, Parliament moved to a new building in Sri Jayawardenepura Kotte, a suburb right next to Colombo, which is the official legislative capital. Colombo remains the commercial capital and the seat of the president's offices and the courts. Most people, and most maps, still think of Colombo as "the capital."

    Anuradhapura¶

    Anuradhapura (uh-noo-RAH-duh-POO-ruh) is an ancient royal capital in the dry north-central plains of Sri Lanka and a holy city for Buddhists. It flourished as a political and religious capital for about 1,300 years, until it was abandoned after an invasion in 993 CE.

    According to Buddhist tradition, in the 200s BCE, the nun Sanghamitta, daughter of the Indian emperor Ashoka (Chapter 2), brought a cutting of the tree under which the Buddha reached enlightenment at Bodh Gaya. The tree that grew from it, the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi, still stands in Anuradhapura and is honored by Buddhist pilgrims as one of the oldest planted trees with a recorded history. Around it stand huge dome-shaped Buddhist monuments called stupas, monasteries, palaces and ancient water reservoirs. UNESCO listed the Sacred City of Anuradhapura as a World Heritage site in 1982.

    Later capitals moved south, to Polonnaruwa and eventually to Kandy in the central hills. Kandy was the capital of the last independent Sinhalese kingdom until the British took it in 1815, and it is home to the Temple of the Tooth Relic, one of the most important Buddhist sites in the world. Dilan's family is from Kandy, and his grandmother visits the temple on full-moon days.

    19.11 The Sri Lankan Civil War¶

    A hard chapter of history

    This section is about a war that many families lived through, on every side. Some classmates may have relatives who were killed, displaced or who fought. Read it with care, and talk about it with respect.

    The Sri Lankan Civil War was an armed conflict from 1983 to 2009 between the government of Sri Lanka and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE, also called the Tamil Tigers), an armed group fighting for an independent Tamil state, Tamil Eelam, in the north and east of the island.

    How it began¶

    The roots of the war lay in the years after independence. Many Tamils felt shut out by laws like the Sinhala Only Act and by policies they saw as favoring the Sinhalese majority. Anti-Tamil riots broke out several times, including in 1958. In 1976 the main Tamil political parties called for a separate state, and armed groups, the strongest of which became the LTTE, began attacks.

    In July 1983, the LTTE killed 13 soldiers in an ambush in the north. Anti-Tamil riots followed in Colombo and across the country, known as Black July: over about a week, mobs attacked and killed Tamil civilians and burned homes and shops. Estimates of the dead range from about 400 to 3,000, and about 150,000 people were made homeless. Many historians date the start of the civil war to these events.

    A long war¶

    The war lasted 26 years. An Indian Peace Keeping Force was sent to the north and east under a 1987 accord between India and Sri Lanka; it became caught up in fighting with the LTTE and withdrew in 1990. A ceasefire brokered by Norway was signed in February 2002, but it broke down, and full-scale war resumed. In May 2009, the Sri Lankan military defeated the LTTE, and its leader was killed.

    The suffering touched every community. The LTTE carried out suicide bombings, assassinated political leaders and attacked Sinhalese and Muslim civilians, and it used child soldiers. Government forces were accused of killing and disappearing Tamil civilians, especially in the final months, when hundreds of thousands of civilians were trapped in a small area of the northeast. Hundreds of thousands of people, mostly Tamils, were displaced, and many fled abroad, building large Sri Lankan Tamil communities in Canada, the United Kingdom and elsewhere.

    How many died?¶

    Numbers are uncertain and painful, so scholars give ranges:

    • Shortly after the war ended, the United Nations estimated that 80,000 to 100,000 people had been killed since the conflict began in the early 1980s.
    • A UN panel of experts reported in 2011 that credible sources estimated there could have been as many as 40,000 civilian deaths in the final phase of the war in 2009, many of them from shelling. The panel found credible allegations of serious violations by both the government and the LTTE.
    • The Sri Lankan government has given much lower figures for the final months and rejected the panel's findings.

    Whose Story?

    Sri Lankans tell the story of 2009 in very different ways. For many Sinhalese, and for the government, May 2009 marks the defeat of a group that had bombed buses, temples and markets for decades, and it is remembered as the end of terror and the return of peace. For many Tamils, at home and in the diaspora, the same weeks are remembered as a time of mass civilian death, and 18 May is a day of mourning. Sri Lankan Muslims remember being expelled from the north by the LTTE in October 1990 and being caught between both sides. Human rights groups and the United Nations have called for independent investigations of what happened, while successive governments have resisted international inquiries.

    Not a religious war¶

    Because most Sinhalese are Buddhist and most Tamils are Hindu, people sometimes call it a religious war. It wasn't, mainly. The conflict was about ethnicity, language, political power and land. The LTTE's goal was a Tamil homeland, not a Hindu one, and there were Christians on both sides. Religion was part of many people's identities, but it was not what the two sides were fighting over.

    19.12 Ceylon Tea and the Hill Country¶

    Ceylon Tea is the black tea grown on Sri Lanka's hill-country plantations, still sold around the world under the island's old name. It is one of Sri Lanka's best-known exports: in 2025, tea exports earned more than 1.5 billion US dollars.

    Tea isn't native to Sri Lanka. In the 1800s, British planters grew coffee in the hills. Then, starting in 1869, a fungus called coffee leaf rust destroyed the coffee plantations. Planters switched to tea, which a British planter named James Taylor had begun growing commercially in 1867. Within a few decades, the green, terraced slopes of the central highlands were covered in tea bushes.

    The plantations depended on workers brought by the British from South India, the ancestors of today's Hill Country Tamils. For generations, many lived in crowded housing on the estates, with low pay, and a 1948 citizenship law left most of them without Sri Lankan citizenship for decades. Their descendants still make up much of the tea workforce, and many families today are still pushing for better wages and housing.

    19.13 Sri Pada (Adam's Peak)¶

    Sri Pada (Adam's Peak) is a cone-shaped mountain 2,243 m (7,359 ft) high in central Sri Lanka, sacred to people of several faiths because of a footprint-shaped mark in the rock near its summit. Sri Pada (ශ්‍රී පාද) means "sacred footprint."

    Each faith tells its own story about that mark:

    Tradition Whose footprint?
    Buddhist The Buddha's
    Sri Lankan Hindu Shiva's (its Tamil name, Sivanolipatha Malai, refers to Shiva)
    Some Muslim and Christian traditions Adam's, from his first step on Earth after leaving paradise (hence "Adam's Peak")

    Pilgrims climb mostly between December and May, often at night by lit stairways, to reach the top at sunrise. At dawn, the mountain casts a perfect triangle of shadow across the plains below. Pilgrims of different faiths climb the same steps in the same crowd, and each honors the mark in their own way.

    19.14 Sri Lanka Today¶

    Like Bangladesh, Sri Lanka has been through big changes recently.

    • 2022 economic crisis: After years of heavy debt, tax cuts and lost tourism, Sri Lanka ran out of foreign currency. In April 2022 the government announced it was defaulting on its foreign debt, the first default in its history. Fuel, food and medicine ran short, and huge protests, called the Aragalaya ("struggle"), filled the streets. President Gotabaya Rajapaksa left the country and resigned in July 2022, and Parliament elected Ranil Wickremesinghe to serve the rest of his term.
    • Recovery: In March 2023, the International Monetary Fund approved a four-year loan program of about 3 billion US dollars, and the economy began to stabilize.
    • 2024 elections: Anura Kumara Dissanayake, of the National People's Power (NPP) alliance, won the presidential election and took office on 23 September 2024. In the parliamentary election of 14 November 2024, the NPP won 159 of 225 seats, the first time a single party won a two-thirds majority under the proportional voting system Sri Lanka has used since 1989. Harini Amarasuriya became prime minister.
    • Cyclone Ditwah: In late November 2025, Cyclone Ditwah brought catastrophic floods and landslides. By early December, the Disaster Management Center reported more than 600 deaths, with Kandy District hit hardest.
    • 2026: In May 2026, the IMF completed another review of Sri Lanka's program, describing performance as generally strong while warning about the cyclone's aftermath. On 4 October 2026, IMF staff reached agreement on the seventh review, with the next payment of about 345 million US dollars depending on a 2027 budget that fits the program.

    As of October 2026, Anura Kumara Dissanayake is president. Sri Lankans still disagree about what caused the crisis and what the country should do next.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: Bangladesh is mostly desert or dry land.
    Reality: Bangladesh is one of the wettest, greenest countries in the world. Most of it is a low river delta, about 83% of it floodplain, and in an average year about 21% of the country floods.

    Myth: Sri Lanka is part of India.
    Reality: Sri Lanka is an independent island country, independent since 4 February 1948, with its own government, languages, currency and history. India is its close neighbor across the narrow Palk Strait.

    Myth: The Sri Lankan civil war was a religious war between Buddhists and Hindus.
    Reality: The war (1983 to 2009) was mainly about ethnicity, language, political power and land, between the government and the LTTE, which fought for a separate Tamil state. Religion overlapped with ethnic identity, but it was not the main cause, and there were Christians and others on both sides.

    Myth: Ceylon tea is a native Sri Lankan plant that has always grown there.
    Reality: British planters introduced tea as a commercial crop in 1867 and expanded it after coffee leaf rust destroyed the coffee plantations from 1869. The plantations relied on workers brought from South India.

    Check Yourself¶

    1. Which three rivers meet in the Bengal Delta, and what are two of them called in Bangladesh?

    The Ganges, the Brahmaputra and the Meghna. In Bangladesh the Ganges is called the Padma and the Brahmaputra the Jamuna.

    2. What happened on 21 February 1952, and how is the day remembered worldwide?

    Students in Dhaka were killed by police while protesting for Bangla to be a state language of Pakistan. Since 1999, UNESCO has marked 21 February as International Mother Language Day.

    3. What is microfinance, and who won the Nobel Peace Prize for it in 2006?

    Microfinance means very small loans and banking services for poor people who can't borrow from ordinary banks. Muhammad Yunus and Grameen Bank shared the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.

    4. What is the difference between Sri Lankan Tamils and Hill Country Tamils?

    Sri Lankan Tamils have lived on the island for many centuries, mostly in the north and east. Hill Country (Indian-origin) Tamils descend from workers the British brought from South India in the 1800s and 1900s to work on plantations.

    5. Why is Sri Pada sacred to more than one faith?

    A footprint-shaped mark near the summit is honored by Buddhists as the Buddha's, by many Sri Lankan Hindus as Shiva's, and in some Muslim and Christian traditions as Adam's.

      Answer

      Bangladesh: Dhaka, 175,686,899 (about 176 million). Sri Lanka: Sri Jayawardenepura Kotte (legislative) and Colombo (commercial), 21,756,000 (about 22 million).

    2. When did the Sri Lankan Civil War begin and end, and what range of deaths did the UN estimate soon after it ended?

      Answer

      It ran from 1983 to May 2009. The UN estimated 80,000 to 100,000 people killed over the whole conflict.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why floods in Bangladesh can be both harmful and helpful.

      Answer

      Big floods destroy homes, crops and lives, but normal floods water the fields and leave fresh silt that keeps delta soil fertile for rice, jute and fish.

    2. Explain how the Sinhala Only Act of 1956 contributed to tensions that led to the civil war.

      Answer

      It made Sinhala the only official language and left out Tamil, so many Tamils felt shut out of government jobs, schools and courts. It became a symbol of discrimination that fed the demand for a separate Tamil state.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Bangladesh, the People's Republic of Bangladesh, became independent in 1971; its capital is Dhaka and it had 175,686,899 people in 2025 (World Bank).
    • Most of Bangladesh sits on the Bengal Delta, the world's largest, where the Padma, Jamuna and Meghna meet; about 21% of the country floods in an average year, and floods bring both danger and fertile silt.
    • The Sundarbans, the world's largest mangrove forest, protects the coast and shelters Bengal tigers; Ekushey February (21 February 1952) is now International Mother Language Day.
    • Garments make up more than four-fifths of Bangladesh's exports, and Grameen Bank's microfinance won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize; since February 2026, Tarique Rahman has been prime minister.
    • Sri Lanka (Ceylon until 1972) is an island of 21,756,000 people (2025, World Bank), mostly Sinhala-speaking Buddhists, with large Tamil, Muslim and Christian communities; Colombo is its commercial capital and Anuradhapura its ancient one.
    • The Sri Lankan Civil War (1983 to 2009) was mainly about ethnicity, language and power; the UN estimated 80,000 to 100,000 deaths, and families remember it in very different ways.
    • Ceylon tea and Sri Pada show the island's layered history; after the 2022 crisis, President Anura Kumara Dissanayake has led Sri Lanka since September 2024.
