    3. 21. The South Asian Diaspora

    Chapter 21: The South Asian Diaspora¶

    Summary¶

    Millions of people with roots in South Asia live outside the region, in places as different as Guyana, Fiji, Kenya, London, Dubai and the Twin Cities. This chapter explains what a diaspora is and follows the major waves of South Asian migration: indentured laborers sent to sugar colonies after slavery ended, workers and traders in East Africa, postwar migrants to Britain, workers in the oil-rich Gulf, and the long, uneven story of South Asians in the United States, from Punjabi farmers in California to families who arrived after 1965. It ends here at home, with Census numbers for Minnesota and a look at desi identity and families that live across borders.

    Every family has a moving story

    Hi, it's Mora! If your family came from somewhere else, someone made a huge decision to leave home, and you are part of what happened next. This chapter helps you see your own family's move as one thread in a much bigger story, and it gives you the words to ask about it.

    21.1 "Why Did You Come Here?"¶

    It's a snowy Saturday, and Zara Hossain is at Ben Lindqvist's house for his grandmother's birthday. On the wall is a framed black-and-white photo of a farm couple standing beside a wooden house. "That's my great-great-grandparents," Ben says. "They came from Sweden in the 1880s. Grandma says they were poor and there wasn't enough land back home, so they came to Minnesota to farm."

    Zara looks at the photo for a long time. She has heard bits of her own family's story: her parents grew up in Chattogram, Bangladesh, and moved to Minnesota in the 2000s, before she was born. But she has never asked the real questions. Why did they leave? Why Minnesota, of all places? Did they plan to stay forever?

    That night at dinner, Zara asks her mother, "Why did you and Abbu come here?" Her mother laughs, then gets quiet, then says, "That's a long story. Ask Nanu about her brother first. He went to England before any of us left." Zara realizes her family's move was not one trip but part of a chain of moves, across generations and continents. How did so many South Asian families end up scattered around the world, and why did they go where they went?

    21.2 What Is a Diaspora?¶

    Start with something Ben already knows. Many Swedish and Norwegian immigrants settled in the Midwest, especially Minnesota, and their descendants still keep some traditions alive, in Lutheran churches, family recipes and places like the American Swedish Institute in Minneapolis. They are Americans, but many still feel a connection to the "old country." That's the basic idea of a diaspora.

    A diaspora (dye-AS-puh-ruh) is a population that lives outside its ancestral homeland but keeps some connection to it, through memory, language, faith, food, family ties or a shared sense of identity. The word comes from Greek and means "scattering," like seeds thrown across a field. People with roots in all eight South Asian countries form diasporas around the world.

    Why do people move? Historians often talk about push factors (things that push people out, such as poverty, war, persecution or lack of land) and pull factors (things that pull them in, such as jobs, schools, safety or family already there). Most migrations have both. Laws and empires matter too: who is allowed to move, and who is wanted.

    South Asian migration came in waves, each with its own causes:

    Wave When Where to Main cause
    Indenture 1830s to about 1920 Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, South Africa Plantations needed labor after slavery was abolished
    East Africa Late 1800s onward Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania Railway work and trade under British rule
    Early North America About 1900 to 1917 California, the US West Coast, Canada Farm, lumber and railway jobs
    Britain After 1947, especially 1950s to 1970s British cities Postwar labor shortages; Commonwealth ties
    The Gulf 1970s onward Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar and neighbors The oil boom
    United States After 1965 Cities and suburbs across the US A new immigration law; jobs and study
    Refugees Many periods Many countries War, persecution, expulsion

    Notice how many of these waves are tied to the British Empire. Britain ruled or controlled much of South Asia until 1947, and it also ran colonies in the Caribbean, Africa and the Pacific. Its ships, laws and labor needs moved South Asians around the world.

    21.3 Indenture: The Sugar Ships¶

    The first large wave began with the end of slavery. When Britain abolished slavery across its empire by an act of Parliament in 1833, plantation owners in sugar colonies such as British Guiana (now Guyana), Trinidad and Mauritius lost their enslaved workforce. They looked for a new source of cheap labor and found it in British India.

    Indentured Labor was a system in which workers signed a contract, called an indenture, to work for a set number of years (usually five) on a plantation far from home, in return for passage, wages, housing and sometimes a return ticket. Indian workers called the contract girmit, their way of saying the English word "agreement," and the workers became known as girmitiyas.

    On 29 January 1838, the ship Hesperus sailed from Calcutta (now Kolkata) with a sister ship, the Whitby. The Hesperus reached British Guiana on 5 May 1838 with 165 workers on board; thirteen others had died on the voyage. Guyanese still mark 5 May as Indian Arrival Day. Over the next eighty years, ships carried workers from the ports of Calcutta and Madras (now Chennai) to colonies across the world. Historians estimate that somewhere between about 1.3 million and more than 1.6 million South Asians went overseas under indenture before the system ended around 1920.

    Destination When indenture ran Note
    Mauritius From 1834 Among the first and largest destinations
    British Guiana (Guyana) 1838 to 1917 About 239,000 arrived
    Trinidad 1845 to 1917 First ship, the Fatel Razack, on 30 May 1845; about 147,600 arrived
    Fiji 1879 to 1916 First ship, the Leonidas, on 14 May 1879
    Also 1800s to early 1900s South Africa (Natal), Suriname, Jamaica and other colonies

    Many recruits came from poor villages in what are now the Indian states of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, and others from the south. Some joined to escape poverty; many were misled by recruiters with false promises, and some were kidnapped outright. Colonial officials called it "free" migration, but conditions on the plantations were often harsh. Public outrage in India and Britain over abuses helped end the system: Fiji stopped indenture in 1916, and all remaining contracts there were cancelled on 1 January 1920.

    Was it a choice?

    Workers signed contracts, so planters called indenture "free labor." Critics at the time called it a new form of slavery, and historians still debate how similar the two were. To me, "signing" doesn't mean much when you're poor, far from home and can be jailed for quitting.

    Indo-Caribbean Communities¶

    Most indentured workers never went back. They stayed, saved, bought land, built temples and mosques, and raised families. Indo-Caribbean Communities are the descendants of these South Asian migrants living in the Caribbean, especially in Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, and Suriname, where they are the largest ethnic group. In Guyana's 2012 census about 40% of people were Indo-Guyanese, and in Trinidad and Tobago's 2011 census about 35% were of Indian descent.

    Indo-Caribbean culture is its own thing, not a copy of India. Over generations, families blended South Asian and Caribbean food, music, language and faith. Many Indo-Caribbeans are Hindu, many are Muslim and many are Christian. Trinidad marks Indian Arrival Day on 30 May.

    Many Indo-Caribbean families have since moved again, especially to the New York area; since the 1960s a large community has grown in Richmond Hill, Queens, where a spring Phagwah (Holi) parade draws thousands of people each year. A person's family history might run from Bihar to Guyana to Queens. The same pattern happened in Fiji, where many Indo-Fijians left after coups in 1987, and in Mauritius and South Africa.

    21.4 East Africa¶

    The Indian Ocean had linked South Asia and East Africa for a long time through trade carried by the monsoon winds (remember Chapter 1).

    British colonial rule brought a much larger wave. Between 1896 and 1901, the British built the Uganda Railway, which ran from the port of Mombasa in Kenya inland to Lake Victoria. More than 30,000 workers were brought from British India to build it, many from Punjab and Gujarat. About 2,500 of them died, from disease, accidents and harsh conditions. Many who survived stayed when their contracts ended, and others followed as shopkeepers, clerks, craftspeople and civil servants.

    South Asians in East Africa refers to the communities of Indian, and later Pakistani, origin that grew up in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania and neighboring countries from the late 1800s on. Under British rule they often held a middle position: below European officials, but in jobs, such as running shops and clerking, that many Africans were kept out of. That position helped some families prosper, and it also bred resentment that politicians would later use.

    The Uganda Expulsion of 1972¶

    The Uganda Expulsion of 1972 was the forced removal of nearly all South Asians from Uganda by the military ruler Idi Amin, who announced on 4 August 1972 that they had 90 days to leave. About 80,000 people of South Asian descent lived in Uganda at the time, many of them born there. Amin accused them of disloyalty and of hurting the economy; their community leaders rejected those claims. Families had to abandon homes and businesses and could take very little with them.

    Most held British passports from the colonial era, and around 28,000 went to the United Kingdom, where many first stayed in temporary resettlement camps. Thousands of others went to Canada, India, Kenya, Pakistan and elsewhere. Many rebuilt businesses and became well known in British towns such as Leicester. Uganda's later president, Yoweri Museveni, invited Asians to return, and some did.

    Whose Story?

    The Uganda expulsion is told very differently depending on who is telling it. In Britain it is often remembered as a story of welcome and success, with refugees who arrived with almost nothing and built successful businesses. But many families recall a colder arrival: the city council of Leicester even placed newspaper adverts in Uganda telling people not to come there. In Uganda, the expulsion is remembered as part of Amin's brutal rule, in which human rights groups estimate that somewhere between 100,000 and 500,000 Ugandans were killed, and a later president, Yoweri Museveni, encouraged Asians to come back. Some Ugandan Asians themselves remember Uganda as the only home they knew, and they don't describe it as "going back" to South Asia at all.

    21.5 British South Asians¶

    South Asians have lived in Britain for centuries. Sailors called lascars, many from Sylhet (in today's Bangladesh), worked on British ships and some settled in port cities. Students, servants and a few wealthy visitors came during colonial rule.

    The big wave came after 1947. British South Asians are people in the United Kingdom with roots in South Asia, a community that grew sharply after independence when Britain needed workers to rebuild after World War II. As Commonwealth citizens, people from India and Pakistan could come fairly easily, and many did in the 1950s and 1960s:

    • From Pakistan, many came from Mirpur, in Pakistani-administered Kashmir, after the Mangla Dam, built in the 1960s, flooded villages and displaced tens of thousands. Others came from Punjab. Many worked in the textile mills of Lancashire and Yorkshire and in factories in the Midlands.
    • From India, many came from Punjab and Gujarat, and others arrived later by way of East Africa, especially after the Uganda expulsion.
    • From Bangladesh, most came from the Sylhet region, with migration peaking in the 1970s. Many settled in East London's Tower Hamlets, around Brick Lane, and Sylheti families opened many of the restaurants Britons call "Indian."

    Britain began restricting Commonwealth immigration with the Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962, which allowed in mainly people with limited work vouchers, and families who came to join relatives. Many communities faced racism, including violent attacks, and organized to fight it.

    Today, British South Asians are one of the largest groups in Britain. In the 2021 Census of England and Wales:

    Group Number (2021) Share of population
    Indian 1,864,304 3.1%
    Pakistani 1,587,822 2.7%
    Bangladeshi 644,900 1.1%

    British South Asians include members of Parliament, doctors, business owners, artists and athletes, and British culture has absorbed South Asian food, music and words. Zara's great-uncle, the one who went to England, is part of this story.

    21.6 The Gulf: Workers and Money Home¶

    In the 1970s, oil prices shot up and the countries of the Persian Gulf, such as Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates (UAE), Kuwait, Qatar, Oman and Bahrain, became very rich very fast. They needed workers to build roads, airports, hospitals and whole new cities, and to staff homes, shops and offices.

    Gulf Migrant Workers are people from South Asia and other regions who go to work in the Gulf states, usually on temporary contracts, without the chance to become citizens. The wave started in the 1970s and has never stopped. Between 1972 and 1983, mass migration from the Indian state of Kerala to the Gulf was so large that it is called the "Kerala Gulf Boom." Workers also come in large numbers from Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal and Sri Lanka. Indians are the largest national group in the UAE.

    Life for Gulf workers varies hugely. Some are engineers, doctors and bankers with good salaries. Many more are construction workers, drivers, domestic workers and cleaners. In most Gulf states, a sponsorship system called kafala has tied a worker's job and legal residence to one employer, giving employers great power. The International Labour Organization and rights groups have warned that this leaves many workers, especially domestic workers, with weak protections. Some countries, such as Qatar and Saudi Arabia, have made reforms in recent years, which critics say are not always enforced.

    Don't flatten Gulf workers into one story

    It's easy to picture Gulf migration as only hardship or only riches. In real families it's usually both: a father missing his kids' childhood while his salary pays for their school. Ask before you assume.

    Remittances¶

    Remittances are money that migrants send home to their families. Gulf workers send much of their pay home, and so do South Asians in Britain, the US and everywhere else. That money pays for food, school fees, weddings, medical care and new houses, and it adds up to very large sums.

    Country Remittances received, 2025 (World Bank)
    India about US$150.7 billion, the most of any country in the world
    Pakistan about US$40.5 billion
    Bangladesh about US$33.9 billion
    Nepal about US$14.7 billion, equal to about 32% of Nepal's GDP

    You read in Chapter 20 how important remittances are to Nepal.

    21.7 South Asians in the United States¶

    Punjabi Pioneers in California¶

    Many people think South Asians first came to the US in the 1960s. They didn't. Punjabi Pioneers in California were the South Asian men, mostly Sikhs from Punjab, who came to the US West Coast in the early 1900s to work in mills, on railways and on farms, and who later farmed in California's agricultural valleys.

    They faced hostility from the start. On 4 September 1907, a mob in Bellingham, Washington, attacked and drove out South Asian mill workers. Newspapers called them "Hindus," even though most were Sikh. In 1912 the pioneers founded a gurdwara in Stockton, California, the first Sikh house of worship in the US. The Immigration Act of 1917 then created an "Asiatic Barred Zone" that banned new immigrants from British India, Afghanistan and much of Asia.

    Because almost no South Asian women could come, and because California law banned marriages between people labeled as different races, many Punjabi men married Mexican and Mexican American women who worked beside them in the fields. Almost 400 of these Punjabi-Mexican couples lived in California's farm valleys, raising children who spoke Spanish and English and ate both roti and tortillas.

    The Bhagat Singh Thind Case¶

    The Bhagat Singh Thind Case (United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind, 1923) was a US Supreme Court decision that ruled South Asians were not "white persons" and therefore could not become naturalized citizens. At the time, US law allowed only "free white persons" and people of African descent to naturalize.

    Bhagat Singh Thind was a Sikh man born in Punjab who served in the US Army during World War I. He argued that, as an Indian of "Aryan" descent, he counted as Caucasian and therefore white. On 19 February 1923 the Court unanimously rejected his claim, saying the law used "white" in its everyday meaning, not a scientific one. After the decision, the government moved to strip citizenship from many South Asians who had already naturalized, and it left them barred from owning farmland under California's 1913 Alien Land Law, which applied to immigrants "ineligible for citizenship."

    The ruling stood for more than twenty years. Thind himself became a citizen in 1936 under a law for World War I veterans. In 1946 the Luce-Celler Act finally let Indians naturalize, with an immigration quota of just 100 people a year. One person who benefited was Dalip Singh Saund, a Punjabi farmer and judge in California, who in 1956 became the first Indian American, and the first Sikh, elected to the US Congress.

    The Immigration Act of 1965¶

    The Immigration Act of 1965 (the Immigration and Nationality Act, also called the Hart-Celler Act) was a US law, signed by President Lyndon Johnson at the Statue of Liberty on 3 October 1965, that ended the "national origins" quota system, which had heavily favored immigrants from northern and western Europe since the 1920s. The new system gave preference to people with family already in the US and to people with skills the country needed.

    The effect on South Asian immigration was huge. Doctors, engineers, scientists and students came first, and then their spouses, parents and siblings through family reunification. Later decades brought more varied arrivals: taxi drivers and shop owners as well as tech workers, refugees from Bhutan and Afghanistan, and students from every South Asian country.

    This is how big South Asian American communities were in 2023, according to Pew Research Center fact sheets based on Census data:

    Origin Population in the US (2023, Pew)
    Indian about 5.2 million
    Pakistani about 680,000
    Bangladeshi about 300,000
    Nepalese about 225,000
    Sri Lankan about 85,000
    Bhutanese about 20,000

    Indian Americans are the second-largest Asian origin group in the US, about 21% of all Asian Americans. Most South Asian Americans are immigrants or children of immigrants: 66% of Indian Americans and 71% of Bangladeshi Americans were born abroad (2023, Pew).

    The Model Minority Myth¶

    The Model Minority Myth is the stereotype that Asian Americans, including South Asian Americans, are all successful, wealthy, highly educated and free of problems, often used to compare them favorably with other minority groups. It is called a myth because it hides real differences and real struggles.

    Look at Pew's numbers (2023 data, published 2025):

    Group Median household income Share living in poverty
    Indian Americans about $151,200 6%
    Nepalese Americans about $94,800 9%
    Bangladeshi Americans about $78,400 14%
    All Asian Americans about $105,600 10%

    Bangladeshi-headed households earned roughly half as much as Indian-headed ones. Behind any average are taxi drivers, restaurant workers and families just getting by, as well as doctors and engineers.

    The myth causes harm in several ways. It makes struggling students feel invisible or ashamed to ask for help. It pressures teens toward a few "approved" careers. It ignores how the 1965 law selected for highly educated immigrants, which explains part of the success story. And it is used to pit Asian Americans against Black and Latino Americans, as if each group's outcomes were only about effort.

    Post-9/11 Backlash¶

    After the terrorist attacks of 11 September 2001, many South Asian, Arab and Muslim Americans faced a wave of hatred. Post-9/11 Backlash refers to the hate crimes, harassment, profiling and discrimination aimed at people perceived as Muslim or Middle Eastern after the attacks, including many South Asians.

    The first killing reported as revenge for the attacks happened on 15 September 2001, in Mesa, Arizona. Balbir Singh Sodhi, a Sikh immigrant who owned a gas station, was shot and killed by a man who wanted revenge for 9/11 and who profiled him as an Arab Muslim because of his turban and beard. Sodhi was not Muslim, and he had nothing to do with the attacks, which shows how hatred lumped together people of different faiths and backgrounds. FBI data show that reported anti-Islamic hate crime incidents jumped from 28 in 2000 to 481 in 2001, and attacks on people because of their ethnicity, including South Asians, also rose sharply.

    Many communities responded by organizing. The Sikh Coalition, for example, was formed by volunteers on the night of 11 September 2001 to defend Sikh Americans' civil rights, and Sodhi's family later gave one of his turbans to the Smithsonian's 9/11 collection. Many teens today hear about this period from their parents, who remember changing how they dressed, or being stopped at airports again and again.

    21.8 South Asians in Minnesota¶

    Now bring it home. South Asians in Minnesota are the residents of Minnesota with roots in South Asia, a community that today includes families from every South Asian country, with temples, mosques, gurdwaras, cultural associations and student clubs across the Twin Cities and beyond.

    How many are there? The best source is the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS). Here are its estimates for Minnesota, averaged over 2020 to 2024 (the most recent five-year data available in October 2026):

    Group (Census label) Estimate in Minnesota, 2020-2024 ACS Margin of error
    Asian Indian 52,192 ± 2,528
    Pakistani 3,613 ± 964
    Nepalese 3,023 ± 707
    Bangladeshi 1,935 ± 572
    Sri Lankan 1,927 ± 598
    Afghan (listed by the Census as Central Asian) 1,380 ± 719
    Bhutanese 694 ± 710

    Add the South Asian groups and you get about 64,000 people, a little over 1% of Minnesota's roughly 5.7 million residents (US Census Bureau, ACS 2020-2024, table B02018).

    How to read a Census table

    That "±" is the margin of error: the Census surveys a sample, not everyone, so small groups have big uncertainty. A person who checks two groups is counted in both, and someone who writes "Punjabi" or "Sikh" might be counted differently from their cousin, so treat these numbers as good estimates, not exact headcounts.

    Minnesota's South Asian community includes people who came for work or study, Nepali, Bhutanese and Afghan families, and second- and third-generation teens like Zara and Nima. Ask around your school and you'll likely find families from several of the eight countries.

    21.9 Desi Identity and Transnational Families¶

    Desi Identity¶

    You've probably heard the word desi. In Bangla it's দেশি (DAY-shee), and in Hindi देसी (DAY-see); both come from desh, "homeland" or "country," the word you met in Chapter 1. Desi Identity is the shared sense of belonging that many people of South Asian heritage, especially in the diaspora, express with the word "desi," meaning roughly "one of us, from back home."

    In the diaspora, people from different countries, faiths and languages often discover they have a lot in common: similar foods, film songs, cricket and big family weddings. A Pakistani kid and an Indian kid in Minnesota may feel closer to each other than either expected. Many South Asian student clubs, like Zara's, are built on that shared desi feeling.

    But "desi" doesn't fit everyone the same way. Some Afghans, Nepalis, Sri Lankans, Bhutanese and Maldivians don't use the word for themselves. Many Indo-Caribbean people have their own words and traditions. A good club makes room for people to name themselves.

    Transnational Families¶

    Transnational Families are families whose members live in two or more countries but stay closely connected, sharing money, decisions, care and daily life across borders. Many South Asian diaspora families live this way. A grandmother in Dhaka joins dinner every night by video call. A cousin in Dubai sends money for a sister's wedding in Kerala. Parents in Minnesota debate whether to retire "back home." Teens spend summers with grandparents in Lahore or Kathmandu.

    Living across borders brings both strength and strain. Families keep languages and traditions alive, and they can help each other in a crisis. But they also miss weddings and funerals, worry about relatives far away, and sometimes feel they don't fully belong in either place. Zara feels that when she answers her Nanu in English. Many second-generation teens describe it as being "too South Asian" in one place and "too American" in the other, and then slowly building an identity that is fully both.

    Back at dinner, Zara's mother finally tells the story. Nanu's brother went to London in the 1970s and worked in a restaurant. Years later, he helped Zara's father get a student visa, and her parents chose Minnesota because a friend from Chattogram already lived here and said the schools were good. "So it was a chain," Zara says. "Everyone pulled the next person." Her mother nods. "That's how it always works."

    Ask Your Elders¶

    Your interview for this chapter

    Talk with a parent, grandparent, aunt, uncle, family friend or community elder about how and why your family (or theirs) moved: when they came, what the first weeks were like, what they brought and what they miss. If you don't have a South Asian elder to ask, interview a club advisor, a local temple, mosque, gurdwara or cultural center volunteer, or a classmate's family (with permission). Ten minutes is enough.

    Before you start: Ask if they would like to look at an old passport, ticket, photo or letter together. Objects bring back memories. Ask before you take notes or record.

    Questions to ask

    1. Where were you living before you moved, and what was a normal day like there?
    2. Why did you decide to leave? Was there one moment when you knew you were going?
    3. Why here? Did you know anyone in this country or city before you came?
    4. What do you remember about your first weeks: the weather, the food, school or work, people who helped?
    5. What did you bring with you, and what do you wish you had brought? How has your connection to back home changed over the years?
    6. What part of where you came from do you hope I'll keep?

    Listen for: push and pull factors behind their move; links to a wave in this chapter, such as the Immigration Act of 1965, Gulf Migrant Workers or British South Asians; and signs of Transnational Families, such as Remittances, video calls or relatives in several countries.

    Save it: Write down one quote or story in their words, with their permission. If they agree, you can share it with the Roots & Rivers Story Archive.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: South Asians only came to the US after 1965.
    Reality: Punjabi Sikh workers came to the US West Coast in the early 1900s, founded a gurdwara in Stockton in 1912 and farmed in California for decades. Laws in 1917 and the 1923 Thind decision then shut most South Asians out until the 1940s and 1960s.

    Myth: All South Asian Americans are doctors or engineers, and they're all well off.
    Reality: That's the model minority myth. In 2023, the median household income of Bangladeshi Americans was about half that of Indian Americans, and 14% of Bangladeshi Americans lived in poverty (Pew). South Asian Americans work in every kind of job.

    Myth: Indenture was just voluntary migration for better jobs.
    Reality: Workers did sign contracts, but many were poor, misled by recruiters and bound by laws that made quitting a crime. Critics at the time compared it to slavery, and historians still debate how similar the two systems were.

    Myth: The South Asian diaspora is all Indian.
    Reality: There are large Pakistani and Bangladeshi communities in Britain, Nepali and Bangladeshi workers in the Gulf, Sri Lankan Tamil communities in Canada, and Afghan, Bhutanese and Nepali families in the US, including in Minnesota.

    Check Yourself¶

    1. What is a diaspora?

    A diaspora is a population living outside its ancestral homeland that keeps some connection to it, through family, faith, language, food or shared identity. The word comes from Greek for "scattering."

    2. Why did British sugar colonies start recruiting indentured workers from India in the 1830s?

    Britain abolished slavery across its empire, so plantation owners lost their enslaved workforce and turned to contract workers from British India.

    3. What happened in Uganda in 1972?

    Idi Amin gave about 80,000 people of South Asian descent 90 days to leave the country. Around 28,000 went to Britain, and others went to Canada, India and elsewhere.

    4. What did the Supreme Court decide in the Bhagat Singh Thind case?

    In 1923 it ruled unanimously that South Asians were not "white persons" under US law and so could not become naturalized citizens. Many who had already naturalized lost their citizenship.

    5. What are remittances, and why do they matter to countries like Nepal?

    Remittances are money migrants send home. In 2025 they equaled about 32% of Nepal's GDP (World Bank), so they pay for food, schooling and housing for many families.

    Exercises¶

    Remember¶

    1. List three destinations of Indian indentured laborers and the year indenture began in one of them.

      Answer

      Any three of: Mauritius (from 1834), British Guiana/Guyana (1838), Trinidad (1845), Fiji (1879), South Africa, Suriname, Jamaica.

    2. What did the Immigration Act of 1965 end, and who signed it?

      Answer

      It ended the national origins quota system that favored northern and western European immigrants. President Lyndon Johnson signed it on 3 October 1965.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why many Punjabi men in early 1900s California married Mexican and Mexican American women.

      Answer

      Almost no South Asian women could immigrate, and California law banned marriages between people labeled as different races. Mexican and Mexican American women often worked beside Punjabi men in the fields, and those marriages were allowed.

    2. Explain why the model minority myth is called a myth.

      Answer

      It hides big differences between groups and families, such as the gap in income and poverty between Indian and Bangladeshi Americans, and it ignores how US immigration law selected highly educated immigrants. It also harms people who struggle and is used to compare minority groups unfairly.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A diaspora is a scattered population that keeps ties to its homeland; South Asians from all eight countries form diasporas worldwide.
    • After Britain abolished slavery, about 1.3 to 1.6 million South Asians went overseas as indentured laborers from the 1830s to about 1920, creating Indo-Caribbean, Indo-Fijian and other communities.
    • South Asians built and traded in East Africa from the late 1800s; in 1972 Idi Amin expelled about 80,000 from Uganda, and around 28,000 went to Britain.
    • British South Asians grew after 1947; in 2021 about 1.86 million Indian, 1.59 million Pakistani and 645,000 Bangladeshi people lived in England and Wales.
    • The 1970s oil boom drew millions of South Asian workers to the Gulf, and remittances now total about US$150.7 billion a year for India alone (2025, World Bank).
    • In the US, Punjabi pioneers arrived around 1900, the 1923 Thind decision blocked citizenship, and the 1965 Immigration Act opened the door to today's large communities, which still face the model minority myth and faced a wave of post-9/11 hate.
    • About 64,000 people in Minnesota belong to South Asian groups (ACS 2020-2024), and many live in transnational families that span continents.
