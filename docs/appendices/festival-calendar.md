# Appendix C: Festival Calendar 2026-27

Planning a club celebration, or just want to know when your friends' big days fall? This appendix lists festivals and national days across South Asia's faiths and countries from November 2026 through December 2027. Before the table, a short guide explains why so many of these dates move around from year to year.

## C.1 Why Festival Dates Move: Calendar Conversion

The calendar on your phone is the **Gregorian calendar**, a solar calendar of 365 days (366 in leap years) that most of the world uses for school, work and government. But many festivals in South Asia are set by other calendars, and every year their Gregorian dates have to be worked out again. **Calendar Conversion** means finding the date in one calendar that matches a date in another, for example working out which Gregorian day will be the first day of the month of Shawwal, when Eid al-Fitr begins.

South Asia uses four main kinds of calendar:

| Kind | What it follows | Year length | Example festivals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solar | The sun only | about 365 days | New Year's Day; Vaisakhi; Pongal; Sinhala and Tamil New Year |
| Lunar | The moon only | 354 or 355 days | Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, Ashura (Hijri calendar) |
| Lunisolar | Moon for months, sun for the year | months of about 29.5 days, with an extra month added some years | Diwali, Holi, Dashain, Vesak, Guru Nanak Jayanti |
| Gregorian (civil) | The sun, with fixed month lengths | 365 or 366 days | National days such as 15 August or 16 December |

**Lunar calendars** count only moons. The **Hijri calendar**, used by Muslims for religious dates, has twelve months of 29 or 30 days, so its year is about 11 days shorter than a Gregorian year. That's why Ramadan and the two Eids move about 11 days earlier every year and slowly travel through all the seasons. In many places a new month begins only when the new crescent moon is actually seen, so the exact day is often announced the night before.

**Lunisolar calendars** use moon-based months but add an extra month every few years, so that festivals stay in roughly the same season. Many Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh and Jain festivals follow these calendars. Diwali, for example, always falls in October or November, but its exact date jumps around within that window.

**Solar festivals** follow the sun's position, so they land on nearly the same Gregorian date each year. Vaisakhi and the Sinhala and Tamil New Year fall around 13 to 14 April, and Pongal and Makar Sankranti around 14 to 15 January.

Two national calendars are worth knowing:

- **Bikram Sambat** is Nepal's official calendar. Its years are about 56 to 57 years "ahead" of the Gregorian count, and its new year begins in mid-April with the month of Baisakh.
- **The Bangla calendar** (Bangabda) is used in Bangladesh and by Bengalis in India. Its new year, Pohela Boishakh, is fixed on 14 April in Bangladesh. In West Bengal and other parts of India, the traditional calculation can put it on 14 or 15 April.

### A worked example

Here is how three calendars line up on the same spring day in 2026, and what happens a year later.

1. **14 April 2026 (Gregorian)** was New Year's Day in two other calendars at once. In Nepal it was Baisakh 1, 2083 in Bikram Sambat. In Bangladesh it was Pohela Boishakh, the first day of the Bangla year 1433.
2. **Converting the year numbers.** From mid-April to December, add 57 to the Gregorian year to get the Bikram Sambat year (2026 + 57 = 2083). From January to mid-April, add 56. For the Bangla year after 14 April, subtract 593 (2026 - 593 = 1433).
3. **Now follow a lunar festival.** Eid al-Fitr was on 21 March 2026 in Pakistan and Bangladesh. In 2027 it is expected around 10 March. That is 11 days earlier, because the Hijri year is about 11 days shorter than the Gregorian year.
4. **And a lunisolar one.** Diwali falls on 8 November 2026 and on 29 October 2027 in India. That's ten days earlier, but still in the same autumn season, because the lunisolar calendar adds an extra month every few years to catch up with the sun.
