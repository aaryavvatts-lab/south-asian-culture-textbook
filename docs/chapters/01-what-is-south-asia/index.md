# Chapter 1: What Is South Asia?

## Summary

South Asia is a region of eight countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. Together they were home to about 1.99 billion people in 2025, roughly one in four people on Earth. This chapter explains the difference between a region, a subcontinent and a country, then tours the land itself: the mountain walls in the north, the three great rivers, the plains, plateau, desert and coasts, and the monsoon that brings most of the year's rain. By the end you'll be able to find all eight countries on a map and explain why so many people live where they do.

## 1.1 "Wait, Isn't Bangladesh Part of India?"

It's a Tuesday after school, and Zara Hossain and Ben Lindqvist are sitting in Ms. Okafor's classroom at Prairie Ridge High School. Zara is co-founding the school's South Asian Student Association, and the first meeting is in two weeks. Ben, her best friend since fourth grade, has already signed up as the club's first member who isn't South Asian. Ms. Okafor, their faculty advisor, has asked them for one thing: a single slide that answers "What counts as South Asia?"

Ben is typing notes. "Okay, so your parents are from Bangladesh. That's part of India, right? Like a state?"

Zara opens her mouth to say no, then stops. She knows Bangladesh is its own country. Her parents grew up in Chattogram, a port city in Bangladesh, and her Nanu (her mother's mother) still tells stories about it at dinner. But Zara has also heard her uncle say "the subcontinent," and her cousin in London say "South Asian," and a relative call everyone from that part of the world "desi." She realizes she can't explain the difference between a country, a subcontinent and a region. If the words themselves are confusing, how is one slide supposed to clear them up? This chapter is that slide, in long form.

## 1.2 Region, Subcontinent, Country: Three Different Words

Ben already knows this idea from his own family. His family's roots are in Sweden and Norway, two separate **countries**, each with its own government, flag and laws. People also talk about "Scandinavia," a **region** that groups those countries (and usually Denmark) because they share some history and culture. Nobody thinks Norway is "part of Sweden" just because both are in Scandinavia.

South Asia works the same way.

- A **country** is a political unit with its own government and borders. India is a country. So is Bangladesh.
- A **region** is a group of places that people choose to talk about together, because of geography, history, culture or politics. Regions don't have governments.
- A **continent** is one of the very large landmasses, like Asia or Africa. A **subcontinent** is a large, distinct part of a continent.

**South Asia** is the region in the south of the Asian continent made up of eight countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka, the members of SAARC (Section 1.3). It's a geographic and political label, and it doesn't put any one country at the center.

The **Indian Subcontinent** is an older, more physical term for the big wedge of land south of the Himalaya that pushes out into the Indian Ocean, between the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. It sits on its own tectonic plate (Section 1.5). Today that wedge is divided mainly among India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, with Nepal and Bhutan along its northern edge and Sri Lanka just off its tip. Afghanistan is usually not counted as part of the subcontinent. In short, "subcontinent" describes the land, and "South Asia" describes a group of countries.

| Term | What kind of thing | What it includes | Who often uses it |
|---|---|---|---|
| South Asia | A region of countries | Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka | Governments, the World Bank, the United Nations, most universities in North America |
| Indian Subcontinent | A physical landmass | The land south of the Himalaya: mainly India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, plus Nepal and Bhutan; Sri Lanka is often included | Geologists, historians, many people in the UK and in South Asian families |
| India | A country | One country, with 28 states and several union territories | Everyone, for that one country |

Why "Indian"? For centuries, outsiders used "India" to mean the whole land around and beyond the Indus River, and historians still sometimes write "India" for the whole subcontinent before 1947. Since 1947, though, India has been the name of one modern country. That's why many people prefer "South Asia" for the region: it doesn't make one country's name stand for everyone.

## 1.3 Eight Countries and SAARC

So which countries count? The answer most governments and researchers use comes from a regional organization.

**SAARC**, the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation, is a group the countries of the region set up to work together on trade, health, education and culture. It was founded in Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh, on 8 December 1985 by seven countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. Afghanistan joined as the eighth member in 2007. SAARC's secretariat (its main office) is in Kathmandu, Nepal. SAARC's leaders last met for a full summit in Nepal in 2014. The next summit, planned for Pakistan in 2016, was postponed and, as of October 2026, has not been held. Even so, "the SAARC countries" is still the most common way to define South Asia.

Here are the eight countries, listed alphabetically. Population numbers are for 2025 from the World Bank.

| Country | Capital | Population (2025, World Bank) | Main language(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Afghanistan | Kabul | 43,844,111 (about 44 million) | Pashto and Dari |
| Bangladesh | Dhaka | 175,686,899 (about 176 million) | Bangla (Bengali) |
| Bhutan | Thimphu | 796,682 (about 800,000) | Dzongkha |
| India | New Delhi | 1,463,865,525 (about 1.46 billion) | Hindi and English are official; 22 languages are listed in the Constitution's Eighth Schedule |
| Maldives | Malé | 529,676 (about 530,000) | Dhivehi |
| Nepal | Kathmandu | 29,618,118 (about 30 million) | Nepali |
| Pakistan | Islamabad | 255,219,554 (about 255 million) | Urdu (national language) and English (official); Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi, Saraiki, Balochi and others are widely spoken |
| Sri Lanka | Sri Jayawardenepura Kotte (legislative) and Colombo (executive) | 21,756,000 (about 22 million) | Sinhala and Tamil |

The sizes are very uneven: India alone has more people than the other seven countries put together. The "main language" column also hides a lot. India's Constitution lists 22 languages in its Eighth Schedule, from Assamese to Urdu, and many more are spoken across the region. You'll meet them in [Chapter 6](../06-languages-and-scripts/index.md). And two of the eight, Sri Lanka and the Maldives, are island countries.

Some lists differ a little. Afghanistan is sometimes grouped with Central Asia instead, and Myanmar or Tibet are occasionally included. This book follows the SAARC list of eight, which is the most widely used.

!!! abstract "Whose Story?"
    Even the name of the region depends on who is talking. "The Indian subcontinent" became common in the British Empire in the early 1900s and is still widely used in Britain and in India, where many writers like it because it points to the culture the region shares. Many people in Pakistan and Bangladesh, which became separate countries in 1947 and 1971, find the phrase uncomfortable because it puts India's name on everyone, and they usually say "South Asia," as historians increasingly do. Afghans may describe their country as part of Central Asia, South Asia or both. Even international bodies differ: since mid-2025 the World Bank groups Afghanistan and Pakistan with the Middle East and North Africa in its regional statistics, though both remain SAARC members.

## 1.4 How Many People Live in South Asia?

The **Population of South Asia** is the total number of people living in its eight countries. Add up the 2025 World Bank numbers from the table and you get about **1.99 billion people** (2025, World Bank). The whole world had about 8.2 billion people that year, so South Asia was home to roughly **one in four people on Earth**.

In 2025 India was the most populous country in the world, ahead of China, and Pakistan and Bangladesh were both among the ten most populous countries on Earth (World Bank).

Here's how the total breaks down.

| Country | Share of South Asia's 2025 population (approximate) |
|---|---|
| India | about 73.5% |
| Pakistan | about 12.8% |
| Bangladesh | about 8.8% |
| Afghanistan | about 2.2% |
| Nepal | about 1.5% |
| Sri Lanka | about 1.1% |
| Bhutan | about 0.04% |
| Maldives | about 0.03% |

!!! mascot-warning "Watch out for this number trap"
    ![Mora with a warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    If you look up "South Asia population" on the World Bank website, you'll get a smaller number than 1.99 billion. That's because the World Bank's own "South Asia" group now leaves out Afghanistan and Pakistan, so always check which countries a total includes.

Why do so many people live here? Part of the answer is history: farming villages and cities have existed in the region for thousands of years (that's [Chapter 2](../02-ancient-roots/index.md)). But a big part of the answer is the land and water you're about to meet. Rich river soil, warm weather and a dependable rainy season make it possible to grow a lot of food, and food supports large populations.

## 1.5 The Mountain Walls: Himalaya and Hindu Kush

Look at any physical map of Asia and you'll see South Asia's northern edge drawn in dark brown. That's a wall of the highest mountains on Earth.

### The Himalaya

The **Himalaya** (हिमालय, *hi-MAA-lay*, "abode of snow") is the mountain range that separates the plains of the subcontinent from the high Tibetan Plateau to the north. The name comes from Sanskrit: *hima* means frost or snow, and *ālaya* means dwelling or home. You'll also see it written "the Himalayas," with an s. Both are correct.

Some key facts:

- The range curves in an arc about 2,400 km (1,500 miles) long, running from west-northwest to east-southeast.
- It holds more than 100 peaks taller than 7,200 m (23,600 ft), including the highest mountain on Earth.
- That mountain is Mount Everest, 8,848.86 m (29,031.7 ft) tall, according to a joint measurement by China and Nepal in 2020. Its summit sits on the border between Nepal and China. In Nepali it is called Sagarmatha.
- The Himalaya spans five countries: Nepal, India, China, Bhutan and Pakistan. So it is not "in" any single country.

How did mountains this tall form? Through a very slow crash. About 80 million years ago, the land that is now the subcontinent was a separate piece of Earth's crust (a tectonic plate) sitting roughly 6,400 km south of Asia. It drifted north. Somewhere between 40 and 50 million years ago, it ran into the Eurasian plate. The crust crumpled and piled upward, and according to the US Geological Survey, the Himalaya is still rising by more than 1 cm a year. That's why it makes sense to think of the subcontinent as a distinct piece of Asia. It really did arrive as its own piece of land.

The mountains matter for people in two big ways. They block the cold air of Central Asia from blowing south, and their snow and glaciers feed the region's largest rivers. Many Himalayan peaks are also sacred in Hindu and Buddhist traditions.

!!! mascot-warning "A disputed area"
    ![Mora with a warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    The western end of the Himalaya runs through Kashmir, a region whose control is disputed among India, Pakistan and China, and each country has its own position on where its borders lie. That's one reason the maps in this book are schematic and never draw official borders.

### The Hindu Kush

West of the Himalaya, another range takes over. The **Hindu Kush** is a mountain range about 800 km (500 miles) long that stretches from central and eastern Afghanistan into northwestern Pakistan, with a small piece in far southeastern Tajikistan. Its highest peak is Tirich Mir, 7,708 m (25,289 ft), in the Chitral District of Pakistan. It is the highest mountain anywhere outside the Himalaya and Karakoram ranges.

The Hindu Kush has been both a wall and a doorway, with routes that carried traders, travelers and armies between Central Asia and South Asia. Buddhism was once widespread in its valleys, and the giant Buddha statues carved into the cliffs at Bamiyan, in Afghanistan, stood until they were destroyed in 2001. Today most people in the region are Muslim.

## 1.6 Three Great Rivers

Mountains collect snow, the snow melts into rivers, and the rivers carry soil down to the plains. That chain is the reason so many people live in South Asia. Three rivers matter most.

### The Indus

The **Indus River** is about 3,180 km (1,980 miles) long. It begins on the Tibetan Plateau in China, flows northwest through the disputed Kashmir region (first through Indian-administered Ladakh, then Pakistani-administered Gilgit-Baltistan), then turns south and runs the full length of Pakistan before emptying into the Arabian Sea near Karachi. Along the way it collects five big tributaries in the Punjab: the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej. The name *Punjab* means "land of five rivers."

Most of Pakistan's farming depends on the Indus. It's also the river that gave the whole subcontinent its outside name. Its old Sanskrit name is Sindhu, and through Old Persian and Greek that name became the English words "Indus" and "India," as well as "Hindustan," "land of the Indus." And it was along the Indus and its neighbors that one of the world's earliest city civilizations grew, a story for Chapter 2.

### The Ganges (Ganga)

The **Ganges (Ganga) River** is about 2,525 km (1,569 miles) long. In India it is called the Ganga (गंगा, *GUNG-gaa*), and in Bangladesh it is called the Padma. It rises in the western Himalaya in the Indian state of Uttarakhand, flows east across the northern plains of India, picks up major tributaries such as the Yamuna and several rivers coming down from Nepal, then crosses into Bangladesh.

Hundreds of millions of people depend on the Ganga, and many big cities stand on it or its connected rivers, including Varanasi, Patna, Kolkata and Dhaka. In Hinduism the Ganga is worshipped as a goddess, and many Hindus bathe in it as an act of devotion. You'll learn more in [Chapter 5](../05-faiths-of-south-asia/index.md).

### The Brahmaputra

The **Brahmaputra River** is about 3,000 km (1,900 miles) long. It begins in Tibet near Mount Kailash, where it's called the Yarlung Tsangpo, and flows east along the north side of the Himalaya. Then it turns and cuts south through the mountains in deep gorges, enters India in the state of Arunachal Pradesh, flows west through the Assam Valley, and turns south into Bangladesh, where it is called the Jamuna. It is a braided river, which means it splits into many shifting channels, and it floods often, especially when Himalayan snow melts.

### Where the rivers meet

In Bangladesh, the Padma (Ganga) and the Jamuna (Brahmaputra) join, then merge with the Meghna River and pour into the Bay of Bengal. Together they built the Ganges Delta, also called the Bengal Delta, which is the largest river delta in the world. It covers most of Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal. At its edge on the sea is the Sundarbans, the largest mangrove forest in the world, shared by Bangladesh and India. The Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna system carries so much water that only two river systems on Earth, the Amazon and the Congo, pour more fresh water into the sea.

That's the land Zara's family comes from: wet, green, flat, crossed by rivers, and shaped by them.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Indus | 3,180 km | Tibet (China) | Kashmir region, Pakistan | Arabian Sea | Sengge Zangbo (in Tibet) |
| Ganges (Ganga) | 2,525 km | Uttarakhand, India | India, Bangladesh | Bay of Bengal | Padma (in Bangladesh) |
| Brahmaputra | 3,000 km | Tibet (China) | China, India, Bangladesh | Bay of Bengal (through the delta) | Yarlung Tsangpo, Jamuna |

## 1.7 Plains, Plateau, Ghats and Desert

Between the mountains and the sea, the land changes a lot. Four landforms explain most of the map.

### The Indo-Gangetic Plain

The **Indo-Gangetic Plain** is the huge, flat, fertile lowland that stretches across the north of the subcontinent, from Pakistan in the west, through northern India and southern Nepal, to almost all of Bangladesh in the east. It covers about 700,000 square km (270,000 square miles). It's named for the two river systems that drain it, the Indus and the Ganga.

The rivers built the plain themselves. Flood after flood, they spread fine mud called **alluvium**, so the plain is one of the largest unbroken stretches of river soil anywhere. It's among the most densely populated and most intensely farmed places on Earth, home to many of the region's biggest cities: Lahore, Delhi, Lucknow, Patna, Kolkata and Dhaka all sit on or near it.

### The Deccan Plateau

South of the plain, the land rises. The **Deccan Plateau** is a large raised tableland that covers much of central and southern India, about 422,000 square km. Its average height is about 600 m (2,000 ft). The name Deccan comes from the Sanskrit word दक्षिण (*DUK-shin*), "south."

The plateau tilts gently from west to east, so most of its big rivers, such as the Godavari, the Krishna and the Kaveri, flow east and empty into the Bay of Bengal. The northwest part of the plateau is made of thick layers of dark volcanic rock called the Deccan Traps, laid down by enormous lava flows about 66 million years ago. Much of the land there has dark black soil. Because mountains on its western edge block rain, much of the Deccan is drier than the coasts, with a semi-arid climate.

### The Western and Eastern Ghats

The **Western and Eastern Ghats** are the two mountain ranges that frame the Deccan Plateau, one along each coast of peninsular India. *Ghat* comes from the Hindi word घाट (*GHAAT*), which means a flight of steps leading down to a river, or a mountain pass. These ranges are like giant steps from the plateau down to the sea.

- The **Western Ghats** run about 1,600 km along India's west coast, from the state of Gujarat south through Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka and Kerala to Tamil Nadu. They form an almost continuous wall, with peaks over 2,000 m. The highest is Anamudi, 2,695 m. They catch the rain-bearing monsoon winds coming off the Arabian Sea, so their western slopes and the coast below them get very heavy rain, while the land behind them stays drier. The Western Ghats are also famous for their forests and wildlife.
- The **Eastern Ghats** run about 1,750 km along the east coast, from Odisha through Andhra Pradesh and Telangana to Tamil Nadu. Unlike the Western Ghats, they are broken into separate hills, with many gaps where rivers cut through to the Bay of Bengal. They are lower, about 600 m on average, so they affect the weather less.

The two ranges meet in the Nilgiri Hills in the far south.

### The Thar Desert

In the northwest, where India meets Pakistan, the land turns to sand. The **Thar Desert**, also called the Great Indian Desert, is a dry region of sandy plains and low hills. About 85 percent of it lies in India, mostly in the state of Rajasthan, and the rest lies in Pakistan's Sindh and Punjab provinces. It gets most of its little rain from the summer monsoon, usually less than 500 mm (20 inches) a year. Summers are extremely hot and winters are cooler.

The Thar is a good reminder that South Asia isn't one climate. People have lived in the Thar for a very long time, getting by with herding and careful water storage, and the desert has its own traditions of music and craft that you'll meet in later chapters.

A quick way to remember the layout: walk south from Tibet and you step down from the Himalaya to the Indo-Gangetic Plain, up onto the Deccan Plateau, then down the Ghats to the coast, with desert to the northwest and delta to the northeast.

## 1.8 The Monsoon

Ask South Asian grandparents about the weather back home, and there's a good chance the first word they say is "monsoon." It's the most important weather pattern in the region.

### What the word means

The English word monsoon comes from the Arabic word موسم (*MAW-sim*), *mawsim*, which means "season." Sailors crossing the Arabian Sea between Africa, Arabia and India learned long ago that the winds blew from the northeast for half the year and from the southwest for the rest, and they used that pattern for their voyages. Ocean trade depended on it, as you'll see in [Chapter 3](../03-empires-and-encounters/index.md).

So a **monsoon** is, first of all, a wind that reverses direction with the seasons. The India Meteorological Department (IMD) describes it as winds along the Indian Ocean coasts that blow from the southwest for about half the year and from the northeast for the other half. In everyday speech, though, "the monsoon" usually means the rainy season that the southwest winds bring in summer. In Bangla, Zara's Nanu calls that season বর্ষা (*BOR-sha*), *borsha*, which means both "rain" and "the rainy season."

### How the summer monsoon works

You can feel the basic idea at a beach. On a hot day the sand gets hot fast, but the water stays cooler, because land heats up faster than water.

1. In spring and early summer, the huge landmass of South Asia and the rest of Asia heats up much faster than the Indian Ocean.
2. Hot air over the land rises, which lowers the air pressure there.
3. Cooler, moist air from over the ocean flows in toward the land to fill the gap. In South Asia that wind blows from the southwest.
4. As the moist air is pushed up over hills and mountains, such as the Western Ghats and later the Himalaya, it cools, and its water falls as rain.
5. In winter the pattern flips. The land cools faster than the sea, and cool, dry winds blow outward from the land, from the northeast.

The Himalaya matters here too. It acts like a wall that stops the monsoon clouds from simply drifting away north, so they drop their rain on the plains and foothills.

### When it arrives

The southwest monsoon arrives on a fairly regular schedule. According to the IMD, it normally reaches the state of Kerala, at India's southwest tip, around **1 June**, then moves north in surges and covers the whole of India around **8 July**. Rain moves up the west coast from the Arabian Sea, while low-pressure systems that form over the Bay of Bengal carry heavy rain westward across the land. The monsoon begins to pull back from northwest India in September and has usually withdrawn from the country by October. Those are average dates, though. In any real year the monsoon can come early or late, and people watch the forecasts closely.

The amount of rain changes a lot from place to place. Mawsynram and Cherrapunji, two towns in the hills of the Indian state of Meghalaya, just north of Bangladesh, trade places as the wettest spots on Earth. Meanwhile, the Thar Desert gets only a little. And Afghanistan sits mostly outside the monsoon zone altogether; most of its rain and snow falls in winter and spring.

Not all monsoon rain comes in summer, either. In the southeast of India, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh get rain from the northeast monsoon during the winter months, after the summer monsoon has left, when winds crossing the Bay of Bengal pick up moisture on the way.

!!! mascot-tip "It doesn't rain all day"
    ![Mora giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    "Monsoon season" doesn't mean nonstop rain. The monsoon comes in active spells of heavy rain and "break" spells of days with little rain, and many monsoon days mix sun, sticky heat and a big afternoon downpour.

### Why it matters so much

The southwest monsoon brings most of the year's rain to most of India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. Many farms in the region don't have large irrigation systems, so they depend on the monsoon to water their crops, especially rice, and to refill wells and underground water for the rest of the year. A late or weak monsoon can hurt harvests and the whole economy. A very strong one can cause floods and landslides. The monsoon also feeds the rivers that power hydroelectric dams.

The monsoon shows up in songs, poems, foods and festivals too, and in a lot of family memories of the first rain of the season.

## 1.9 Water All Around: The Bay of Bengal and the Indian Ocean

South Asia's southern half is a peninsula, which means water on three sides. Two big bodies of water shape the coasts.

The **Bay of Bengal** is the northeastern part of the Indian Ocean, lying east of India and south of Bangladesh, with Myanmar on its eastern side and Sri Lanka at its southwestern edge. It covers about 2.2 million square km (about 840,000 square miles). Many of the subcontinent's biggest rivers flow into it, including the Ganga, the Brahmaputra, the Godavari, the Krishna and the Kaveri.

The bay's coasts include the Sundarbans mangrove forest and some of the longest natural beaches in the world, such as Cox's Bazar in Bangladesh and Marina Beach in Chennai, India. Severe cyclones also form over the bay, which is one reason coastal Bangladesh and eastern India plan so carefully for storms. Chattogram, where Zara's parents grew up, is Bangladesh's busiest port, right on the bay.

The **Indian Ocean** is the third-largest of the world's five oceans, covering about 70.6 million square km, or about 20% of the water on Earth's surface. It has Asia to the north, Africa to the west and Australia to the east. It's also the warmest ocean, and monsoons shape the climate all around it. The Arabian Sea, to the west of India and south of Pakistan, and the Bay of Bengal are both arms of it.

Two of South Asia's countries sit out in this ocean:

- **Sri Lanka** is a large island just south of India's tip, mostly flat to rolling coastal plains with mountains rising in the south-central part.
- **The Maldives** is a chain of 1,192 small coral islands grouped into 26 atolls (rings of coral reef around a lagoon), stretched across the equator southwest of India and Sri Lanka. More than 80% of its islands sit less than 1 m above sea level, which makes the Maldives the lowest-lying country in the world and very vulnerable to rising seas.

For thousands of years the Indian Ocean worked more like a highway than a barrier, carrying ships, goods, faiths and languages between South Asia, Arabia, East Africa and Southeast Asia. Arab and Persian traders helped bring Islam to the Maldives in the 12th century.

## 1.10 Putting It Together: Regional Diversity

**Regional Diversity** is the idea that South Asia is not one place with one climate, one language, one faith or one way of life, but many different places shaped by different land, water and history. The geography you've just read about is one of the biggest reasons for that diversity.

Here's how different everyday life can be, depending on where you stand:

| Place | Country | Landscape | What shapes daily life |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kabul | Afghanistan | High valley ringed by mountains | Most rain and snow from December to April; dry summers; little monsoon rain |
| Lahore | Pakistan | Indo-Gangetic Plain, Punjab | Hot summers, monsoon rain from June to September, farming on river-fed land |
| Kathmandu | Nepal | Valley in the Himalayan foothills | Summer monsoon rains, mountains all around |
| Chattogram | Bangladesh | Coast of the Bay of Bengal, near the delta | Heavy monsoon rain, rivers and floods, port life, cyclone risk |
| Jaisalmer | India | Thar Desert | Little rain, extremely hot summers, careful water use |
| Kandy | Sri Lanka | Central highlands | Cooler than the coast, green and rainy |
| Malé | Maldives | Low coral island | Ocean on every side; fishing and tourism; sea-level rise |

Each of those places has its own foods, clothes, building styles and festivals, and the land is one big reason why. In the chapters ahead, every time you meet a tradition, try asking: what about the land and weather made this make sense?

Geography isn't the only reason. History, trade, migration, faith and language add their own layers, as the rest of this book shows.

## Common Misconceptions

**Myth:** South Asia is just another name for India.  
**Reality:** South Asia is a region of eight countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. India is the largest by far, but Bangladesh, Pakistan and the others are separate, independent countries with their own governments, languages and histories.

**Myth:** The Himalaya is in Nepal (or in India).  
**Reality:** The Himalaya stretches about 2,400 km across five countries: Pakistan, India, Nepal, Bhutan and China. Mount Everest itself sits on the Nepal-China border.

**Myth:** Monsoon means it rains all day, every day for months.  
**Reality:** A monsoon is a seasonal shift in wind direction. The summer monsoon brings a rainy season, but rain comes in active spells and break spells, and many monsoon days include sun and heat between heavy showers.

**Myth:** All of South Asia is hot all the time.  
**Reality:** The region includes snowy Himalayan peaks, cold, dry high valleys on the northern side of the western Himalaya, and Sri Lanka's central highlands, where frost can occur on some winter nights, as well as hot plains, deserts and tropical coasts. Climate depends a lot on height and distance from the sea.

**Myth:** The "Indian subcontinent" and "India" mean the same thing.  
**Reality:** "Indian subcontinent" is a geographic term for the landmass south of the Himalaya, which today includes several countries. "India" is the name of one modern country since 1947. That's why many people prefer the term "South Asia" for the region.

## Check Yourself

??? question "1. Name the eight countries of South Asia."
    Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. They are the eight members of SAARC.

??? question "2. What is the difference between a region and a country?"
    A country has its own government, borders and laws. A region is a group of places people talk about together because of geography, history or culture; it has no government of its own.

??? question "3. About how many people lived in South Asia in 2025, and what share of the world is that?"
    About 1.99 billion people (2025, World Bank), roughly one in four people on Earth.

??? question "4. Which two rivers join in Bangladesh to form the world's largest delta, and what are they called there?"
    The Ganga (called the Padma in Bangladesh) and the Brahmaputra (called the Jamuna). They join and flow with the Meghna into the Bay of Bengal.

??? question "5. Why does the summer monsoon blow from the southwest?"
    In early summer the land heats up faster than the Indian Ocean. Hot air rises over the land, and cooler, moist air from the ocean to the southwest flows in to replace it, bringing rain.

    ??? note "Answer"
        SAARC was founded in Dhaka on 8 December 1985 by seven countries. Afghanistan joined as the eighth member in 2007.

### Understand

3. Explain in your own words why the land south of the Himalaya can be called a "subcontinent."

    ??? note "Answer"
        The land that is now the subcontinent sits on its own tectonic plate. It drifted north for millions of years and collided with Asia between about 40 and 50 million years ago, pushing up the Himalaya. It is a distinct piece of the Asian continent.

4. Explain why the Deccan Plateau is drier than the coast of Kerala, even though both are in southern India.

    ??? note "Answer"
        The Western Ghats stand between the Arabian Sea and the plateau. Monsoon winds rise over the Ghats and drop much of their rain on the western slopes and the coast, so the plateau behind them gets less.

## Key Takeaways

- South Asia is a region of eight countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka, the members of SAARC.
- "Indian Subcontinent" describes the landmass south of the Himalaya; "South Asia" describes the group of countries. Many people prefer "South Asia" because it doesn't center one country.
- About 1.99 billion people lived in South Asia in 2025 (World Bank), roughly one in four people on Earth.
- The Himalaya and Hindu Kush form the region's northern mountain walls. The Himalaya spans five countries and was pushed up when the subcontinent's plate collided with Asia.
- The Indus, the Ganges (Ganga) and the Brahmaputra carry snowmelt and soil to the plains; the Ganga and Brahmaputra build the world's largest delta in Bangladesh and India.
- The southwest monsoon normally reaches Kerala around 1 June and covers India by about 8 July, bringing most of the year's rain to much of the region, but not to most of Afghanistan.
- Mountains, plains, plateau, desert, delta and islands help explain South Asia's regional diversity in food, dress, buildings and ways of life.
