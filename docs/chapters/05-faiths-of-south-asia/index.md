# Chapter 5: Faiths of South Asia

## Summary

South Asia is home to many of the world's faiths. Four of them, Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism, began in the region, and others, including Islam, Christianity, Zoroastrianism and Judaism, arrived long ago and put down deep roots. This chapter introduces each faith through the same five lenses: where it came from, its sacred texts, its place of worship, a major festival, and a core idea. It also looks at pilgrimage, at the places and practices that different faiths share, and at religious pluralism, the fact of many faiths living side by side, with all its friendships and tensions. The goal is understanding, not ranking: you'll learn how followers describe their own beliefs, in their own words where possible.

## 5.1 Shoes Off, Three Times

On a Saturday in October, the Prairie Ridge South Asian Student Association goes on a field trip. Ms. Okafor has arranged three visits in the Twin Cities: a mosque in the morning, a gurdwara at lunchtime, and a mandir (a Hindu temple) in the afternoon. Zara Hossain's family prays at the mosque. Simran Kaur Gill's family goes to the gurdwara every Sunday. Arjun Reddy's grandmother volunteers at the mandir.

At the mosque, Zara shows Ben Lindqvist the shelves by the door, and everyone leaves their shoes there before entering the prayer hall. At the gurdwara, Simran hands Ben an orange scarf to cover his head, and again, shoes come off. Then they all sit in rows on the floor and eat dal, roti and kheer for free, side by side with strangers. At the mandir, Arjun points to a long rack of sneakers and sandals. Ben laughs. "Okay, three buildings, three religions, and I've taken my shoes off three times. So are these faiths basically the same, or totally different?"

Simran shakes her head. "Not the same. But you can't understand any of them from the outside in five minutes." On the bus home, Ben keeps asking questions. How do you learn about many faiths without mixing them up, and without deciding one is better than another?

## 5.2 Five Lenses, No Ranking

Ben already knows one faith from the inside. His family is Lutheran, a kind of Christian. If a friend asked him about it, he could answer a few basic questions: Where did it start? What book do you read? Where do you go to worship? What's your biggest holiday? What's one idea that matters most? Those answers wouldn't be the whole of his faith, but they'd be a fair start.

This chapter uses those same five questions, or **lenses**, for every faith:

1. **Origins:** where and when it began, and who followers say founded or taught it.
2. **Sacred texts:** the writings it treasures.
3. **Place of worship:** the building where people gather.
4. **A major festival:** one celebration among many.
5. **A core idea:** one teaching that followers often describe as central.

Using the same lenses for every faith keeps the comparison fair. It also comes with three ground rules that this whole book follows:

- **Describe, don't judge.** We say "many Hindus believe" or "in Sikh tradition," because these are the beliefs of followers, not claims the book is making.
- **No ranking.** Older doesn't mean better, and bigger doesn't mean more important.
- **Practice varies.** Inside every faith there are many communities, schools and family customs. When you read "Muslims do X," remember that real families are more varied than any summary.

!!! mascot-warning "Ask before assuming"
    ![Mora with a warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A person's name, clothes or country don't tell you their faith, or how they practice it. If you're curious, ask politely, and accept "I'd rather not say" as a full answer.

## 5.4 Hinduism

### Origins and variety

**Hinduism** is a family of related traditions, beliefs and practices that grew in South Asia over thousands of years, with no single founder, no single holy book and no single central authority. Most Hindus live in India and Nepal, the two Hindu-majority countries of South Asia. There are also large Hindu communities in Bangladesh, in Sri Lanka (mostly among Tamils in the north and east), in Pakistan (mostly in Sindh), in Bhutan, and around the world.

The word "Hindu" was first used by outsiders, from the name of the Indus River (Sindhu). Many Hindus also call their tradition *Sanatana Dharma*, "the eternal way." Inside that family are many traditions. Some Hindus focus their devotion on Vishnu (often as Krishna or Rama), some on Shiva, and some on the Goddess (Devi) in forms such as Durga or Kali. Many worship several deities, and many describe them as forms of one divine reality. In Pew Research Center's 2021 survey of India, 84% of Hindus who believe in God either picked more than one god as feeling close to them or said they have many personal gods, and 61% said there is one God with many manifestations.

### Sacred texts

Hinduism has many sacred texts, not one. The oldest are the **Vedas**, hymns in Sanskrit passed down by memory for many centuries. The **Upanishads** explore questions about the self and ultimate reality. The great epics, the **Ramayana** and the **Mahabharata** (which includes the **Bhagavad Gita**), are known to almost everyone through stories, TV shows and festivals. The **Puranas** and devotional poems in Tamil, Bengali, Hindi and many other languages matter too. Different communities value different texts most.

### Three key ideas

**Dharma** (धर्म, *DHUR-muh*) is the idea of right order, duty and right conduct: the way things should be, and the way a person should act. In Hindi, *dharm* can also simply mean "religion." Many Hindus think of their own dharma as depending on their stage of life and responsibilities, such as being a student, a parent or a teacher.

**Karma and Rebirth** is the belief that a person's actions and intentions (karma, कर्म, *KUR-muh*, "action") have effects, in this life and in future lives, and that the soul is reborn again and again in a cycle called *samsara*. In many Hindu traditions, the highest goal is *moksha*, release from that cycle. Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs also speak of karma and rebirth, each in their own way. In the 2021 Pew survey of India, 77% of Hindus and 77% of Muslims said they believe in karma, a sign of how widely the idea has spread across faith lines.

**Puja** (पूजा, *POO-jah*, "worship" or "honor") is the Hindu ritual of offering worship to a deity, often with light, flowers, water, incense or food. Puja can be a big temple ceremony with priests, or a few quiet minutes at a home shrine in the morning. Arjun's grandmother does a short puja every day at the small shrine in their kitchen.

### Places and festivals

Hindus worship at a mandir (मंदिर, *MUN-dir*, "temple"), and many families also keep a home shrine. Big festivals include Diwali, the festival of lights; Holi, the spring festival of colors; Navaratri and Durga Puja in autumn; and harvest festivals such as Pongal. In Bangladesh, Durga Puja is the largest Hindu festival, and Dhaka's Dhakeshwari National Temple is dedicated to the goddess many say gave the city its name. In Pakistan, the Hinglaj Mata temple in Balochistan draws the country's largest Hindu pilgrimage. You'll meet the festivals in [Chapter 7](../07-festivals/index.md).

## 5.5 Buddhism

**Buddhism** is the faith based on the teachings of Siddhartha Gautama, called the Buddha ("awakened one"), who Buddhists believe found the path to freedom from suffering. He was born at Lumbini, in present-day Nepal, about 2,500 years ago (the exact dates are debated). Buddhists believe he reached enlightenment while meditating under a tree at Bodh Gaya, in present-day India. Both Lumbini and the Mahabodhi Temple at Bodh Gaya are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

In Buddhist teaching, the Buddha described **Four Noble Truths**: life involves suffering and dissatisfaction; suffering has a cause, especially craving; suffering can end; and the **Eightfold Path** of right understanding, ethical living and meditation leads to that end. Buddhists take refuge in the "Three Jewels": the Buddha, his teaching (the Dharma), and the community of monks, nuns and followers (the Sangha).

Buddhism spread from South Asia across Asia and developed different schools. In India itself, many Buddhists today are Dalits whose families followed the reformer B. R. Ambedkar into Buddhism after his conversion at Nagpur in 1956 (you met him in [Chapter 4](../04-colonialism-and-partition/index.md)). Two schools matter most in South Asia today.

### Theravada and Vajrayana

**Theravada and Vajrayana** are two of the main schools of Buddhism. Theravada ("teaching of the elders") is the school of Sri Lanka, and its scriptures are the **Pali Canon**, preserved in the Pali language. Buddhism reached Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE, and the island has been a center of Theravada learning ever since. Vajrayana (the "diamond" or "thunderbolt" vehicle) developed later and became the Buddhism of Tibet, Bhutan and Himalayan Nepal. It uses Mahayana scriptures and Tibetan teachings, and is known for its rituals, chanted mantras and the close bond between students and their teachers.

| | Theravada | Vajrayana |
|---|---|---|
| Where in South Asia | Sri Lanka; also most Buddhists in Bangladesh | Bhutan, Himalayan Nepal, Ladakh and other Himalayan areas |
| Scriptures | Pali Canon | Mahayana sutras and Tibetan Buddhist texts |
| Typical place | A vihara (monastery temple) with a stupa | A monastery; in Bhutan, many sit inside dzongs (fortress-like centers) |
| Example | Dilan Perera's family in Kandy visits the Temple of the Tooth Relic | Nima Sherpa's family follows the Nyingma school of Tibetan Buddhism |

The big Buddhist festival in Sri Lanka is Vesak (Sinhala වෙසක්, *VEH-suk*), held on the full moon of the month of Vesak, usually in May. In Theravada tradition it marks the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and passing. Streets and homes glow with lanterns called Vesak kudu, and volunteers set up stalls called dansalas that hand out free food and drinks to passersby. In Bangladesh, Buddhists are a small minority (0.61% in 2022), and most of them live in Chattogram Division, where Zara's parents grew up, especially in the Chittagong Hill Tracts to the east of the city.

## 5.6 Jainism and Ahimsa

**Jainism** is a faith from South Asia that teaches that every living being has a soul, and that souls can be freed from the cycle of rebirth through non-violence, self-discipline and right knowledge. Jains believe their path has been taught by 24 *tirthankaras* ("ford-makers," teachers who help people cross the river of rebirth). The 24th, **Mahavira**, lived in what is now Bihar, India, around the 6th or 5th century BCE, close to the time of the Buddha.

Jains belong to two main groups, Digambara and Svetambara, which differ in some practices and in which collections of scripture they accept. In India, Jains live in many states, with especially large communities in Maharashtra and Gujarat. Jain temples, often carved in white marble, are famous for their detail. Jain merchant families have long been prominent in trade, and Jain families have supported temples, libraries and charities for centuries.

### Ahimsa

**Ahimsa** (अहिंसा, *uh-HIM-sah*) means non-violence: not harming any living being, in action, word or thought. Hindus and Buddhists also value ahimsa, but Jains put it at the very center of their life. Many Jains follow a strict vegetarian diet that also leaves out root vegetables such as potatoes, onions and garlic, to avoid destroying the many tiny living things in and around them. In the 20th century, Mohandas K. Gandhi made ahimsa famous worldwide as a method of peaceful protest.

The most important Jain festival is **Paryushan**, a period of fasting, reflection and study, eight days long for Svetambara Jains. It ends with a day of asking forgiveness from everyone you may have hurt in the past year. Jains also celebrate Diwali, which in Jain tradition marks the anniversary of Mahavira's final liberation.

## 5.7 Islam in South Asia

### Origins and basic practice

Islam began in Arabia in the early 600s CE. Muslims believe that God (Allah, the Arabic word for God) revealed the **Quran** to the Prophet Muhammad, and they also follow the **Hadith**, reports of what the Prophet said and did. Muslims around the world share the **Five Pillars** of practice: declaring faith in one God and in Muhammad as God's messenger (*shahada*), praying five times a day (*salah*, called *namaz* in South Asia), giving to those in need (*zakat*), fasting from dawn to dusk in the month of Ramadan (*sawm*), and, for those who are able, making the pilgrimage to Mecca (*hajj*).

**Islam in South Asia** is the faith and culture of the region's Muslims, who today form one of the largest Muslim populations on Earth. As you read in [Chapter 3](../03-empires-and-encounters/index.md), Islam arrived over many centuries, through Arab traders on the coasts, Sufi teachers and conquest in the northwest. Most South Asian Muslims are descended from people who lived in the region long before. Pakistan, Bangladesh, Afghanistan and the Maldives are Muslim-majority countries. India had about 172 million Muslims in 2011, one of the largest Muslim populations in the world, and Sri Lanka and Nepal have significant Muslim communities too.

A Muslim place of worship is a masjid (Urdu مسجد, *MUS-jid*), or mosque. Many South Asian Muslims also visit Sufi shrines (dargahs), though some Muslims, especially in reformist movements, disapprove of some shrine practices. One of the largest in South Asia is Data Darbar in Lahore, the shrine of the 11th-century saint Ali al-Hujwiri. In the 2021 Pew survey, 37% of Muslims in North India said they identify with Sufism.

Zara's family celebrates two big festivals: **Eid al-Fitr**, which ends the month of Ramadan fasting, and **Eid al-Adha**, which comes at the time of the hajj. On Eid morning her family goes to prayer in new clothes, and her Nanu hands out money to the children.

### Sunni and Shia Islam

**Sunni and Shia Islam** are the two main branches of Islam, which separated over a disagreement about who should lead the Muslim community after the Prophet Muhammad died in 632. Sunni Muslims hold that the community rightly chose the Prophet's companion Abu Bakr as the first leader (caliph). Shia Muslims hold that the Prophet named his cousin and son-in-law, Ali, as his successor, and they honor a line of leaders called imams descended from Ali.

Both branches share the Quran, the Five Pillars and most beliefs, and in many South Asian towns they have lived side by side for centuries. Most South Asian Muslims are Sunni. Shia Muslims are an important minority: Pew Research Center has estimated them at about 10% to 15% of Muslims in both India and Pakistan (2009 estimate), and some scholars estimate more. During the month of Muharram, many Shia Muslims mourn the death of Imam Husain, the Prophet's grandson, at the Battle of Karbala in 680, with processions, poetry and gatherings.

## 5.8 Sikhism

**Sikhism** is a faith founded in Punjab by Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539), which teaches devotion to one God, honest work, sharing with others, and the equality of all people. You met Guru Nanak in [Chapter 3](../03-empires-and-encounters/index.md). In Sikh tradition, nine more human Gurus followed him. The tenth, **Guru Gobind Singh**, created the **Khalsa**, the community of initiated Sikhs, on Vaisakhi in 1699. Initiated Sikhs keep five articles of faith, including uncut hair (often covered by a turban) and a steel bracelet called a kara.

Sikhs believe in one God, expressed in the phrase **Ik Onkar**, "one creator," the opening words of their scripture. Most Sikhs live in Punjab in India, where they are the majority. Some of Sikhism's holiest places, including Nankana Sahib, Guru Nanak's birthplace, and Kartarpur, where he died, are in Pakistan. In 2019 a visa-free corridor opened so pilgrims from India could cross to the gurdwara at Kartarpur. India suspended it in May 2025 amid tensions between the two countries. It was still closed to Indian pilgrims at its sixth anniversary in November 2025, although Pakistan says its side is open.

### The Guru Granth Sahib

The **Guru Granth Sahib** is the Sikh scripture, a collection of hymns that Sikhs honor as their living Guru. The fifth Guru, Guru Arjan, compiled its first version in 1604 and placed it in the Golden Temple at Amritsar. In October 1708, shortly before he died, Guru Gobind Singh declared that after him the scripture itself would be the Guru. It has 1,430 pages, and its hymns are meant to be sung. Most are by the Sikh Gurus, but it also includes hymns by fifteen other saints (bhagats), including bhakti poets such as Kabir and Namdev and the Muslim Sufi saint Sheikh Farid.

In a gurdwara (ਗੁਰਦੁਆਰਾ, *GUR-dwah-rah*, "door to the Guru"), the Guru Granth Sahib sits on a raised platform under a canopy. Visitors remove their shoes and cover their heads, and they usually sit on the floor. The holiest gurdwara is the Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) in Amritsar, which receives about 100,000 visitors on a typical day.

### Langar

**Langar** (ਲੰਗਰ, *LUN-gur*) is the free community kitchen of a gurdwara, where volunteers cook and serve a vegetarian meal to anyone who comes, regardless of religion, caste, gender or wealth. Everyone sits together on the floor, in rows, as equals. Sikh tradition traces langar to Guru Nanak, and it shows his teaching that all people are equal. A Sikh tradition even says that the Mughal emperor Akbar sat and ate langar with ordinary people before meeting the third Guru, Guru Amar Das.

The cooking, serving and cleaning are done as *seva* (ਸੇਵਾ, *SAY-vah*), selfless service. Simran has helped make roti for langar since she was eight, and she's the reason Ben now knows how to hold out both hands to receive it.

## 5.9 Christianity in South Asia

**Christianity in South Asia** refers to the region's many Christian communities, some among the oldest in the world, others formed later through Portuguese, Dutch, British and other missions. Christians believe that Jesus is the Son of God, and they read the **Bible**. Their place of worship is a church, and their biggest festivals are Christmas and Easter.

- **Kerala's St. Thomas Christians** trace their church to the apostle Thomas, who, according to their tradition, came to the Malabar coast in the 1st century CE. They speak Malayalam and have their own ancient forms of worship.
- **Goa**, ruled by Portugal from 1510 until 1961, has many Catholic churches. Its old churches, including the Basilica of Bom Jesus, which holds the tomb of St. Francis Xavier, are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **Northeast India:** Christians are the majority in the states of Nagaland, Mizoram and Meghalaya.
- **Pakistan:** the 2023 census counted about 3.3 million Christians (1.37%), most of them in Punjab. Some church leaders say the real number is higher.
- **Sri Lanka:** about 6.9% of people were Christian in the 2024 census, most of them Roman Catholic, among Sinhala, Tamil and Burgher communities.

In India as a whole, Christians were 2.3% of the population in 2011, about 28 million people. South Asian churches celebrate Christmas with local touches: star lanterns, carols in regional languages and festive sweets.

## 5.10 Smaller Communities, Long Histories

Two communities in South Asia are small in number but have long, important histories.

### Zoroastrians (Parsis)

**Zoroastrians (Parsis)** are followers of Zoroastrianism, an ancient faith founded by the prophet Zoroaster (Zarathustra) in Iran. In South Asia, most belong to the Parsi community, whose ancestors came from Iran. *Parsi* means "Persian." According to the community's own story, the *Qissa-i Sanjan*, Zoroastrians left Iran to keep practicing their faith and landed on the coast of Gujarat. Historians place the migration somewhere between the 8th and 10th centuries.

In the story, the local king worried that his kingdom was already full. A Parsi priest answered by stirring sugar into a vessel of milk: like the sugar, the newcomers would blend in and sweeten life without making it overflow. Parsis often tell this story to explain how they settled in.

Zoroastrians teach the ideals of good thoughts, good words and good deeds, and believe each person chooses between good and evil. They pray in fire temples, where a sacred fire is kept burning. The Iranshah fire at Udvada, Gujarat, is the most revered in India, and tradition dates it to the community's earliest years. Parsis celebrate Navroz, the New Year. Most Parsis in South Asia live in Mumbai (formerly Bombay), and there are smaller communities in Karachi and other cities.

### Jewish Communities

**Jewish Communities** in South Asia are the old Jewish groups that settled in India, especially on the west coast, long before modern times. Jews believe in one God and a covenant (a sacred agreement) between God and the Jewish people, and they study the Torah. Their place of worship is a synagogue.

There were three main communities:

- **Cochin Jews** in Kerala. The Paradesi Synagogue in Kochi, built in 1568, is the oldest active synagogue in the Commonwealth.
- **Bene Israel**, who lived for centuries in villages of the Konkan coast and later moved to Mumbai, where they opened their first synagogue in 1796.
- **Baghdadi Jews**, traders from the Middle East who settled first in Surat (from 1730) and later in Mumbai and Kolkata.

India's Jewish population peaked in the 1940s and declined quickly after 1948, as most moved to the new state of Israel. Most Cochin Jews left in the mid-1950s. Small communities remain, and their synagogues are still cared for.

## 5.11 Pilgrimage: Journeys of Faith

**Pilgrimage** is a journey to a sacred place for prayer, blessing, remembrance or renewal. Every faith in this chapter has pilgrimage places, and South Asia is full of them.

| Faith | Pilgrimage example | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Hinduism | The Kumbh Mela, where millions bathe in a sacred river. It rotates among four cities | Prayagraj (Allahabad), Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik, India |
| Hinduism | Hinglaj Yatra | Balochistan, Pakistan |
| Buddhism | The Buddha's birthplace and place of enlightenment | Lumbini, Nepal; Bodh Gaya, India |
| Buddhism | The Temple of the Tooth Relic | Kandy, Sri Lanka |
| Islam | The hajj to Mecca, plus visits to Sufi shrines such as Ajmer and Data Darbar | Saudi Arabia; Rajasthan, India; Lahore, Pakistan |
| Sikhism | The Golden Temple; Nankana Sahib and Kartarpur | Amritsar, India; Punjab, Pakistan |
| Christianity | The Basilica of Bom Jesus | Old Goa, India |
| Zoroastrianism | The Iranshah fire temple | Udvada, Gujarat, India |

UNESCO lists the Kumbh Mela as part of humanity's Intangible Cultural Heritage (2017) and calls it the largest peaceful gathering of pilgrims on Earth.

Some pilgrimage places are shared. Adam's Peak (Sri Pada) in Sri Lanka is a 2,243 m mountain with a footprint-shaped rock at the top. In Buddhist tradition it is the Buddha's footprint; many Hindus see it as Shiva's; and in some Muslim and Christian traditions it is the footprint of Adam or of St. Thomas. Pilgrims of all four faiths climb it, often through the night to watch the sunrise. The Ajmer dargah of Moinuddin Chishti, too, draws many Hindus as well as Muslims.

## 5.12 Religious Pluralism

**Religious Pluralism** means many faiths living side by side in the same society, and, as an ideal, people of different faiths respecting each other's right to believe and practice. South Asia has lived with pluralism for thousands of years. That history includes deep friendships and shared traditions, and also real conflict and discrimination.

Some signs of shared life:

- **Shared ideas.** In Pew's 2021 survey of India, the same share of Hindus and Muslims (77%) said they believe in karma.
- **Shared places.** Hindus visit Sufi dargahs; Buddhists, Hindus, Muslims and Christians climb Adam's Peak; anyone may eat at a gurdwara's langar.
- **Shared scripture.** The Guru Granth Sahib includes hymns by Hindu and Muslim saints.
- **Shared values.** In the same survey, most Indians of every major faith said respecting all religions is very important to being truly Indian.

But the same Pew report was titled "Tolerance and Segregation." It found that many people in India, while valuing respect for other faiths, said their close friends came mostly from their own group, and large majorities of both Hindus and Muslims said it was very important to stop members of their community from marrying outside the faith. Across the region, religious minorities have at times faced discrimination and violence, and communal violence has caused terrible suffering on all sides, as [Chapter 4](../04-colonialism-and-partition/index.md) shows for Partition.

!!! abstract "Whose Story?"
    Who gets to define a faith? Many Hindus prefer the name Sanatana Dharma to "Hinduism," a label that grew from an outsider's word. Many Sikhs object when textbooks call Sikhism a "mix" of Hinduism and Islam, because in Sikh understanding it is a distinct revelation through Guru Nanak. In Pakistan, some church leaders say the 2023 census undercounted Christians, whom it counted at 1.37% of the population. Ahmadis describe themselves as Muslims, but most other Muslims reject their belief that their founder, Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, was a prophet. Since 1974, Pakistan's constitution has classified them as non-Muslims, so the same people are counted differently depending on who is counting. That's why this book describes each faith the way its own followers usually describe it, and notes where counts are disputed.

The sim opens in Compare mode with Sikhism and Islam side by side through the Place of worship lens. Start there, then switch the lens to Sacred texts, A major festival and A core idea, and read both cards each time. Then tap different tiles to compare other pairs: Hinduism and Jainism on "A core idea," or Buddhism and Christianity on "Origins."

As you compare, notice what is similar and what is different, and notice the note under the tiles: the order is alphabetical and every tile is the same size. Try writing one sentence for each pair that compares them without saying either is better. That's harder than it sounds.

## Ask Your Elders

!!! note "Your interview for this chapter"
    Talk with a parent, grandparent, aunt, uncle, family friend or community elder about what faith practice meant in their childhood home, and how they practice now. If your family is not religious, ask about the values they grew up with and how those values were passed down. If you don't have a South Asian elder to ask, interview a club advisor, a local temple, mosque, gurdwara or cultural center volunteer, or a classmate's family (with permission). Ten minutes is enough.

**Before you start:** Faith can be personal. Ask if they're comfortable talking about it, let them skip any question, and ask permission before taking notes.

**Questions to ask**

1. When you were a child, what did a normal day or week of faith (or family values) look like in your home?
2. Is there a prayer, song, story or ritual you remember from childhood? What did it mean to you then?
3. Where did your family go to worship or celebrate, and what was it like inside?
4. Did you have friends or neighbors of other faiths? What did you learn from them, or share with them?
5. How has your practice changed over your life, or since moving to a new country?
6. What do you most want younger people in our family to understand or keep?

**Listen for:** practices such as Puja, Langar or the Five Pillars of Islam in South Asia; moments of Pilgrimage; and stories of Religious Pluralism, such as neighbors of different faiths sharing festivals or food.

**Save it:** Write down one quote or story in their words, with their permission. If they agree, you can share it with the [Roots & Rivers Story Archive](../../oral-history.md).

## Common Misconceptions

**Myth:** Hinduism has one holy book, like a Hindu Bible.  
**Reality:** Hinduism has many sacred texts, including the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Ramayana, the Mahabharata (with the Bhagavad Gita) and the Puranas, and different communities value different ones most.

**Myth:** Sikhism is a mix of Hinduism and Islam.  
**Reality:** Sikhs understand their faith as a distinct revelation through Guru Nanak and the Gurus who followed him, with its own scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, and its own practices such as langar. The scripture includes some hymns by Hindu and Muslim saints, but that doesn't make Sikhism a blend.

**Myth:** All Buddhists are the same.  
**Reality:** Buddhism has several schools. Sri Lanka follows Theravada, with the Pali Canon, while Bhutan and Himalayan Nepal follow Vajrayana, with Tibetan teachings and rituals. Their temples, festivals and practices differ.

**Myth:** South Asian Muslims are recent converts or outsiders.  
**Reality:** Muslims have lived in South Asia for well over a thousand years, starting with traders on the coasts in the 600s and 700s. Most South Asian Muslims descend from families who have lived in the region for many centuries.

**Myth:** Religion and country map one-to-one: India is Hindu, Pakistan is Muslim, Sri Lanka is Buddhist.  
**Reality:** Every South Asian country is home to several faiths. India had about 172 million Muslims in 2011, Bangladesh has millions of Hindus, Sri Lanka has large Hindu, Muslim and Christian communities, and Sikhism's holiest sites include places in Pakistan.

## Check Yourself

??? question "1. Name the four faiths in this chapter that began in South Asia."
    Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism.

??? question "2. What is the difference between Theravada and Vajrayana Buddhism, and where is each found in South Asia?"
    Theravada uses the Pali Canon and is the Buddhism of Sri Lanka. Vajrayana developed later, uses Mahayana and Tibetan teachings and rituals, and is the Buddhism of Bhutan and Himalayan Nepal.

??? question "3. What is langar, and what idea does it express?"
    Langar is the free community kitchen of a gurdwara, where everyone sits together and eats the same meal regardless of religion, caste, gender or wealth. It expresses the Sikh teaching that all people are equal.

??? question "4. Why did Sunni and Shia Islam separate?"
    They disagreed about who should lead the Muslim community after the Prophet Muhammad died in 632: Sunnis accept the choice of Abu Bakr, while Shias believe the Prophet named Ali.

??? question "5. What does ahimsa mean, and which faith puts it at the very center of life?"
    Ahimsa means non-violence toward all living beings. Jainism puts it at the center, which is why many Jains are strict vegetarians.

## Exercises

### Remember

1. Match each faith to its place of worship: Hinduism, Islam, Sikhism, Zoroastrianism, Judaism.

    ??? note "Answer"
        Hinduism: mandir (temple). Islam: masjid (mosque). Sikhism: gurdwara. Zoroastrianism: fire temple. Judaism: synagogue.

2. Who compiled the first version of the Guru Granth Sahib, and in what year?

    ??? note "Answer"
        Guru Arjan, the fifth Sikh Guru, in 1604.

### Understand

3. Explain the idea of karma and rebirth in your own words, and name two faiths besides Hinduism that use the idea.

    ??? note "Answer"
        Karma is the belief that a person's actions and intentions have effects, in this life and in future lives, as the soul is reborn in a cycle. Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism also speak of karma and rebirth, each in its own way.

4. Explain why India's 14.2% Muslim share in 2011 still meant one of the largest Muslim populations in the world.

    ??? note "Answer"
        India's total population is so large that 14.2% was about 172 million people, more than the total population of most countries.

## Key Takeaways

- South Asia is home to Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism, which began there, and to Islam, Christianity, Zoroastrianism and Judaism, which arrived long ago and took deep root.
- Comparing faiths fairly means using the same lenses for each, describing beliefs as their followers do, and never ranking them.
- Hinduism has no single founder or book. Its key ideas include dharma, karma and rebirth, and puja, the offering of worship.
- Buddhism in South Asia includes Theravada (Sri Lanka) and Vajrayana (Bhutan, Himalayan Nepal). Jainism puts ahimsa, non-violence, at its center.
- Most South Asian Muslims are Sunni, with an important Shia minority. Muslims are the majority in Pakistan, Bangladesh, Afghanistan and the Maldives, and number about 172 million in India (2011).
- Sikhs honor the Guru Granth Sahib as their living Guru, and the langar feeds everyone as equals.
- Pilgrimage and shared sites show religious pluralism in action, but pluralism also involves real tensions that communities must work to overcome.
