# Chapter 7: Festivals Through the Year

## Summary

South Asia's festivals follow three different kinds of calendar. Some follow the sun and land on almost the same date every year. Some follow the moon alone and slide about 11 days earlier each year. Many follow both, so they wobble by a few weeks but stay in the same season. This chapter explains those three calendars first. Then it goes through the year's major festivals across the eight countries: what each one marks, who celebrates it, and how celebrations differ from place to place. By the end you'll be able to predict, roughly, when a festival will fall next year and explain why.

## 7.1 Ben Builds a Calendar

It's a Thursday in late August, and Ben Lindqvist has volunteered for a job nobody else wanted: building the South Asian Student Association's calendar for the whole school year. Zara Hossain wants the club to mark every big festival its members celebrate, so nobody schedules a meeting on someone's Eid or Diwali. Ben figures it'll take ten minutes. He'll just copy last year's dates.

Twenty minutes later he's frowning at his laptop. "Zara, something's broken. Last year Diwali was on 20 October. This year it says 8 November. And Eid al-Fitr was 31 March in 2025, then 21 March in 2026, and next year it's around 10 March. Do people just pick new dates every year?" Zara laughs, then stops, because she realizes she doesn't know why. Her Nanu always says "Eid depends on the moon," but Diwali follows the moon too, and it moved *later*, not earlier. If both festivals follow the moon, why don't they move the same way?

## 7.2 Three Ways to Count a Year

Start with something Ben already knows. His birthday falls on the same date every year because the calendar on his wall follows the sun. The **Gregorian calendar**, the one used for school, sports schedules and taxes in the United States, is a **solar calendar**: its year is built to match one trip of the Earth around the sun, about 365.24 days. That's why the seasons stay put, and hockey season always starts when it's cold.

Now imagine a second calendar that ignores the sun and counts only the moon. The moon goes from new moon to full moon and back to new moon in about 29.53 days. That cycle is a **lunar month**. Twelve lunar months add up to about

$$ 12 \times 29.53 \approx 354.4 \text{ days}, $$

which is about 11 days shorter than a solar year. A calendar built only on the moon is a **lunar calendar**.

There's also a third option: count months by the moon, but every few years add an extra month so the calendar catches up with the sun. That's a **Lunisolar Calendar**, a calendar whose months follow the moon but whose years are kept in step with the sun and the seasons by adding a leap month from time to time. In many Hindu calendars of South Asia, this extra month is called *adhik maas*, "added month," and it is inserted about once every 32.5 months on average, roughly every two and a half to three years.

| Calendar type | Months follow | Year length | What happens to festival dates on the Gregorian calendar | South Asian examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solar | The sun | About 365 days | Almost the same date every year | Vaisakhi, Pohela Boishakh in Bangladesh, Pongal, Nowruz, Christmas |
| Lunisolar | The moon, with a leap month | 354 days in most years, about 384 in a leap year | Jumps back and forth by up to about a month, but stays in the same season | Diwali, Holi, Navaratri, Dashain, Vesak, Losar |
| Lunar | The moon only | 354 or 355 days | About 11 days earlier every year, moving through all seasons | Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, Ashura |

The **Islamic Lunar Calendar** (also called the Hijri calendar) is a purely lunar calendar of 12 months, with a year of 354 or 355 days and no leap month. It is the calendar Muslims use for religious dates, from the start of Ramadan to the two Eids. Because it never adds a month to catch up, its dates drift backward through the Gregorian year. A little arithmetic shows how fast:

$$ 365.24 - 354.37 \approx 10.9 \text{ days per year}. $$

To slide all the way around the Gregorian year, an Islamic date needs about

$$ \frac{365.24}{10.9} \approx 33.5 \text{ Gregorian years}. $$

So someone who fasts during Ramadan for most of their life will fast through every season, more than once.

That answers Ben's puzzle. Eid al-Fitr follows a lunar calendar, so it moved about 11 days earlier from 2026 to 2027. Diwali follows a lunisolar calendar. In most years it also lands about 11 days earlier than the year before, but whenever a leap month is added, it jumps about 19 days later instead. Between 2025 and 2026, a leap month was added, so Diwali jumped from 20 October to 8 November.

### Why a festival can be "a day off"

Calendars that follow the moon raise one more question: who decides when a month starts? In Islamic practice, a new month traditionally begins when people see the thin crescent moon just after sunset, and committees in many countries confirm the sighting. The crescent can be visible in one place and not another, so Eid can fall on different days in, say, Saudi Arabia and Bangladesh, or even in two cities of one country.

In July 2025, for example, the Government of India published its holiday list for 2026 and set the Eid al-Adha holiday (often called Bakrid in India) for 27 May 2026, "subject to change" depending on the moon. On 22 May 2026, after the moon was sighted, the government moved the holiday in Delhi to 28 May. Lunisolar calendars have their own version of this: many Hindu festivals are tied to a *tithi* (a lunar day), and regional calendars sometimes disagree about which sunrise or evening it falls on, so a festival can be marked on two neighboring days in different places.

## 7.3 Festivals of Light and Color: Diwali and Holi

### Diwali

**Diwali** (दिवाली, *dee-VAH-lee*), from the Sanskrit *Deepavali*, "row of lamps," is the festival of lights, held on the new moon at the end of the lunar month of Ashvin (Kartika in some regional calendars), usually in October or November. Families clean and decorate their homes, light small clay oil lamps called *diyas*, draw colored designs at the doorway (you'll learn about rangoli and kolam in [Chapter 11](../11-art-architecture-craft/index.md)), wear new clothes, share sweets and set off fireworks.

What Diwali celebrates depends on who you ask.

- **Many Hindus** celebrate the victory of light over darkness and of good over evil. In much of North India, Diwali recalls the god Rama returning home after defeating Ravana. Many families worship Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth and good fortune, on the main night. In Bengal and among Hindus in Bangladesh, the same new moon night is Kali Puja, honoring the goddess Kali.
- **Sikhs** celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, "day of liberation," on the same day. In Sikh tradition, it marks the release of the sixth Guru, Guru Hargobind, from imprisonment in Gwalior Fort by the Mughal emperor Jahangir, along with 52 imprisoned kings.
- **Jains** mark Diwali as the anniversary of the *nirvana* (final liberation) of Mahavira, the last of the Jain Tirthankaras.
- **Newar Buddhists** of Nepal's Kathmandu Valley also celebrate the festival, which they call Swanti.

Diwali is a public holiday in India (8 November in 2026, according to the Government of India's holiday list), and Hindu communities in Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka celebrate it too. In Nepal, the five-day festival of the same season is called Tihar (Section 7.4). In 2025, a resolution introduced in the US House of Representatives recognized Diwali as a festival celebrated by "Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, and others" across the United States.

### Holi

**Holi** (होली, *HOH-lee*) is the spring festival of colors, held on the full moon of the month of Phalguna and the day after it, usually in March. On the first evening, communities light a bonfire called Holika Dahan. On the main day, people of all ages chase each other through the streets throwing colored powder and water, and old arguments are supposed to be forgiven.

Holi carries several meanings at once. Many Hindus connect the bonfire to the story of the young devotee Prahlad, who survived when his aunt Holika tried to burn him. Holi also celebrates the love of Radha and Krishna, the end of winter and the coming spring harvest. In Bengal (West Bengal in India, and Bangladesh) it is often called Dol Jatra or Basanta Utsab, "spring festival." Nepal celebrates Holi as Fagu Purnima, and Hindu communities in Pakistan and Bangladesh celebrate it too. In 2026, India's holiday list put Holi on 4 March.

Because Holi follows a lunisolar calendar, it can fall anywhere from late February to late March, but the leap month keeps it from ever leaving spring.

## 7.4 Nine Nights and the Tenth Day

### Navaratri and Durga Puja

**Navaratri and Durga Puja** are two names for the great autumn festival of the goddess. *Navaratri* (नवरात्रि, *nuh-vuh-RAHT-ree*) means "nine nights." It begins the day after the new moon of the month of Ashvin, usually in September or October, and the tenth day is Vijayadashami, often called Dussehra, "the tenth day of victory." In 2026, India's central government listed Dussehra on 20 October.

The same nine nights look very different across the region.

- **In Gujarat**, people dance Garba, a circle dance around a lamp or an image of the goddess, night after night. UNESCO added the Garba of Gujarat to its list of intangible cultural heritage in 2023.
- **In Bengal, Assam and Odisha**, and among Hindus in Bangladesh, the festival is **Durga Puja**, the worship of the goddess Durga (दुर्गा, *DOOR-gah*), who in many Hindu traditions defeats the buffalo demon Mahishasura. Artisans spend months sculpting images of Durga and her children from clay. Communities build temporary decorated structures called *pandals* to house them, and on the last day the images are carried to a river and immersed. UNESCO inscribed "Durga Puja in Kolkata" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2021.
- **In much of North India**, the tenth day recalls Rama's victory over Ravana. Plays called Ramlila retell the story, and towering effigies of Ravana are burned with fireworks.

### Dashain and Tihar

**Dashain and Tihar** are Nepal's two biggest festivals, about three weeks apart in the autumn.

**Dashain** (दशैं, *duh-SHAIN*) is Nepal's version of the Navaratri season. It is the longest and most important festival in the country's calendar, lasting 15 days, usually in September or October. On the first day, families plant barley seeds indoors. By the tenth day the seeds have grown into pale yellow shoots called *jamara*. On that day, elders put a *tika* (a mark of red rice and yogurt) on the foreheads of younger relatives, tuck *jamara* behind their ears and give their blessing. Dashain is celebrated by Nepali families of many backgrounds, and also in Bhutan and in Nepali-speaking parts of India.

**Tihar** (तिहार, *tee-HAAR*) is Nepal's five-day festival of lights, in the same season as Diwali. Each day honors someone different: crows on the first day, dogs on the second (Kukur Tihar, when dogs get flower garlands and treats), cows and the goddess Lakshmi on the third, oxen on the fourth, and siblings on the last day, Bhai Tika, when sisters bless their brothers. Children go house to house singing songs called Deusi and Bhailo, a little like caroling. Among Newar communities of the Kathmandu Valley the festival is called Swanti.

!!! mascot-tip "Same night, different names"
    ![Mora giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    If a friend from Kolkata says "Durga Puja," a friend from Ahmedabad says "Navaratri," and a friend from Kathmandu says "Dashain," they're often talking about the very same days in the calendar. Ask how *their* family celebrates before you assume it's the same festival in every way.

## 7.5 The Islamic Year

Muslims make up most of the population of Afghanistan, Pakistan, Bangladesh and the Maldives, and large communities in India and Sri Lanka. For all of them, the year's most important religious dates follow the Islamic Lunar Calendar.

### Ramadan

**Ramadan** (Arabic رمضان, *rah-mah-DAHN*; in Bangla রমজান, *rom-JAHN*) is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, a month of fasting, prayer and charity that lasts 29 or 30 days, from one sighting of the crescent moon to the next. Fasting during Ramadan is one of the Five Pillars of Islam. Adult Muslims go without food and drink from dawn until sunset. People who are ill, traveling, elderly, pregnant or breastfeeding are excused, and many can make up the days later.

Zara's family wakes up before dawn for *sehri* (the predawn meal; in Arabic it's called *suhur*) and gathers at sunset for *iftar*, the meal that breaks the fast, often starting with dates and water. In Minnesota, where summer days are long and winter days are short, the length of the fast changes a lot depending on which season Ramadan falls in. Over a lifetime, Zara will fast through Minnesota summers and winters alike.

### Eid al-Fitr

**Eid al-Fitr** (Bangla ঈদুল ফিতর, *EED-ul FIT-er*), "the festival of breaking the fast," begins on the first day of the month of Shawwal, when the new crescent is sighted at the end of Ramadan. The night of the sighting is celebrated as *Chand Raat*, "night of the moon," with last-minute shopping and henna. On Eid morning, families put on new clothes and attend special prayers. Before the prayer, Muslims give a required charity called *zakat al-fitr* so that people in need can celebrate too. Children receive *Eidi* (gifts of money), and visits to relatives last all day, often with sweet vermicelli (*shemai* in Bangla, *sheer khurma* or *seviyan* in Urdu and Hindi). Eid al-Fitr is celebrated for one to three days depending on the country. In 2026, India's holiday list put it on 21 March.

### Eid al-Adha

**Eid al-Adha** (Bangla ঈদুল আযহা, *EED-ul AD-ha*), "the festival of sacrifice," falls on the 10th day of Dhu al-Hijjah, the last month of the Islamic year, during the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca. In Islamic tradition, it honors the prophet Ibrahim's (Abraham's) willingness to sacrifice his son at God's command. Families who can afford it sacrifice an animal (a goat, sheep, cow or camel) and share the meat with relatives, neighbors and people in need. In Pakistan and Bangladesh, busy animal markets appear in cities in the weeks before. In India it is often called Bakrid, and in 2026 it fell on 28 May in Delhi, one day after the date first printed on the holiday list.

### Ashura

**Ashura** (Persian عاشورا, *aa-SHOO-rah*, "the tenth") is the 10th day of Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year. It means different things to different Muslims.

- For **Shia Muslims**, Ashura is the most solemn day of the year, a day of mourning for Husayn ibn Ali, grandson of the Prophet Muhammad, who was killed with his companions at the Battle of Karbala, in present-day Iraq, on 10 Muharram in 680 CE. During the first ten days of Muharram, Shia communities hold gatherings called *majlis* where Husayn's story is retold, and on Ashura they hold processions. In Dhaka, the Hussaini Dalan, a Shia hall first built in the 17th century under Mughal rule, is the center of Muharram gatherings. Large Muharram processions also take place in cities across Pakistan and India.
- **Sunni Muslims** also regard Husayn's death as a tragedy and remember it in sermons. Many Sunnis also fast on Ashura, a practice linked in Islamic tradition to the day Moses and the Israelites were saved from Pharaoh.

## 7.6 New Years and Harvests Set by the Sun

A surprising number of South Asia's new years land in mid-April. That's because many regional calendars start the year when the sun enters Mesha (the sign of Aries in the Indian zodiac), around 13 to 15 April. Since these festivals follow the sun, their dates barely move.

### Vaisakhi

**Vaisakhi** (*VAI-saa-khee*), also spelled Baisakhi, falls on 13 or 14 April and is both a harvest festival and a new year in Punjab and in much of North India. For Sikhs, it is one of the most important days of the year. In Sikh tradition, on Vaisakhi in 1699 the tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, founded the Khalsa, the community of initiated Sikhs, at Anandpur. Sikhs mark the day with prayers at the gurdwara and *nagar kirtan*, processions with hymn singing through the streets. Because many of Sikhism's holiest sites are in present-day Pakistan, thousands of Sikh pilgrims from India cross the border each year for Vaisakhi, with the main gathering at Gurdwara Panja Sahib in Hasan Abdal, in Pakistan's Punjab province. Simran Kaur Gill, the club treasurer, tells Ben that her grandparents in Ludhiana start the day at the gurdwara and spend the afternoon at a fair.

The same mid-April date is also Vishu in Kerala, Puthandu (Tamil New Year) in Tamil Nadu, and Bohag Bihu in Assam. In Sri Lanka, Sinhala and Tamil families celebrate the Sinhala and Tamil New Year on 13 or 14 April. Dilan Perera's family in Kandy waits for the exact auspicious time, set by astrologers, to light the hearth and cook *kiribath* (milk rice), and many neighbors do the same at the same minute.

### Pohela Boishakh

**Pohela Boishakh** (পহেলা বৈশাখ, *po-HEH-la boy-SHAKH*), "the first of Boishakh," is the Bengali New Year. In Bangladesh it is a national holiday on 14 April every year. That date is fixed because Bangladesh revised its version of the Bengali calendar: a committee led by the scholar Muhammad Shahidullah proposed the reform in 1966, and Bangladesh officially adopted it in 1987. In West Bengal and other Indian states, which still use the traditional Bengali calendar, Pohela Boishakh falls on 14 or 15 April. India's 2026 holiday list put "Vaisakhadi (Bengal)" on 15 April.

In Dhaka, the day starts at dawn with songs by the cultural group Chhayanaut in Ramna Park. Students and teachers from the Faculty of Fine Art at the University of Dhaka lead a huge street procession with giant masks and floats. That tradition began in 1989, when students under military rule wanted to bring people hope, and UNESCO added it to its intangible heritage list in 2016 as Mangal Shobhajatra. Its official name was changed in 2025 and debated again in 2026, so you may hear it called by different names.

Shopkeepers close the old year's accounts and open new account books, a custom called *haal khata*. In recent decades many families in Dhaka have also made *panta bhat* (cooked rice soaked overnight in water) part of the day's breakfast.

!!! abstract "Whose Story?"
    Who invented the Bengali year? Many Bangladeshi textbooks and writers credit the Mughal emperor Akbar, who in the 1500s wanted a calendar that matched the harvest so taxes could be collected after the crops came in. Other historians point to older Bengali rulers, and to temple inscriptions that suggest a Bengali year was counted before Akbar's time. Some writers stress the festival's roots in Hindu solar new year traditions, while in Bangladesh it is also celebrated as a shared, secular Bengali holiday by Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists and Christians alike. Even the date is a border story: Dhaka celebrates on the 14th every year, while Kolkata sometimes celebrates on the 15th, because the two cities now use different versions of the same calendar.

### Pongal and Makar Sankranti

**Pongal and Makar Sankranti** are winter harvest festivals that mark the sun's move into Makara (Capricorn in the Indian zodiac), usually on 14 or 15 January. India's 2026 holiday list put both on 14 January. Because they follow the sun, they're among the most predictable dates in the South Asian year.

**Pongal** (பொங்கல், *PONG-gul*), "boiling over," is the Tamil harvest festival, held over three or four days at the start of the Tamil month of Thai. On the main day, families cook rice with milk and jaggery (unrefined cane sugar) in a new pot outdoors until it boils over, and everyone shouts "Pongalo pongal!" On Mattu Pongal, cattle are bathed, their horns painted and garlanded, to thank them for their work. Tamil communities in Sri Lanka celebrate Thai Pongal too.

**Makar Sankranti** (मकर संक्रांति) is the name used in much of India for the same solar moment. In Gujarat it's a kite festival called Uttarayan; in Assam it's Magh Bihu; in Nepal it is Maghe Sankranti. In Punjab, the bonfire festival Lohri falls the night before, usually on 13 January, and Hindus, Sikhs and some Muslims also mark it in rural Pakistani Punjab and in cities such as Lahore and Faisalabad.

### Onam

**Onam** (ഓണം, *OH-num*) is the harvest festival of Kerala, celebrated by Malayali families of many faiths. It falls in the Malayalam month of Chingam (August to September), on the day of the star Thiruvonam. In 2026, India's holiday list put Thiru Onam on 26 August. In Kerala tradition, Onam is when the generous king Mahabali, who once ruled Kerala, returns each year to visit his people. Families make a *pookalam*, a round carpet of flower petals at the doorway, and share a *sadya*, a feast of many dishes served on a banana leaf. In some towns, long snake boats race on the backwaters. Onam's date depends on both a solar month and the moon's position among the stars, so it moves around within late August and September.

### Nowruz

**Nowruz** (نوروز, *noh-ROOZ*), "new day," is the Persian New Year, which begins at the March equinox, on 20 or 21 March. In Afghanistan, where it's called Nawruz, it was long the start of the official calendar year. Families prepare *haft mewa*, a dish of seven dried fruits and nuts soaked in water, and the biggest celebrations took place at Mazar-i-Sharif, where a banner-raising ceremony drew crowds from across the country. After the Taliban took power in 2021, their government stopped observing Nowruz as an official holiday in 2022, while saying people could celebrate privately. In India, Parsi and Irani families, who trace their roots to Iran, also celebrate Navroz. UNESCO lists Nowruz as shared heritage of many countries; its 2024 listing includes Afghanistan, India and Pakistan.

## 7.7 Full Moons and Mountain New Years

### Vesak

**Vesak** (Sinhala වෙසක්, *VAY-suck*), also called Buddha Purnima in India and Nepal, is the most important Buddhist festival in South Asia. It falls on the full moon of the month of Vesak (Vaisakha), usually in May, and Buddhists in South Asia remember the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and passing away all on this one day. In 1999 the United Nations General Assembly formally recognized the Day of Vesak.

In Sri Lanka, Vesak is a two-day national celebration. Streets glow with colored paper lanterns called *Vesak kudu*, and huge electrically lit panels called *pandols* (or *thoranas*) tell Jataka tales, stories of the Buddha's earlier lives. Many people set up free food stalls for anyone passing by, and the sale of alcohol and meat is usually stopped for the celebrations. In Sri Lanka every full-moon day, called Poya, is a public holiday, and Vesak Poya is the biggest of them. Dilan's family visits the temple in Kandy dressed in white. Nepal, where Lumbini (traditionally the Buddha's birthplace) lies, celebrates Buddha Jayanti, and Buddhists in Bangladesh, Bhutan and India also mark the day. In 2026, India's holiday list put Buddha Purnima on 1 May.

### Losar

**Losar** (Tibetan ལོ་གསར, *LOH-sar*), "new year," is the new year of the Tibetan lunisolar calendar, usually in February or March, celebrated for up to 15 days with the main celebrations in the first three. In Bhutan it is called Dawa Dangpa Losar. It is also celebrated by Sherpa, Tibetan and other Himalayan communities in Nepal and by people in Ladakh and Sikkim in India. Families clean their homes thoroughly beforehand, make special foods, visit monasteries and hang new prayer flags.

Nepal is a good reminder that one festival name can cover several dates. Nima Sherpa's family in Solukhumbu celebrates Gyalpo Losar, the Tibetan-calendar new year, in February or March. The Tamang community's Sonam Lhosar usually comes about a month earlier (19 January in 2026), and the Gurung community's Tamu Lhosar falls in late December or early January. Tamu Lhosar is a public holiday in Nepal.

## 7.8 Christmas in South Asia

**Christmas in South Asia** is the celebration of the birth of Jesus on 25 December by the region's Christian communities. Christianity in South Asia goes back a long way. Christians in Kerala trace their church to the apostle Thomas, who in their tradition arrived on India's southwest coast in the first century. According to India's 2011 census, about 2.3% of Indians, roughly 28 million people, were Christian. Kerala has the largest Christian population of any Indian state, and Christians are the majority in the northeastern states of Nagaland, Mizoram and Meghalaya. Goa has a long Catholic history from the centuries of Portuguese rule, and many families there go to midnight Mass on Christmas Eve.

Christmas is a public holiday in India. In Bangladesh it is called Borodin (বড়দিন, *BOH-roh-din*), "the big day." In Pakistan, 25 December is a public holiday for two reasons: Christmas, and Quaid-e-Azam Day, the birthday of Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Pakistan's founder. Christians in Sri Lanka, both Sinhala- and Tamil-speaking, celebrate with church services and family gatherings. Because Christmas follows the Gregorian calendar, which is solar, it's the easiest date on Ben's calendar.

## Common Misconceptions

**Myth:** Diwali is only a Hindu festival.  
**Reality:** Hindus, Sikhs, Jains and some Buddhists all celebrate on the same night, each for their own reasons. For Sikhs it is Bandi Chhor Divas, and for Jains it marks Mahavira's nirvana.

**Myth:** Eid is "the Muslim Christmas."  
**Reality:** There are two Eids, with different meanings: Eid al-Fitr ends the fasting month of Ramadan, and Eid al-Adha honors Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice and falls during the Hajj. Neither celebrates anyone's birth, and both move through the year on the lunar calendar.

**Myth:** Holi is just about throwing colors.  
**Reality:** Holi begins with the Holika Dahan bonfire and carries religious stories (Prahlad, and Radha and Krishna), the welcome of spring, and a tradition of forgiving old quarrels.

**Myth:** Festivals that follow the moon all move the same way.  
**Reality:** Islamic festivals follow a purely lunar calendar and move about 11 days earlier every year. Lunisolar festivals like Diwali add a leap month every two to three years, so they jump back later and stay in the same season.

**Myth:** Every festival is celebrated the same way everywhere.  
**Reality:** The same autumn nights are Garba in Gujarat, Durga Puja in Kolkata and Dhaka, Ramlila in North India, and Dashain in Nepal. Even the date of Pohela Boishakh differs between Bangladesh and West Bengal.

## Check Yourself

??? question "1. What is the difference between a lunar calendar and a lunisolar calendar?"
    Both count months by the moon. A lunar calendar, like the Islamic calendar, never adds extra months, so its year is about 354 days. A lunisolar calendar adds a leap month every two to three years to stay in step with the sun and the seasons.

??? question "2. Why does Eid al-Fitr come about 11 days earlier each year on the Gregorian calendar?"
    Twelve lunar months add up to about 354 days, roughly 11 days less than a solar year of about 365 days. The Islamic calendar never adds a month to catch up, so each new Eid comes about 11 days earlier.

??? question "3. Name the three groups who celebrate on Diwali night and one thing each group remembers."
    Many Hindus celebrate light over darkness (Rama's return, Lakshmi puja); Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, Guru Hargobind's release from Gwalior Fort; Jains remember Mahavira's nirvana. Some Buddhists celebrate too.

??? question "4. Why is Pohela Boishakh always on 14 April in Bangladesh but sometimes on 15 April in West Bengal?"
    Bangladesh adopted a revised Bengali calendar in 1987 (based on a 1966 proposal), which fixes the new year on 14 April. West Bengal still uses the traditional calendar, whose new year falls on 14 or 15 April.

??? question "5. What is Ashura, and how do Shia and many Sunni Muslims observe it?"
    Ashura is the 10th of Muharram. For Shia Muslims it is a day of mourning for Husayn, killed at Karbala in 680 CE, with gatherings and processions. Many Sunni Muslims fast on Ashura.

## Key Takeaways

- South Asian festivals follow three kinds of calendar: solar (fixed dates), lunisolar (moves within a season) and lunar (moves through every season).
- The Islamic Lunar Calendar has 12 months and about 354 days, so Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha and Ashura come about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year and circle the whole year in about 33 years.
- Lunisolar calendars add a leap month about every 32.5 months, which keeps Diwali in autumn, Holi in spring and Vesak near May.
- Many festivals are shared across faiths and borders: Diwali by Hindus, Sikhs, Jains and some Buddhists; Pohela Boishakh by Bengalis of every faith; Vesak across five South Asian countries.
- The same season can carry different names and customs: Navaratri, Durga Puja, Dussehra and Dashain; Diwali and Tihar; Vaisakhi, Pohela Boishakh and the Sinhala and Tamil New Year.
- Exact dates depend on local calendars and, for Islamic months, on sighting the moon, so official holiday lists sometimes change, as India's did for Eid al-Adha in 2026.
