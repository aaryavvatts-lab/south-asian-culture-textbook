# Oral History Project and Story Archive

Every chapter of this book ends with **Ask Your Elders**, a short interview guide. Do the ones in Chapters 1 to 21 and you'll have 21 conversations with the people who carry your family's or community's memory. This page shows how to turn those conversations into something lasting: a family archive, a club project, or a story shared with other readers.

!!! mascot-tip "Start small"
    ![Mora giving a tip](img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    You don't need a microphone or a plan for all 21 interviews. Pick the chapter you care about most, ask one person for ten minutes, and write down one story in their words. That's an oral history.

## What oral history is

**Oral history** is the practice of recording people's memories of their own lives, in their own words, so that others can learn from them later. Libraries and museums collect oral histories because official records leave a lot out: what a festival smelled like, what it felt like to cross a new border, why a grandmother still sets an extra plate. The [South Asian American Digital Archive (SAADA)](https://www.saada.org/) collects stories like these from the diaspora.

## The method in four steps

### 1. Ask permission (informed consent)

Before you start, tell the person what you're doing, how long it will take, whether you'll record or take notes, and who might see it. Ask if it's OK. They can skip any question, stop at any time, or ask you not to share something later. This is called **informed consent**, and it's the first rule of oral history ([Oral History Association](https://oralhistory.org/principles-and-best-practices-revised-2018/)).

A simple script:

> "I'm doing a project for my school's South Asian club about [topic]. Can I ask you a few questions for about ten minutes? I'd like to take notes. You can skip anything you don't want to answer. Later, I might share one short story from our talk, with only your first name or initial, if you say that's OK."

### 2. Listen more than you talk

Use the questions in the chapter's Ask Your Elders section, but follow the conversation. The best answers often come from a follow-up: "What happened next?" "What did that look like?" "How did that feel?" Don't correct them; memory isn't a test. If what they say differs from the book, that's interesting, not wrong. Write down the difference.

### 3. Save it carefully

Write the date, who you talked to (relationship and first name), the chapter, and the best story in their words. If you recorded audio, keep it somewhere private and ask before sharing it with anyone. Never post an elder's full name, address, phone number or photo without clear permission.

### 4. Give something back

Show the person what you wrote. Many elders love seeing their story taken seriously. Some families make a printed booklet of a year of interviews as a gift.

## Turn it into a project

- **Family archive:** collect all 21 interviews into one document with photos the family chooses to share.
- **Club night:** at a South Asian Student Association meeting, each member shares one short story (with permission). See the [Club Kit](club-kit.md).
- **Class project:** many of the [Teacher Guide](teachers.md) activities use these interviews.
- **Interview Builder:** the MicroSim in [Chapter 22](chapters/22-bring-it-home/index.md) builds a printable interview plan for any chapter.

## The Roots & Rivers Story Archive

The Story Archive collects short stories from readers' interviews, shared with permission, so that other readers can hear how traditions are lived in real families.

**The archive has no stories yet.** Version 1.0 of the book has just been published. Every story here will come from a real reader, shared with consent, and approved before it appears. We will never write or invent stories for the archive.

### How to share a story

1. Pick one story from an interview, 150 words or fewer.
2. Get permission from the person who told it, and from your parent or guardian if you're under 18.
3. Use first names or initials only.
4. Submit it with the [Share a Story form](https://github.com/aaryavvatts-lab/south-asian-culture-textbook/issues/new?template=6-share-a-story.yml) on GitHub. That form is public, so if you'd rather not post publicly, share your story through your school's South Asian club instead.

Stories are reviewed, may be shortened (never changed in meaning), and are added here with the chapter they connect to.
