    Frequently Asked Questions¶

    These are the questions readers ask most, from "Is this book for me?" to "Why does Eid move every year?" Each answer is short and links to the section of the book that explains it in full.

    Getting Started¶

    What is South Asia?¶

    South Asia is a region in the south of the Asian continent made up of eight countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. These are the members of SAARC, the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation, which was founded in Dhaka in 1985; Afghanistan joined in 2007. A region is a group of places people talk about together, but it has no government of its own. See Region, Subcontinent, Country and Eight Countries and SAARC.

    Is "South Asia" the same as "the Indian subcontinent"?¶

    Not quite. "Indian subcontinent" is an older, physical term for the wedge of land south of the Himalaya, today divided mainly among India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, with Nepal and Bhutan along its edge and Sri Lanka often included. "South Asia" names a group of eight countries, including Afghanistan. Many people prefer "South Asia" because it doesn't put one country's name on everyone. See Region, Subcontinent, Country.

    How many people live in South Asia?¶

    About 1.99 billion people lived in the eight countries in 2025 (2025, World Bank), roughly one in four people on Earth. India alone had about 1.46 billion, more than the other seven countries put together. The World Bank's own "South Asia" total is smaller because, since 2025, it groups Afghanistan and Pakistan with the Middle East and North Africa. See How Many People Live in South Asia?

    Is this book only for South Asian students?¶

    No. The book is written both for teens who want to understand their own heritage and for friends who are curious and start with zero background. One of the two main characters, Ben, has Swedish and Norwegian roots and is the first non-South Asian member of his school's club. As he puts it in Chapter 22, the club is for anyone who wants to learn. See Being a Good Ally.

    Do I have to read the chapters in order?¶

    No. Chapter 1 is the best first stop because it introduces the eight countries, the land and the monsoon, but every chapter lists its prerequisites at the top, so you can jump straight to festivals or food. The How to Use This Book page explains the layout. Start with What Is South Asia? if you're not sure.

    Who are Zara, Ben and Mora? Are they real?¶

    They are all made up. Zara Hossain and Ben Lindqvist are fictional sophomores at a Minnesota high school; Zara's family is from Chattogram, Bangladesh, and Ben's is Swedish and Norwegian. Mora is a young peacock who pops up with tips and warnings. Each chapter opens with a question one of them can't answer yet, like Zara's in Wait, Isn't Bangladesh Part of India?

    What are the "Whose Story?" boxes?¶

    They show how different communities, countries or historians tell the same event differently. For example, the box on Partition explains why 14 August is mainly a day of celebration in Pakistan, why 15 August is Independence Day in India, and why many Punjabi and Bengali families remember 1947 first as the year they lost their homes. See the box in What Partition Was.

    How do I pronounce all the new words?¶

    The first time a key word appears, the book gives it in its original script, a simple respelling with the stressed syllable in capitals, and its meaning, like Diwali, dee-VAH-lee, "row of lamps." The respellings are approximate, so the best way to learn is to ask someone who speaks the language. See how it works for Diwali and the Pronunciation Guide.

    Foundations¶

    Why do the Himalaya and the monsoon matter so much?¶

    The Himalaya stretches about 2,400 km across Pakistan, India, Nepal, Bhutan and China, and great rivers such as the Ganges rise in its mountains. The monsoon is a seasonal shift in wind direction that brings a rainy season most farming depends on. Rich river soil, warm weather and dependable rain make it possible to grow a lot of food, which is a big reason so many people live here. See The Mountain Walls and The Monsoon.

    What was the Indus Valley Civilization?¶

    It was a civilization of planned cities that flourished from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, in an area bigger than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia. Its best-known cities, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, are in present-day Pakistan. The cities declined gradually, likely because of floods, shifting rivers and a drier climate, and their script is still undeciphered. See The Indus Script and The Indus Valley Civilization.

    Did "Aryans" invade India?¶

    The explanation most scholars accept is the Indo-Aryan Migration: people speaking early Indo-Aryan languages moved into the northwest in gradual waves after about 2000 BCE and mixed with people already there. The evidence comes from language, ancient DNA and the texts themselves. The older "Aryan invasion" theory, in which conquerors destroyed the Indus cities, is rejected by scholars, and some writers and scholars in India dispute migration altogether. See The Indo-Aryan Migration.

    Who was Ashoka?¶

    Ashoka was an emperor of the Maurya Empire who fought a bloody war to conquer Kalinga around 260 BCE and then turned to Buddhism and dhamma. He had his ideas carved in edicts across his empire, including one in Greek and Aramaic near Kandahar in present-day Afghanistan. He still kept his army and his empire after his change of heart. See The Maurya Empire and Ashoka.

    How did Islam come to South Asia?¶

    By several routes. Muslim traders were living on the coasts of Kerala and Sri Lanka by the 7th and 8th centuries, the Maldives became Muslim after its king converted in the 12th century, and Sufi teachers drew many followers in Punjab, Sindh and Bengal. Conquest was one route among several. See Early Islam in South Asia and Sufism.

    What was the British Raj?¶

    The East India Company was a private trading company that built armies and ruled territory. After the Revolt of 1857, the Government of India Act of 1858 ended Company rule and handed its lands to the British Crown. The British Raj is the name for that direct Crown rule, from 1858 to 1947. See The East India Company and The British Raj.

    What was Partition?¶

    Partition was the division of British India into two independent countries, India and Pakistan, in August 1947, largely along religious lines. Punjab and Bengal were cut in two, about 14 million to 18 million people were displaced, and estimates of the dead range from about 200,000 to 2 million. People of every community were killed and forced from their homes, and there were also many acts of courage by neighbors. See What Partition Was and Partition Migration.

    Why is Kashmir disputed?¶

    When Britain left in 1947, the ruler of Jammu and Kashmir, a Hindu maharaja over a mostly Muslim population, joined India after fighters from Pakistan's northwest entered the state, and the first India-Pakistan war followed. India's position is that the accession was legal and final; Pakistan's position is that it was not valid and that Kashmiris should decide by a vote. Many Kashmiris hold their own views, and China also controls part of the wider region. As of October 2026 the dispute remains unresolved, and this book does not take a side. See The Kashmir Dispute.

    How did Bangladesh become a country?¶

    In 1947 the area became East Pakistan. On 21 February 1952, students in Dhaka were killed protesting for Bangla to be a state language, and in 1971, after a military crackdown, East Pakistan fought the Liberation War. Independence was declared on 26 March 1971 and the war ended on 16 December 1971. See The Language Movement of 1952 and The Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971.

    Faiths and Languages¶

    Which faiths are practiced in South Asia?¶

    Hinduism, Islam, Buddhism, Sikhism, Jainism and Christianity all have large communities, alongside smaller ones such as Zoroastrians (Parsis) and Jewish communities. No country lines up with only one faith: India's 14.2% Muslim share in 2011 was about 172 million people, and Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Nepal all have sizable religious minorities. See A Faith Map of South Asia.

    Is Hinduism a religion or a way of life?¶

    The book describes Hinduism as a family of related traditions, beliefs and practices with no single founder, no single holy book and no single central authority. The word "Hindu" was first used by outsiders, from the name of the Indus River, and many Hindus prefer to call their tradition Sanatana Dharma, "the eternal way." In Hindi, dharm can simply mean "religion." How to label it is something followers themselves describe in different ways. See Hinduism: Origins and Variety.

    What do dharma and karma mean?¶

    Dharma is the idea of right order, duty and right conduct, which many Hindus see as depending on their stage of life and responsibilities. Karma is the belief that a person's actions and intentions have effects in this life and future lives. Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs also speak of karma and rebirth, each in their own way, and in a 2021 Pew survey of India, 77% of Hindus and 77% of Muslims said they believe in karma. See Three Key Ideas.

    What is langar?¶

    Langar is the free community kitchen of a Sikh gurdwara, where volunteers serve a vegetarian meal to anyone who comes, regardless of religion, caste, gender or wealth, and everyone sits together as equals. It is one of the practices that give Sikhism its distinct identity, along with its scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib. See Langar and Religious Pluralism.

    How can I ask about someone's faith without being rude?¶

    Describe, don't judge, and don't rank: say "many Hindus believe" or "in Sikh tradition" rather than stating beliefs as facts or myths. Remember that practice varies a lot inside every faith. A person's name, clothes or country don't tell you their faith, so ask politely and accept "I'd rather not say" as a full answer. See Five Lenses, No Ranking.

    Do people of different faiths in South Asia get along?¶

    South Asia has lived with religious pluralism for thousands of years, with shared ideas, shared pilgrimage sites and even shared scripture. But it has also seen real discrimination and communal violence, and a Pew survey of India found that many people value respect for all faiths while keeping close friendships and marriages mostly inside their own group. Pluralism is something communities build and protect. See Religious Pluralism.

    Are Hindi and Urdu the same language?¶

    Many linguists call them two standard forms of one spoken language, Hindi-Urdu (Hindustani), because everyday speakers understand each other well. They differ in script (Devanagari versus Perso-Arabic) and in formal vocabulary (more Sanskrit words versus more Persian and Arabic words). Many people in India and Pakistan see them as two separate languages, tied to history and identity. See One Language or Two? Hindi-Urdu.

    What's the difference between a language and a script?¶

    A language is spoken; a script is a way to write it. Punjabi is written in Gurmukhi in India and Shahmukhi in Pakistan, and one script, Devanagari, is used for Hindi, Marathi, Nepali and Sanskrit. See A Language Is Not a Script.

    I understand my family's language but answer in English. Have I lost it?¶

    No. Understanding a language you grew up hearing is real knowledge, and it makes you a heritage speaker. Heritage speakers usually understand far more than they speak and often learn to speak, read and write faster than beginners when they get the chance. Mixing languages, or code-switching, is normal across South Asia too. See Living Between Languages.

    Festivals and Food¶

    Why do Diwali and Eid change dates every year?¶

    They follow different calendars. Islamic festivals like Eid follow a purely lunar calendar of 354 or 355 days, so they move about 11 days earlier every year and pass through every season. Diwali follows a lunisolar calendar that adds a leap month every two to three years, so it jumps back later and stays in autumn. See Three Ways to Count a Year.

    Who celebrates Diwali?¶

    Diwali, the festival of lights, falls on a new moon, usually in October or November. Hindus, Sikhs, Jains and some Buddhists all celebrate on the same night, each for their own reasons: for Sikhs it is Bandi Chhor Divas, and for Jains it marks Mahavira's nirvana. See Diwali.

    What's the difference between Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha?¶

    Eid al-Fitr ends the fasting month of Ramadan. Eid al-Adha honors Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice and falls during the Hajj. Both move through the year on the Islamic lunar calendar. See The Islamic Year.

    What happens during Ramadan?¶

    Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, a month of fasting, prayer and charity. Adult Muslims go without food and drink from dawn until sunset, with exceptions for people who are ill, traveling, elderly or pregnant, among others. Many families eat a predawn meal (sehri) and break the fast at sunset with iftar, often starting with dates and water. See Ramadan.

    Why does the same autumn festival look so different from place to place?¶

    The same autumn nights are Garba in Gujarat, Durga Puja in Kolkata and Dhaka, Ramlila in North India and Dashain in Nepal. Each region and community tells its own stories and keeps its own customs. See Nine Nights and the Tenth Day.

    What is a thali?¶

    A thali is a large round plate, usually metal, holding a complete meal: a staple in the middle and several small dishes around it in bowls called katori. It tries to balance flavors and textures, from sour and salty to mildly bitter and sweet, and the same idea shows up across the region under other names. See The Thali.

    Are most South Asians vegetarian?¶

    No. In India, 39% of adults called themselves vegetarian in a 2019-2020 Pew survey, though 81% limit meat in some way. In Pakistan, Bangladesh, Afghanistan and the Maldives most people eat meat or fish, and many people in Sri Lanka, Nepal and Bhutan do too. See Vegetarianism in South Asia.

    What does halal mean?¶

    Halal is an Arabic word meaning "permitted," and in Islamic law it describes food Muslims may eat. Pork, alcohol and blood are forbidden, and halal meat must come from a permitted animal slaughtered in a set way. Most vegetarian food is halal as long as it has no alcohol or other forbidden ingredients. See Halal.

    Were chili peppers always part of South Asian cooking?¶

    No. Chilies come from the Americas, and Portuguese traders brought them to India in the 1500s, through Goa, as part of the Columbian Exchange. Before that, heat came from black pepper and other local spices. See Chili Peppers and the Columbian Exchange.

    Arts, Stories and Pop Culture¶

    What are raga and tala?¶

    A raga is a framework for creating melody: it sets which notes may be used, how to go up and down, which notes to stress and its signature phrases. A tala is a repeating cycle of beats, more like a clock face than a row of bars, and its first beat is called the sam. Musicians improvise inside these frameworks while landing exactly on sam. See Raga and Sargam and Tala.

    What's the difference between Hindustani and Carnatic music?¶

    They share raga, tala and sargam, but by about the 1500s they were treated as separate systems. Hindustani music belongs to the north (northern and central India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nepal) and often gives a lot of time to improvisation. Carnatic music belongs to southern India and Tamil communities in Sri Lanka and centers on composed songs, many of them devotional. See Two Classical Systems.

    What makes a dance "classical," and how many classical dances are there?¶

    In this book, a classical dance has a long written theory behind it, usually traced to the Natya Shastra, a fixed technique that takes years to learn, and official recognition. India's Sangeet Natak Akademi recognizes eight, including Bharatanatyam, Kathak and Odissi; the Ministry of Culture also lists Chhau, and others draw the line differently. "Classical" describes a trained, codified form, not something better than folk dance. See What Makes a Dance "Classical"?

    What's the difference between rangoli and kolam?¶

    They are related doorstep arts. Kolam is traditionally drawn in white rice flour, often as lines looping around a grid of dots, and is made daily in many Tamil homes; rangoli usually uses colored powders or petals and is made most often for festivals like Diwali. Alpona in Bengal is painted with wet rice paste. See Art at the Threshold.

    Who wears the saree and the shalwar kameez?¶

    The saree is worn in India, Bangladesh, Nepal and Sri Lanka and in parts of Pakistan; it is widely seen as the national dress for women in Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka has its own Kandyan style. The shalwar kameez is Pakistan's national dress but is also widely worn in India (especially Punjab), Bangladesh and Afghanistan, by people of many faiths. See Draped Garments and Stitched Garments.

    Why are there so many versions of the Ramayana?¶

    Valmiki's Sanskrit Ramayana is the oldest well-known telling, but the story has been retold for centuries in Tamil, Bangla, Awadhi, Nepali, Thai, Javanese, Malay and more, plus Buddhist and Jain tellings. Scholars such as A. K. Ramanujan call them "tellings," because each is a real performance of the story for its own audience. See The Two Great Epics and One Story, Many Tellings.

    Is Bollywood the only South Asian film industry?¶

    No. Bollywood is the Hindi-language industry in Mumbai. India also has large Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, Malayalam, Bengali, Marathi and other industries, and in 2023 it certified films in more than 25 languages. Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Bhutan have their own cinemas too. See Not Just Bollywood.

    Why is India versus Pakistan such a big deal in cricket?¶

    It is often called the most intense rivalry in the sport. The teams first played Test matches in 1952, politics has shaped the rivalry ever since, and no cricket was played between them from 1961 to 1978. When they meet, huge audiences watch, including Indian and Pakistani families in the diaspora who are friends and neighbors. See The India-Pakistan Rivalry.

    Did India invent zero?¶

    Zero grew over centuries, with placeholder marks in Babylon, Greece, the Maya world and South Asia. Brahmagupta, in 628 CE, was the first known writer to give rules for zero as a number, building on earlier Indian place value. The digits then traveled through scholars writing in Arabic, which is why historians say "Hindu-Arabic numerals." See Place Value and the Zero and How the Numbers Traveled.

    Can Ayurveda or yoga cure diseases?¶

    The book describes them as traditions, not cures. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says few well-designed studies have tested Ayurveda, and some products have contained dangerous levels of lead, mercury or arsenic. It says research suggests yoga may help with stress, sleep, balance and some kinds of pain, but it does not claim yoga cures any disease. This is not medical advice. See Ayurveda and Yoga.

    The Eight Countries¶

    How is India organized?¶

    India is a federal republic with 28 states and 8 union territories, governed under the Constitution of India, which came into effect on 26 January 1950. Its capital is New Delhi, while Mumbai is its biggest business and film city. Many state borders were drawn along language lines. See The Constitution of India and States and Union Territories.

    What changed in Jammu and Kashmir in 2019?¶

    Until 2019, Jammu and Kashmir was a state with special autonomy under Article 370 of India's constitution. In August 2019, India's government revoked that status and split the state into two union territories, Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, and in December 2023 India's Supreme Court upheld the change. These territories are part of a larger region whose status is disputed. See Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh.

    What languages do people speak in Pakistan?¶

    Urdu is the national language, but in the 2023 census only about 9% of Pakistanis spoke it as their first language. Punjabi (about 37%), Pashto (about 18%), Sindhi (about 14%) and Saraiki (about 12%) are all more common mother tongues, and most Pakistanis learn Urdu as a second language. See Urdu and Pakistan's Many Mother Tongues.

    Is Afghanistan part of South Asia or the Middle East?¶

    It depends on the purpose. Afghanistan is a landlocked country where South Asia meets Central Asia and Iran; it is a SAARC member, so this book counts it as South Asian, while some scholars group it with Central Asia. Since 2025 the World Bank has put Afghanistan and Pakistan in a "Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan and Pakistan" region for statistics. See Eight Countries and SAARC and Afghanistan.

    Who governs Afghanistan today?¶

    The Taliban, an Islamist movement, took control in August 2021 as US and NATO forces withdrew, and call their government the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan. Afghans are about 44 million people (2025, World Bank) of many ethnic groups, languages and opinions, and many inside and outside the country disagree with the government's policies. See Afghanistan Today.

    Why is 21 February important in Bangladesh?¶

    On 21 February 1952, students in Dhaka were killed while protesting for Bangla to be recognized as a state language. Bangladeshis remember them on Ekushey February, and in 1999 UNESCO declared 21 February International Mother Language Day, so the whole world now marks a date that began in Dhaka. See Ekushey February.

    What caused the Sri Lankan civil war?¶

    The war, from 1983 to 2009, was mainly about ethnicity, language, political power and land, fought between the government and the LTTE, which sought a separate Tamil state. Many Tamils had felt shut out by laws like the Sinhala Only Act, and the Black July riots of 1983 are often treated as its start. The UN estimated 80,000 to 100,000 people were killed; religion overlapped with ethnic identity but was not the main cause. See The Sri Lankan Civil War.

    Was Nepal ever a British colony?¶

    No. Nepal was never formally colonized, though it lost land to the East India Company in the 1816 Treaty of Sugauli and kept close ties with Britain. Bhutan was also never colonized, though it let Britain guide its foreign relations after 1910. See The Neighbors Britain Never Fully Ruled and Nepal.

    What is Gross National Happiness?¶

    Gross National Happiness (GNH) is Bhutan's idea that a country should measure progress by the well-being of its people and environment, not only by its economy. Bhutan built it into its 2008 Constitution. It is a policy goal, not a claim that everyone is happy: Bhutan's own 2022 GNH Index classed 6.4% of people as unhappy. See Gross National Happiness.

    Diaspora and Identity¶

    What is a diaspora?¶

    A diaspora is a population that lives outside its ancestral homeland but keeps some connection to it, through memory, language, faith, food or family ties. South Asians moved in waves: indentured workers to the Caribbean and Fiji, workers and traders to East Africa, postwar migrants to Britain, workers to the Gulf, and immigrants to the US after 1965. Many of those waves were tied to the British Empire. See What Is a Diaspora?

    When did South Asians first come to the United States?¶

    Punjabi Sikh workers came to the US West Coast in the early 1900s, founded a gurdwara in Stockton in 1912 and farmed in California for decades. Laws in 1917 and the 1923 Thind decision then shut most South Asians out until the 1940s and 1960s. See Punjabi Pioneers in California.

    What was the Bhagat Singh Thind case?¶

    In 1923 the US Supreme Court ruled that South Asians were not "white persons" and so could not become naturalized citizens. Thind was a Sikh man from Punjab who had served in the US Army in World War I. After the ruling, the government moved to strip citizenship from many South Asians who had already naturalized. See The Bhagat Singh Thind Case.

    What changed in 1965?¶

    The Immigration Act of 1965 ended the "national origins" quota system that had heavily favored immigrants from northern and western Europe. It gave preference to people with family in the US and people with needed skills. Doctors, engineers, scientists and students came first, followed by their families. See The Immigration Act of 1965.

    What is the model minority myth?¶

    It is the stereotype that Asian Americans, including South Asian Americans, are all successful, wealthy and free of problems. It hides real differences: in 2023 the median household income of Bangladeshi Americans was about half that of Indian Americans, and 14% of Bangladeshi Americans lived in poverty (Pew). See The Model Minority Myth.

    How many South Asians live in Minnesota?¶

    About 64,000 people, a little over 1% of the state, according to US Census Bureau estimates for 2020 to 2024. The community includes families from every South Asian country, with temples, mosques, gurdwaras and student clubs across the Twin Cities and beyond. These are estimates with margins of error, especially for small groups. See South Asians in Minnesota.

    What does "desi" mean, and does everyone use it?¶

    "Desi" comes from desh, "homeland" or "country," and many people of South Asian heritage use it to mean roughly "one of us, from back home." But not everyone does: some Afghans, Nepalis, Sri Lankans, Bhutanese and Maldivians don't use it for themselves, and many Indo-Caribbean people have their own words. Ask people what they call themselves. See Desi Identity and Labels and the Hyphen.

    Can I wear a saree or get mehndi if I'm not South Asian?¶

    Often, yes, when it's appreciation: you're invited, you understand what it means, and you give credit. Wearing a saree to a friend's wedding when their family helps you dress is very different from wearing a bindi as a party costume or selling mehndi designs as "tribal tattoos." People inside South Asian communities don't all agree on where the line is, so when in doubt, ask. See Appreciation or Appropriation?

    What is colorism?¶

    Colorism is prejudice based on skin tone, usually favoring lighter skin, including among people of the same background. In many South Asian families and media, lighter skin has long been praised. After years of criticism, the cream Fair & Lovely was renamed Glow & Lovely in 2020, though it is still sold. See Colorism.

    Common Myths¶

    Is South Asia just another name for India?¶

    No. South Asia is eight countries, and Bangladesh, Pakistan and the others are separate, independent countries with their own governments, languages and histories. India is the largest by far. See Eight Countries and SAARC.

    Did Partition in 1947 create Bangladesh?¶

    No. Partition created India and Pakistan, and the area that is now Bangladesh became East Pakistan. Bangladesh was created in a separate event, the Liberation War of 1971. See From the Language Movement to Bangladesh.

    Did India win independence through nonviolence alone?¶

    Gandhi's nonviolent campaigns were central, but independence also came from elections and political bargaining, armed revolutionaries, the Indian National Army, strikes and protests, and Britain's weakness after the Second World War. Historians still debate how much each mattered. See Organizing for Freedom.

    Is Sikhism a mix of Hinduism and Islam?¶

    Sikhs understand their faith as a distinct revelation through Guru Nanak and the Gurus who followed him, with its own scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, and its own practices such as langar. The scripture includes some hymns by Hindu and Muslim saints, but that doesn't make Sikhism a blend. See Sikhism.

    Is Hindi India's national language?¶

    India's Constitution does not name a national language. Hindi in Devanagari is the official language of the central government, English is also used officially, and 22 languages are listed in the Eighth Schedule. States choose their own official languages. See Official Languages and the Eighth Schedule.

    Is Sanskrit the mother of all South Asian languages?¶

    No. Sanskrit is an ancestor of many Indo-Aryan languages, like Hindi and Bangla, but Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam belong to the separate Dravidian family, and Tibeto-Burman, Austroasiatic and Iranian languages have their own ancestors. Languages borrow words, but borrowing is not descent. See The Five Big Language Families.

    Is "curry" one dish everyone in South Asia eats?¶

    No. "Curry" is an English umbrella word, probably borrowed from the Tamil kari, that British traders applied to hundreds of different dishes. In South Asia people use the dishes' own names, such as dal, korma, sambar, karahi or jhol. See Regional Cuisines.

    Does a turban mean someone belongs to one religion?¶

    No. Turbans are worn by Sikhs, many Pashtun men, elders in Rajasthan, some Hindu monks and some Muslim scholars, among others. For Sikhs the dastar has a specific religious meaning, but the turban itself belongs to many traditions. See The Turban and the Dastar.

    Are all South Asian marriages forced?¶

    No. In an arranged marriage, families help find a partner, but both people freely agree and either can say no. Forced marriage, without real consent, is a human rights abuse that South Asian faiths and laws reject, and many South Asians choose their own partners. See Arranged Marriage.

    Does caste exist only in India, and only in the past?¶

    No. Caste is a historical and continuing social system that still shapes marriage and social life in India and Nepal and appears among people of many faiths. India's Constitution abolished "untouchability" in Article 17, and laws in India and Nepal make caste discrimination a crime, though rights groups say enforcement is uneven. In 2023, Seattle and Fresno added caste to their anti-discrimination laws, and South Asian Americans were divided over those laws. See Caste: Varna, Jati, and the Law.

    Is the Sri Lankan civil war a religious war between Buddhists and Hindus?¶

    No. It was mainly about ethnicity, language, political power and land, between the government and the LTTE. Religion overlapped with ethnic identity, but it was not the main cause, and there were Christians and others on both sides. See Not a Religious War.

    Talking With Your Family¶

    What is Ask Your Elders?¶

    It is a short interview guide near the end of every chapter. You talk with a parent, grandparent, aunt, uncle, family friend or community elder about the chapter's topic, using six suggested questions, and ten minutes is enough. The book calls it its most important part. See any chapter's guide, such as Ask Your Elders in Chapter 1.

    How do I get an elder talking?¶

    Start with something they can see or touch. The chapter guides suggest bringing a map and asking them to point to where they grew up, looking at old wedding or festival photos together, doing the interview in the kitchen over chai, or asking them to write their name in their own script. Always ask first whether it's okay to take notes. See the guides in Chapter 1 and Chapter 8.

    What if I don't have South Asian elders to ask?¶

    Every guide offers other options: interview a club advisor, a volunteer at a local temple, mosque, gurdwara or cultural center, or a classmate's family, with permission. Non-South Asian readers can also interview their own grandparents about their traditions and compare. See Ask Your Elders in Chapter 7.

    What if a topic is painful for my family?¶

    Some memories, such as 1947 and 1971, are painful. Ask first whether your elder wants to talk about those years at all, let them choose what to share and never push for details. If they ask you to keep a story private, it stays in the family: in Chapter 22, Zara's grandmother asks her to keep the 1971 story "just for the family," and Zara does. See Ask Your Elders in Chapter 4 and Informed Consent.

    Can I record the interview?¶

    Only with permission. Informed consent means the person understands what you're doing, how their words will be used and who might see them, and freely agrees, with the right to skip questions or stop at any time. If you're under 18 and want to share anything outside the family, get a parent or guardian's OK, and use first names or initials only. See Informed Consent.

    What if my family's version of history is different from the book's?¶

    That's normal, and it's what the Whose Story? boxes are for. One person's story is one person's experience: a Partition story from a family in Lahore and another from a family in Amritsar can both be true and still be very different. That's why oral historians collect many voices. See What Partition Was and Culture Lives in People.

    Why do my relatives have a different word for every uncle and aunt?¶

    Many South Asian languages give separate words to relatives English lumps together, so you can tell from one word which side of the family someone is on, and sometimes whether they are older or younger. English has one word, "uncle," for at least four different people. See Kinship Terms.

    What if my family celebrates differently from what the book describes?¶

    Practice varies inside every faith, region and family, and the book uses "many families" and "in some regions" for exactly that reason. Traditions also change from generation to generation, and adapting one to a new home is a normal part of keeping it alive. Every family does it a little differently, and there is no wrong answer here. See Five Lenses, No Ranking and Who Am I?

    For Teachers and Clubs¶

    Can I use this book in my classroom?¶

    Yes. The book is free under a Creative Commons BY-NC-SA 4.0 license: you can use it in class, print it, share it and adapt it if you give credit, share your version under the same license and don't sell it. It also fits Minnesota's 2023 law requiring every public high school to offer an ethnic studies course, starting this school year, 2026-27. See For Teachers and Clubs, Organizing and Impact.

    How should I teach Partition and 1971?¶

    The book describes suffering on all sides, gives death and displacement figures as ranges, and avoids graphic detail. Tell students a day ahead what the chapter covers, offer a quiet alternative task without asking why, and never ask a student to explain "their side." See Partition Migration and the guidance in For Teachers.

    How should caste come up in class?¶

    Keep the discussion on the system, the laws and how people have resisted it, not on individuals. Never ask a classmate their caste or guess it from a surname, and never describe communities as "high" or "low." Use "Dalit" and "Adivasi" respectfully, as the book does. See Caste: Varna, Jati, and the Law.

    Do students have to share their family history?¶

    No. Ask Your Elders must never require students to disclose family history. Offer everyone the same choices without asking which applies: interview a family member, interview a community member, or do a "research interview" from a published oral history. Grade the process, not anyone's family life, and keep share-outs voluntary. See For Teachers and Ask Your Elders in Chapter 22.

    How can a South Asian club welcome non-South Asian members?¶

    Say out loud, at the first meeting and on every poster, that the club is open to everyone, and give every member real roles. Avoid "explain your culture" moments for anyone: South Asian members aren't spokespeople, and non-South Asian members aren't tourists. An ally's job is to listen, show up and help while members lead. See Being a Good Ally and the Club Kit.

    How do we plan food for a club meeting?¶

    Members may have different food rules. Many Muslims eat only halal food, which rules out pork and alcohol, and some members may be vegetarian or avoid certain meats. Label dishes so everyone can choose. See Food and Faith and the Club Kit.

    How do we know if a club project made a difference?¶

    Measure impact, not just activity. Ten meetings is activity; five new members who say they finally feel they belong is impact. Before each project, write down one question you'll ask and one number you'll count, then check them afterward. See Clubs, Organizing and Impact.

    How was this book checked for accuracy?¶

    Each chapter went through a structural check by script, a fact-check of every claim against its source, a review for nuance and balance, and a final plain-language edit. Historians use evidence from the ground and from memory, and the book shows where they still disagree. If you spot an error, the Accuracy and Corrections page explains how to report it. See also How Do We Know?
